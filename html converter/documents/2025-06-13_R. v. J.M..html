    March, M.G., J. :

     

     

    Introduction:

     

    1.           The accused, J.M., stands charged that at the Township of Gorham, ON, between August 1 and 31, 2022, he did:

    a)      for a sexual purpose touch, P.P., a person under the age of 16 with his hands contrary to s. 151 of the Criminal Code of Canada (“the Code”), and

    b)       commit a sexual assault on P.P. contrary to s. 271 of the Code.

    2.           By way of background and following a contested pre-trial application brought by the Crown under s. 486.2 of the Code on March 20, 2025, I ruled that P.P. would be permitted to give her evidence at J.M.’s trial from the Toba Centre in Winnipeg, MB.  When she did so on April 25, 2025, she was 13 years old.  The language of the Code is mandatory – “the judge . . . . shall, on application of the prosecutor in respect of a witness who is under the age of 18 . . . . order that the witness testify outside the court room”. 

    3.           J.M. and his counsel were able to see P.P. from the same vantage point on a large screen within the court room as Crown counsel and I, as the presiding judge, could.  Her location elsewhere while she gave her evidence did not, in my opinion, “interfere with the proper administration of justice”.  It made no difference that she was in Winnipeg as opposed to being at the Court House in Thunder Bay testifying from a ‘child friendly’ anteroom.

    4.      At the outset of the trial proper on April 25, 2025, J.M. admitted, through his counsel, the complainant P.P.’s ability to identify him as the accused before the Court and the jurisdiction of this Court to hear this matter.

    5.           As with so many criminal trials, the outcome of this case turned, as I predicted while giving my s. 486.2 ruling, on an assessment of the credibility and reliability of the witnesses called to testify.

    6.           The central issue was whether the Crown had proven beyond a reasonable doubt if the acts complained of by P.P. happened.

    Family Dynamic:

    7.           P.P. is the daughter of C.M., the accused J.M.’s wife.  P.P. referred to C.M. as her “biological mom” in her statement to police on August 4, 2023, when she first spoke to the authorities about what she alleged occurred to her in August 2022 at the hands of J.M.

    8.           P.P. explained that her father and mother are divorced.  At present, she lives in McCreary, MB, with her father and her stepmother. 

    9.           Her biological mother, C.M., lives just outside Thunder Bay with J.M., an old high school flame of hers with whom she rekindled a relationship in the past five years.  C.M. and J.M. married on August 24, 2023.

    10.      C.M. testified that she and her former partner, P.P.’s father, separated in 2018 after living together for several years in McCreary.  In 2020, C.M. then moved to Thunder Bay to care for her ailing father.  P.P. remained in McCreary residing with her father.

    11.      By agreement C.M. had with P.P.’s father, P.P. would spend four to five weeks in Thunder Bay during the summer post-separation.

    12.      In 2022, P.P. recalled spending part of her summer visiting C.M. at J.M.’s place.  P.P. stayed there for almost all of August.  At the end of her visit, on August 27, 2022, P.P. flew back to Winnipeg accompanied by mother, C.M.

    13.      P.P. added that she did not really know J.M., but she loved C.M. Her mother was the person in Thunder Bay for whom P.P. had more affection, and with whom she had the greater connection.  At the same time, P.P. made clear, C.M. was not the individual to whom she would go if she had any kind of problem.

    The Incident:

    14.      On April 25, 2025, P.P. was asked about a statement she gave to police on August 4, 2023.  The videotaped interview was played in court and adopted by her under s. 715.1 of the Code, thus forming part of her evidence-in-chief at J.M.’s trial.

    15.       She recalled an occasion when she slept in C.M.’s and J.M.’s bed with them while staying at their trailer home near Thunder Bay in August 2022. At that time, she was 10 years of age.  P.P. wanted to watch a movie.  The only TV capable of streaming movies was the one located in C.M.’s and J.M.’s bedroom, P.P. explained. For that reason, they watched one there together.

    16.      P.P. may have fallen asleep watching the movie, “The Greatest Showman”, she thought.  She later awoke multiple times during the night to J.M. touching her under her nightgown and on her underwear line. She “made sure he didn’t”, but it “happened multiple times”, she told police.

    17.      His chest, shins and feet were touching her body. She thought he was wearing a T-shirt and underwear, but she was not sure. He may also have been wearing a robe because he wears that “all the time”.

    18.      She described him as having gone under her underwear strap.  He was “trying to reach for my vagina, and I was blocking it.” She was attempting to push her elbow into her hip to prevent access. In turn, he was seeking to push her hand out of the way.

    19.      She knew he was awake half the time because he snores, and at times he would not be, so she knew he was up almost all night.

    20.  She had tried laying on her stomach, so he could not touch her. She moved to the foot of the bed to create distance between them. However, she was tired. It was quite late at night. She fell asleep again.

    21.  At one point too, she recalled, he was touching her vagina and rubbing it in a circular motion with his finger. It made her feel very uncomfortable.  She was scared.  She did not know what to do. Her nightgown was pulled up and he was under her underwear.  She tried and managed to push his hand away. She did not know how long he had been doing it before she awoke.

    22.  She could only see his arm while he was rubbing her vagina.  A tiny bit of light was entering the bedroom from the bathroom.   

    23.      About 10 minutes later, he got up to go to the bathroom.  That was when she crawled her way to sleep at the end of the bed.  She had curled herself up into “a ball”, but while he was awake, he continued reaching with his hand for her thigh every few seconds, attempting to make his way to her vagina. Sometimes, she would try pulling down her nightgown, but he kept it up.

    25.      He was saying something to her while she was at the end of the bed, asking why she had moved there.  She did not answer or say anything.  She was too frightened.

    26.       P.P. did not tell her mother, C.M., who was on the other side of the bed from J.M. sleeping the whole night.  P.P. was between C.M. and J.M. in the middle of the bed. J.M. was to the right of her, and C.M. was to the left.

She clarified that J.M. was telling her not to say anything to anybody about the incident as C.M. was going to work.  P.P stated, “But like he wasn’t really talking to me. He was making sure, like I heard it, but not to tell anybody about it,” and “trying to get into my head”.  P.P. maintained, “He was just repeating himself about that”.  P.P. just kept playing Xbox while this was happening.

It was the one and only time that P.P. had ever been in her mother’s and J.M.’s bed. However, P.P. denied that that was the last night she spent in Thunder Bay while visiting them before flying the next day to Winnipeg with her mother.

    35.      P.P. did not know whether her mother was awake during any point in time when J.M. was touching her.

    36.      P.P. did not disclose what had occurred until she started school in 2022. She then told her friend, E.B., that J.M. had touched her inappropriately. She did not say anything to her father or her stepmother until the beginning of July in 2023. As P.P. put it, she “got the nerve” to tell her parents because she did not want to go visit C.M. and J.M. that summer.

In 2022, C.M. specifically recalled that the last night P.P. was with her, they watched “The Greatest Showman” in her bed along with her new partner, J.M.  Usually, P.P. would sleep in the spare bedroom of the trailer home C.M. shared with J.M.

Standing at the foot of the bed and facing it, J.M. was on the left, P.P. was in the middle, and C.M. was on the right. She wanted to stay longer in Thunder Bay. She told her mother, C.M., “I don’t want to go home. I’m going to miss you.”

    The Issues:

    86.      Crown and defence counsel were agreed that I must, of course, apply a W.(D.) analysis to the evidence adduced at J.M.’s trial.  Clearly, the credibility of P.P., C.M. and J.M. must be carefully assessed.  Their reliability as historians for significant past events is very much worthy of consideration as well.

    87.      Essentially, in applying the law, I must acquit J.M. if I believe his testimony regarding the incidents in question, after I assess the evidence as a whole (see R. v. Hull, (2006) CanLII 26572 (ONCA) at paras. 4 – 5).

    88.      If I do not believe J.M., but his evidence nevertheless raises a reasonable doubt in my mind upon my assessment of the evidence in its totality, I must find him not guilty.

    89.      Even if I do not accept the evidence of J.M., nor does it raise a reasonable doubt, I must still be convinced of his guilt beyond a reasonable doubt based on the evidence which I do accept, if I am to convict him.

    The Law

    Credibility and Reliability:

    90.      Last year, the Supreme Court of Canada reminded triers of fact of basic principles to be observed and followed in every criminal trial in R. v. Kruk, 2024 SCC 7 (CanLII).  The Court held:

    [59]                          The overarching principle of the presumption of innocence, enshrined in s. 11(d) of the Charter, and the correlative principle of the Crown’s burden of proof, must always govern the fact-finding process. The presumption of innocence — a “hallowed principle lying at the very heart of criminal law” (R. v. Oakes, 1986 CanLII 46 (SCC), [1986] 1 S.C.R. 103, at p. 119) — requires the Crown to bear the onus of proving all essential elements of the offence charged, beyond a reasonable doubt, before a conviction may be entered (Osolin) . . .

    [61]                          The presumption of innocence also restricts how credibility is assessed in cases of conflicting testimony between defence and Crown witnesses. The analysis of testimony must never be treated as a contest of credibility, and triers of fact need not accept the defence’s evidence or version of events in order to acquit (R. v. Van, 2009 SCC 22, [2009] 1 S.C.R. 716, at para. 23; R. v. W. (D.), 1991 CanLII 93 (SCC), [1991] 1 S.C.R. 742, at p. 757). The burden never shifts to the accused to establish their own innocence, and the onus always lies with the Crown to prove every essential element (R. v. J.H.S., 2008 SCC 30, [2008] 2 S.C.R. 152, at para. 13).

    [62]                          Reasonable doubt applies to credibility assessments such that if the evidence the Crown adduced does not rise to the level required of a criminal conviction, an accused cannot be found guilty simply because they are disbelieved (see W. (D.)). Some elements of the totality of the evidence may give rise to a reasonable doubt, even where much — or all — of the accused’s evidence is disbelieved. Any aspect of the accepted evidence, or the absence of evidence, may ground a reasonable doubt. Moreover, where the trier of fact does not know whether to believe the accused’s testimony, or does not know who to believe, the accused is entitled to an acquittal (J.H.S., at paras. 9-13; R. v. H. (C.W.) (1991), 1991 CanLII 3956 (BC CA), 68 C.C.C. (3d) 146 (B.C.C.A.); R. v. S. (W.D.), 1994 CanLII 76 (SCC), [1994] 3 S.C.R. 521, at p. 533; R. v. Avetysan, 2000 SCC 56, [2000] 2 S.C.R. 745, at para. 19). 

    91.      Later, the Court instructed trial judges on their roles in assessing the credibility of witnesses in the following passages:

    [72]                        It is widely recognized that testimonial assessment requires triers of fact to rely on common-sense assumptions about the evidence. In R. v. Delmas, 2020 ABCA 152, 452 D.L.R. (4th) 375, at para. 31, aff’d 2020 SCC 39, [2020] 3 S.C.R. 780, the Alberta Court of Appeal observed that triers of fact may rely on reason and common sense, life experience, and logic in assessing credibility. In R. v. R.R., 2018 ABCA 287, 366 C.C.C. (3d) 293, the same court held that triers of fact “must invariably fall back on their common sense, and their acquired knowledge about human behaviour in assessing the credibility and reliability of witnesses” (para. 6). Finally, in R. v. S. (R.D.), 1997 CanLII 324 (SCC), [1997] 3 S.C.R. 484, this Court considered that the life experience of trial judges — though of course not a substitute for evidence, and subject to appropriately circumscribed limits — “is an important ingredient in the ability to understand human behaviour, to weigh the evidence, and to determine credibility”, and assists with a “myriad of decisions arising during the course of most trials” (para. 13). Reasoning about how people generally tend to behave, and how things tend to happen, is not only permissible, it is often a necessary component of a complete testimonial assessment.

    [73]                        In turn, common-sense assumptions necessarily underlie all credibility and reliability assessments. Credibility can only be assessed against a general understanding of “the way things can and do happen”; it is by applying common sense and generalizing based on their accumulated knowledge about human behaviour that trial judges assess whether a narrative is plausible or “inherently improbable” (R. v. Kiss, 2018 ONCA 184, at para. 31 (CanLII); R. v. Adebogun, 2021 SKCA 136, [2022] 1 W.W.R. 187, at para. 24; R. v. Kontzamanis, 2011 BCCA 184, at para. 38 (CanLII)). Common sense underpins well-established principles guiding credibility assessment — including the now-universal idea that witnesses who are inconsistent are less likely to be telling the truth — and assists in assessing the scope and impact of particular inconsistencies. Reliability also requires reference to common-sense assumptions about how witnesses perceive, remember, and relay information, invoking generalizations about how individuals tend to present information that they are remembering accurately and completely, as opposed to matters about which they are unsure or mistaken. A trial judge may, for example, infer that a witness was credible yet unreliable because they appeared sincere but displayed indicia that tend to suggest an unclear or uncertain memory (e.g., equivocation, phrases such as “hmm . . . let me see”, long pauses, or failure to provide much detail).

    [81]                        Assessments of credibility and reliability can be the most important judicial determinations in a criminal trial. They are certainly among the most difficult. This is especially so in sexual assault cases, which often involve acts that allegedly occurred in private and hinge on the contradictory testimony of two witnesses. The trial judge, while remaining grounded in the totality of the evidence, is obliged to evaluate the testimony of each witness and to make determinations that are entirely personal and particular to that individual. Credibility and reliability assessments are also context-specific and multifactorial: they do not operate along fixed lines and are “more of an ‘art than a science’” (S. (R.D.), at para. 128; R. v. Gagnon, 2006 SCC 17, [2006] 1 S.C.R. 621).[4] With respect to credibility in particular, while coherent reasons are crucial, it is often difficult for trial judges to precisely articulate the reasons why they believed or disbelieved a witness due to “the complex intermingling of impressions that emerge after watching and listening to witnesses and attempting to reconcile the various versions of events” (Gagnon, at para. 20; see also R. v. R.E.M., 2008 SCC 51, [2008] 3 S.C.R. 3, at para. 28; R. v. G.F., 2021 SCC 20, [2021] 1 S.C.R. 801, at para. 81). The task is further complicated by the trial judge’s ability to accept some, all, or none of a witness’s testimony.

    92.      I will, of course, apply these fundamental legal principles to the evidence called at J.M.’s trial.

    Inconsistencies:

    93.      Further, I will adhere to the general direction offered by way of an endorsement of the Court of Appeal for Ontario in R. v. A.M., 2014 ONCA 769 as follows:

    [8] Several basic principles inform our decision regarding the trial judge's reasons for judgment.

    [9] First, every witness, irrespective of age, is an individual whose credibility and evidence should be assessed according to criteria appropriate to his or her mental development, understanding and ability to communicate: R. v. W. (R.), 1992 CanLII 56 (SCC), [1992] 2 S.C.R. 122, [1992] S.C.J. No. 56, at p. 134 S.C.R.

    [10] Second, no inflexible rules mandate when a witness' evidence should be evaluated according to "adult" or "child" standards. Indeed, in its provisions regarding testimonial capacity, the Canada Evidence Act, R.S.C., 1985, c. C-5 eschews any reference to "adult" or "child", preferring the terms "14 years or older" and "under 14 years of age". An inflexible, category-based system would resurrect stereotypes as rigid and unyielding as those rejected by the recent developments in our approach to children's evidence: W. (R.), at p. 134 S.C.R.

    [11] Third, despite this flexibility, there are some guiding principles. Generally, where an adult testifies about events that occurred when she was a child, her credibility should be assessed according to the criteria applicable to adult witnesses. However, the presence of inconsistencies, especially on peripheral matters such as time and location, should be considered in the context of her age at the time the events about which she is testifying occurred: W. (R.), at p. 134 S.C.R. See, also, R. v. Kendall, 1962 CanLII 7 (SCC), [1962] S.C.R. 469, [1962] S.C.J. No. 27.

    [12] Fourth, one of the most valuable means of assessing witness credibility is to examine the consistency between what the witness said in the witness box and what she has said on other occasions, whether or not under oath: R. v. G. (M.), 1994 CanLII 8733 (ON CA), [1994] O.J. No. 2086, 93 C.C.C. (3d) 347 (C.A.), at p. 354 C.C.C., leave to appeal to S.C.C. refused [1994] S.C.C.A. No. 390. Inconsistencies may emerge in a witness' testimony at trial, or between their trial testimony and statements previously given. Inconsistencies [page540] may also emerge from things said differently at different times, or from omitting to refer to certain events at one time while referring to them on other occasions.

    [13] Inconsistencies vary in their nature and importance. Some are minor, others are not. Some concern material issues, others peripheral subjects. Where an inconsistency involves something material about which an honest witness is unlikely to be mistaken, the inconsistency may demonstrate a carelessness with the truth about which the trier of fact should be concerned: G. (M.), at p. 354 C.C.C.

    [14] Fifth, a trial judge giving reasons for judgment is neither under the obligation to review and resolve every inconsistency in a witness' evidence, nor respond to every argument advanced by counsel: R. v. M. (R.E.), [2008] 3 S.C.R. 3, [2008] S.C.J. No. 52, 2008 SCC 51, at para. 64. That said, a trial judge should address and explain how she or he has resolved major inconsistencies in the evidence of material witnesses: G. (M.), at p. 356 C.C.C.; R. v. Dinardo, [2008] 1 S.C.R. 788, [2008] S.C.J. No, 24, 2008 SCC 24, at para. 31.

    [15] Sixth, prior consistent statements of a witness are not admissible for their truth: R. v. Stirling, [2008] 1 S.C.R. 272, [2008] S.C.J. No. 10, 2008 SCC 10, at para. 7. Mere repetition of a story on a prior occasion does not make the in-court description of the events any more credible or reliable: R. v. Curto, [2008] O.J. No. 889, 2008 ONCA 161, 230 C.C.C. (3d) 145, at paras. 32, 35; R. v. Ay, 1994 CanLII 8749 (BC CA), [1994] B.C.J. No. 2024, 93 C.C.C. (3d) 456 (C.A.), at p. 471 C.C.C.

    The Meaning of Proof beyond a Reasonable Doubt:

    94.      In a case I decided a couple of years ago, R. v. McLaren, 2023 ONCJ 92 (CanLII), I adverted, as I must, to the meaning of “reasonable doubt”.  I had the following to say:

    181.   The standard of proof beyond a reasonable doubt is an age-old principle of criminal law in Canada and virtually every other common law jurisdiction. Triers of fact must be ever conscious of the high burden of proof placed upon the Crown, if ever the accused’s right to be presumed innocent is to be rebutted.

    182.   Recently, my colleague, Brochu J., in R. v. Hawryluk, (unreported), offered these helpful commentaries on the meaning of reasonable doubt:

    [39] Like every person accused of a criminal offence, Mr. Hawryluk begins this trial presumed to be innocent of the offence with which he is charged. That presumption remains intact unless and until the Crown proves beyond a reasonable doubt that he is guilty. It is the Crown that bears the onus of proving the essential elements of the offence beyond a reasonable doubt.  That onus never shifts to the accused.

    [40] Intermingled with the presumption of innocence is the standard of proof required to displace that presumption. To secure a conviction in a criminal case, the Crown must establish each essential element of the charge against the accused beyond reasonable doubt. This standard of proof is very stringent. It is a standard far beyond the civil threshold of proof on a balance of probabilities.

    [41] The expression “proof beyond a reasonable doubt" has no precise definition, but it is well understood. The Supreme Court of Canada outlined a suggested model jury charge in R. v. Lifchus, 1997 CanLII 319 (SCC), [1997] 3 S.C.R. 320. This is the definitive guide for criminal trial courts in Canada. It is worth setting out here verbatim:

    *  The term "beyond a reasonable doubt" has been used for a very long time and is a part of our history and traditions of justice. It is so engrained in our criminal law that some think it needs no explanation, yet something must be said regarding its meaning.

    *  A reasonable doubt is not an imaginary or frivolous doubt. It must not be based upon sympathy or prejudice. Rather, it is based on reason and common sense. It is logically derived from the evidence or absence of evidence.

    *  Even if you believe the accused is probably guilty or likely guilty, that is not sufficient. In those circumstances you must give the benefit of the doubt to the accused and acquit because the Crown has failed to satisfy you of the guilt of the accused beyond a reasonable doubt.

    *  On the other hand you must remember that it is virtually impossible to prove anything to an absolute certainty and the Crown is not required to do so. Such a standard of proof is impossibly high.

    *  In short if, based upon the evidence before the court, you are sure that the accused committed the offence you should convict since this demonstrates that you are satisfied of his guilt beyond a reasonable doubt.

    [42] Three years later, in R. v. Avetysan, 2000 SCC 56 (CanLII), [2000] 2 S.C.R. 745, the Supreme Court of Canada clarified at para. 13 that:

    In situating the criminal standard of proof, “it falls much closer to absolute certainty than to proof on a balance of probabilities”:  Starr, at para. 242, per Iacobucci J.

    W.(D.):

    95.      Further, in assessing the evidence of an accused, I will bear in mind the dicta of L’Heureux-Dubé J. in R. v. L. (D.O.), 1993 CanLII 46 (SCC), [1993] 4 S.C.R. 419, where she stated:

    Appropriate Test

    79 The respondent submitted a brief to the trial judge with regard to the appropriate test for weighing the evidence and in particular with regard to assessing the credibility of witnesses. He argued that the proper test was whether an account given by, or on behalf, of an accused might "reasonably be true". The trial judge did not agree and explained:

    Whether an account given by, or on behalf of, an accused might reasonably be true, is not in my view the honest and proper and established test of whether the Crown's evidence should be rejected. It is simply one factor in assessing the overall impact of the evidence as a whole. If one were to determine criminal cases simply on an academic test, unrelated to all the other facts, of whether something might reasonably be true, much of the impact of truly and compellingly credible Crown evidence such as that here, would go for naught, and truth would be subjugated by plausibility.

    In my view, the trial judge was correct, as was O'Sullivan J.A. who succinctly enunciated the test as follows (at p. 317):

    The only question for the trier of fact at the end of the trial is whether or not, on the whole of the evidence, the Crown has proved its case beyond a reasonable doubt. If it has, the accused must be convicted. If there is a reasonable doubt, the accused must be acquitted.

    This is the proper test on which Cory J. in R. v. W. (D.), 1991 CanLII 93 (SCC), [1991] 1 S.C.R. 742, at pp. 757-58, expanded as follows:

    A trial judge might well instruct the jury on the question of credibility along these lines:

    First, if you believe the evidence of the accused, obviously you must acquit.

    Second, if you do not believe the testimony of the accused but you are left in reasonable doubt by it, you must acquit.

    Third, even if you are not left in doubt by the evidence of the accused, you must ask yourself whether, on the basis of the evidence which you do accept, you are convinced beyond a reasonable doubt by that evidence of the guilt of the accused.

    The respondent's submissions on this point, also made to us, cannot succeed.

    Analysis:

    Do I believe the evidence of J.M.?

    96.      I cannot believe J.M.’s evidence for three main reasons.  Firstly, his absolute certainty that he had only three short cans of Budweiser on the night in question is not credible in the slightest.  It taints his veracity on virtually everything else he says about his recollection of P.P.’s last night at his trailer home on August 26, going into the wee hours of the morning of August 27, 2022.

    97.      J.M. had no reason to reflect on the level of his alcohol consumption that night until almost a year after the fact when P.P. decided to confirm for her parents their suspicion as to why she was reluctant to visit C.M. and J.M. during the summer of 2023.  It makes no sense that J.M. would remember with such clarity the actual number of beers he drank, two at the campfire, one while watching the movie, no more, no less.  Yet he maintains he can.  Why then could he not remember what he ate for dinner that night?

    98.      Secondly, his insistence that he used the bathroom at the end of the movie, but not during the night, leaves me with no confidence either that he was telling the truth about how many times he had to go, nor that his recollection of it is accurate.  Whether one goes to the washroom during the night is a mundane detail.  It is not something likely to be committed to, or to be etched in one’s memory, especially when the need to recall the number of one’s trips does not arise until a year later.

    99.      J.M.’s evidence also conflicted somewhat with his wife, C.M.’s, on this point.  She was clear that it was not unusual for him to use the washroom during the night.

    100.   To my mind, J.M. was plainly being dishonest with this Court when asserting that he knew he did not wake through the night, and that C.M. was snuggled into him for the duration.  His aim, it seems, was to rule out any opportunity for him to have done to P.P. what she says he did. He simply needed to change from sleeping on his right side to his left to gain access to P.P.  He would only have to put his arm over C.M.’s body to do so.  It was ridiculous for him to suggest he could not reach her.  The three of them occupied a queen-size bed in his trailer home.  P.P. was always literally an arm’s length away from J.M. throughout the night in question with some simple repositioning on his part.

    101.   Thirdly, and less significantly, I seriously doubt he waited to have his first beer at the campfire, and not earlier in the evening, after other adult company, C. and B., had arrived, and even though C.M. was already having a glass of wine over dinner.

    102.   Accordingly, I cannot find that J.M. was a credible and reliable witness.

    Does the evidence of J.M.. raise a reasonable doubt?

    103.   It does not.  It was strained and contrived, in my view, to marry up with his wife, C.M.’s testimony.  He did not wish to contradict her because he clearly understood her testimony could completely exculpate him, if believed.

    104.   J.M.’s evidence struck me as being intent upon parroting almost everything about which his wife, C.M., had previously testified.  J.M. had the benefit of listening to her give her evidence before him.   The only significant difference in her evidence and his was whether P.P. typically wore a nightgown to bed.  J.M. said he could not remember a single instance when P.P. did, whereas C.M. agreed that that was her daughter’s usual bedtime attire. To my mind, C.M.’s concession on this point enhanced her credibility.  J.M.’s apparent lack of recall that P.P. typically wore a nightgown detracted from his.

    105.   I will give credit to J.M. for conceding that he could have put his hand down P.P.’s pants if he had tried, but his testimony still does not raise a reasonable doubt, in my mind, about whether he sexually touched P.P.

    On the basis of the evidence I do accept, am I convinced beyond a reasonable doubt in the guilt of J.M.?

    106.   My assessment of the evidence as a whole adduced at J.M.’s trial does not, of course, end with the completion of my inquiries under the first two branches of W.(D.). I must articulate what I am convinced of beyond a reasonable doubt.

    107.   Turning my mind to the evidence which I do accept, I am confident in making the following findings.  They sequentially are:

    a)      After a night involving alcohol consumption by C.M. and J.M. around a campfire, P.P. was invited into their bed to watch the movie, “The Greatest Showman”.  It was a treat for the then 10-year-old child, and her idea to be permitted to do so. 

    b)      It was also the only time P.P. slept in that bed.  It was the last night of her summer stay with her mother, whom she would not see again for some time.

    c)      P.P. may have fallen asleep during the movie, or she may have watched it to its conclusion.  Nothing turns on whether she did or not.

    d)      Most critically, I believe C.M. that she moved P.P. from her placement initially between the adults in the bed to have the child sleep on the other side of her mother, so that P.P. would be impeded by C.M.’s body from having even accidental contact with J.M. This makes sense for reasons of propriety. 

    e)      C.M. is a light sleeper and P.P. a restless one.  I accept C.M.’s evidence that she was periodically struck in her side by P.P. as the child fidgeted in her sleep in a strange bed.

    f)      C.M. is not a woman prepared to stand by her man at all costs, to lie for him to protect him, and to concoct a story to exonerate him.  She was a mother pained by the choice she had to make in testifying as she did.  I believe what she told the Court about where her daughter slept through the night and why.  I do so in spite of what P.P. told police was her mother’s tendency never to believe “anything I say most of the times and lots of times”.  P.P.’s allegiance lies with her father and her stepmother, her custodial parents.

    g)      In essence, I assess C.M.’s account of what happened in that bed as both honest and accurate.  At a minimum, it was capable of reasonably being true, so as to raise a reasonable doubt in my mind as trier of fact.

    h)      J.M. and C.M. spoke on several occasions about their recollections of the night in question.  They consulted photos to assist them in recalling details such as the clothing P.P. wore at the campfire and to bed.  That is normal for a couple such as J.M. and C.M. to do when a serious allegation such as the one levelled at J.M. by P.P. was made almost a year after the fact.

    i)      In my view, C.M.’s credibility would have been severely and negatively impacted if she had denied having such conversations with J.M. As a caring and loving mother to P.P., I expect C.M. would have been all over J.M. when the accusation was first made by her daughter, P.P.  As a mother, C.M. would have made her inquiries of J.M. with vigour to try to get at the truth.

    The Inconsistencies:

    108.   I consider the following to be a major inconsistency in the evidence of P.P.:

    a)      When challenged by defence counsel that she spoke to J.M. while he was trying to reach under her nightgown to touch her vagina, she denied she had.  She then was reminded of what she said to police during her videotaped interview, a statement which she gave some time ago on August 4, 2023, but which was played in Court on April 25, 2025, for her to adopt under s. 715.1 of the Code to form part of her evidence-in-chief, a short while before her cross-examination commenced.

    b)      All other inconsistencies were minor and peripheral.  P.P. is a bright and energetic child.  She was fluid in her answers offered to police in giving her statement, as well as when questioned by Crown and defence counsel in Court.  She did not strike me as a vengeful child with an animus for J.M. bent on telling falsehoods about him.

    c)      For example, whether C. and B. were present in the spare bedroom or not, when P.P. alleged J.M. was sexually touching her, is trivial in nature and of minor significance.  Her older sister’s presence with her fiancé in a different bedroom is a nonfactor.   

     

    Conclusion:

     

    109.  Let me be clear.  P.P. could have been telling this Court the truth.  However, that possibility comes nowhere close to meeting the high threshold the Crown must attain to persuade a trier of fact beyond a reasonable doubt that the offence(s), with which an accused has or have been charged, have been made out.  Her mother, C.M.’s evidence, prevents me from being sure that J.M. sexually touched her daughter as P.P. testified he did.

    110.  C.M. neither saw nor heard anything untoward occurring despite being a light sleeper and being present in the bed for the entire night in question.

    111.  There was also the influence exerted by P.P.’s father and stepmother over P.P. which troubled me.  It sounded quite clear to me as though the suggestion was made to P.P. by her father and stepmother that the reason for her reluctance in wanting to go visit C.M. and J.M. was because P.P. had been sexually touched by J.M.  P.P.’s adopted statement given to police speaks loudly to her disclosure of the incident coming to light in this way.

    112.  I also highly doubt that a 10-year-old child would form the opinion that J.M. is an alcoholic and has liver problems completely on her own.

    113.   In sum, I am not convinced beyond a reasonable doubt in J.M.’s guilt after a complete review of the evidence called at his trial.

    114.   For the above reasons, I must acquit him.  Assessing the evidence in a balanced and fair manner as a whole as I must, I cannot be sure whether J.M. sexually touched or sexually assaulted P.P.
