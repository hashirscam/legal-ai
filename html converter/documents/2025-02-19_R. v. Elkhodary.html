    ONTARIO COURT OF JUSTICE

     

    HIS MAJESTY THE KING

    v.

    WAISUDDIN AKBARI

     

     

    R E A S O N S   F O R   J U D G M E N T

     

    BEFORE THE HONOURABLE JUSTICE B. BROWN

    On February 19, 2025, at TORONTO, Ontario

     

     

     

    APPEARANCES:

     

    C. Coughlan                     Counsel for the Crown

    J. Ortega                       Counsel for Omar Elkhodary

     

     

    BROWN J.: (Orally)

     

     

    Mr. Omar Elkhodary is charged with an assault on Vicky Moscoe on November 2nd, 2025, contrary to Section 266 of the Criminal Code.  The Crown proceeded summarily.  The trial commenced on January 20th, 2025.  The court made an order excluding witnesses.

     

    The Crown called the complainant, Ms. Vicky Moscoe, and put in evidence two video clips, one of which recorded events from the complainant's cellphone at the time of the allegations.  The defence called two witnesses, Mr. Omar Elkhodary and his girlfriend, Ms. Jo Marie Ortega.  The allegation is that Mr. Elkhodary pushed Ms. Moscoe.  The defence has argued that Mr. Elkhodary was acting in self-defence.

     

    UNCONTRADICTED EVIDENCE

     

    The allegations relate to November 2nd, 2025, in the evening around 7:20 p.m., on November 2nd or 3rd of 2025.  There is no issue in this trial as to the date of the allegations.  This was a few weeks after the October 7th, 2024 attack by what the complainant related was by the Hamas terrorists, wherein numerous hostages were taken from Israel.  Ms. Moscoe had posters of children hostages she was placing on poles in the area to raise awareness about the hostages.  One was of a little girl, “Aviv”, with her mother and little sister.  Another poster was of a little girl separate from “Aviv”.

     

    Ms. Moscoe was on the east side of Yonge Street just south of Empress in the City of Toronto.  She was in the process of placing the posters on poles in that area.  She was approached by Mr. Elkhodary who started to tear down the posters she had placed on the poles.  As he had his hands up to tear down her posters, Ms. Moscoe put up her hand on the poster to stop him from pulling it down.  This was recorded on the video clip in evidence.  The video clip also recorded a number of statements made by both parties.  The allegation related to events that followed those acts. 

     

    After the exchange with the complainant, there were two other women in the area who approached.  These women were not known to the complainant.  They appeared to try to intervene to stop Mr. Elkhodary from doing what he was doing and the second video clip relates to their involvement.  This involvement with the two women is not the subject of any charges in this trial. 

     

    ANALYSIS

     

    The defendant, Mr. Elkhodary, is presumed to be innocent.  The Crown must prove his guilt beyond a reasonable doubt.  The defendant has no burden to prove anything, to explain anything, or to persuade the court of anything.  The principle of reasonable doubt also applies to the issue of credibility.  The assessment of credibility is not a matter of choosing between competing versions, or determining which is more credible or to be preferred.

     

    Where the defendant has testified, or called evidence from other defence witnesses, or exculpatory evidence has been elicited from other witnesses in the trial, that evidence is entitled to the benefit of the application of reasonable doubt in the assessment of its credibility and the fact-finding that follows.  If the evidence is believed and it affords a defence, the defendant is entitled to an acquittal.  Even if that evidence is not believed in the sense of believing it to be true, if it nonetheless raises a reasonable doubt, the defendant is entitled to that doubt and an acquittal will follow.  Even if that evidence is rejected, the Crown must still prove guilt beyond a reasonable doubt, based on all of the evidence in the trial. 

     

    There is no legal requirement that the evidence of a complainant be corroborated, that is, supported by other independent evidence.  A determination of guilt or innocence must not turn into a mere credibility contest between two witnesses, or a bipolar choice between competing evidence called by the Crown and by the defence.  This approach would erode the presumption of innocence and the burden on the Crown of proving guilt beyond a reasonable doubt.  In the often quoted reasons of the Supreme Court of Canada in R. v. W.(D.), 1991 CanLII 93 (SCC), [1991] 63 CCC (3d) 397, the Court suggested an appropriate jury instruction for the consideration of evidence in the trial, which this Court finds is appropriately captured as follows:

     

    1. if you believe the evidence of the accused and it affords a defence, obviously you must acquit.

     

    2. if you do not believe the testimony of the accused but you are left in a reasonable doubt by it, you must acquit, and 

     

    3. even if you are not left in doubt by the evidence of the accused, you must ask yourself whether, on the basis of the evidence which you do accept, and on the basis of all of the evidence in the trial, you are convinced beyond a reasonable doubt, by that evidence, of the guilt of the accused. 

     

    SELF-DEFENCE

     

    The court also considers the law with respect to self-defence.  This is set out in Section 34 of the Criminal Code which states: 

     

    34 (1) A person is not guilty of an offence if

     

    (a) they believe on reasonable grounds that force is being used against them or another person or that a threat of force is being made against them or another person;

     

    (b) the act that constitutes the offence is committed for the purpose of defending or protecting themselves or the other person from that use or threat of force; and

     

    (c) the act committed is reasonable in the circumstances.

     

    (2) In determining whether the act committed is reasonable in the circumstances, the court shall consider the relevant circumstances of the person, the other parties and the act, including, but not limited to, the following factors:

     

    (a)   the nature of the force or threat;

     

    (b)   the extent to which the use of force was imminent and whether there were other means available to respond to the potential use of force;

     

    (c)   the person's role in the incident;

     

    (d)   whether any party to the incident used threatened to use a weapon;

     

    (e)    the size, age, gender and physical capabilities of the parties to the incident;

     

    (f)   the nature, duration and history of any relationship between the parties to the incident, including any prior use or threat of force and the nature of that force or threat;

     

    (f.1) any history of interaction or communication between the parties to the incident;

     

    (g) the nature and proportionality of the person's response to the use or threat of force; and

     

    (g)   whether the act committed was in response to a use or threat of force that the person knew was lawful.

     

    Section 34(3) is not quoted as it is not relevant in this case. 

     

    The Supreme Court of Canada considered this provision in R. v. Khill, 2021 SCC 37.  The court considers self-defence in light of this judgment.

     

    For the defence in this trial, the court will consider whether the accused first, 

     

    believed on reasonable grounds that force was threatened or being used against him or someone else; 

     

    whether he acted for the purpose of defending himself or the other person; and, 

     

    whether the accused's actions were reasonable in the circumstances.

     

    As characterized by the court in Khill at paragraph 18, the Crown bears the onus of proving beyond a reasonable doubt that the answer to at least one of these questions is no.  In Khill, the court noted that the act must be reasonable in the circumstances and the fact finder must consider a wide range of factors to determine what a reasonable person would have done in a comparable situation.  The current provision came into force in March of 2013.  A driving force to the amendments to the Criminal Code was the desire to simplify the law of self-defence. 

     

    At that time, the offences and situations to which self-defence could apply were expanded.  However, there was a greater degree of flexibility to the trier of fact in being the requirement that the court assess the reasonableness of the accused's response by reference to a non-exhaustive list of factors.  The key issue considered by the court in Khill was the accused's role in the incident and the extent to which the accused played a role in bringing about the conflict.  In consideration of that aspect, the conduct of the accused sheds light on the nature and extent of the accused's responsibility for the final confrontation that culminated in the act giving rise to the charge. 

     

    In considering the new provisions for self-defence, the Supreme Court of Canada in Khill at paragraph 39, noted that the scope and application of self-defence was broadened and that it employed a multifactorial reasonableness assessment.  The court also predicted in paragraph 44 that additional claims of self-defence will be placed before triers of fact.  However, as noted in paragraph 46, based upon the requirement to consider enumerated factors, the provisions narrow the scope of self-defence in some factual circumstances and broaden it in others. 

     

    In that case at trial, the Crown argued that the actions of the accused were rash and unreasonable, that he could have exercised a number of prudent alternatives, including calling 911, staying in the house, and that it was only a property offence.  The defence had submitted on appeal that the Section 34(2)(c) criteria should be limited to unlawful, provocative, or morally blameworthy conduct on the part of the accused and not a broader type of conduct of an accused in a wider context in which the accused acted: See paragraph 26. 

     

    It is important to bear in mind, as set out in paragraph 66, that the court can consider any mistaken beliefs reasonably held by the accused and the subjective purpose of the accused under Sections 34(1)(a) and (b) and the criteria in 34(2).  It is noteworthy that Section 34(1)(c) is primarily concerned with the reasonableness of the accused's actions, not his mental state.  In order to properly consider the relevant issues, the court needs to consider the evidence and make findings of fact. 

     

    CONSIDERATION OF EVIDENCE

    Before assessing the credibility of the various witnesses, the court will summarize the evidence on the video clips relevant to the court's consideration of the testimony of the witnesses in the assessment of evidence in this case.

     

    Video Clip of Allegations 

     

    This first video clip is fairly brief, and it was marked as Exhibit 1.  It is the product of filing on the smartphone held by the complainant, Ms. Moscoe, as she held it in her hand.  The view and the vantage point is therefore constrained from being held by the complainant as she was engaged in an interaction with Mr. Elkhodary.  For instance, it does not show the full view of both parties from a point further back, in order to capture everything that took place.

     

    There is no doubt that as the camera was filming, Mr. Elkhodary used this as a tool on his behalf to narrate events.  For instance, at the outset, he said, "You just physically assaulted me" when he was, at that point, clearly several feet away from the complainant.  He then chose to walk back towards the complainant and where she was located near a pole where the poster had been placed.  It is important, this court would note, that even if the complainant had touched him earlier, his response was to walk back to the complainant, rather than to continue his departure from the scene.  In other words, he chose to go back to the complainant and continue to rip the posters and potentially have a confrontation with the complainant as a result, and that is what took place. 

     

    This video clip is significant and it is helpful in the consideration of testimony in this case.  It is only 1 minute and 29 seconds in length.  No transcript was provided for this clip.  The court is not going to produce a transcript of everything on the video as it can be played for details. 

     

    Although Mr. Elkhodary used profanities when he spoke, and this was recorded on the video, and the court would generally not use those profanities, it is important to state the actual evidence to reflect the true context of the events.  When the complainant asked him his name, he said, "None of your fucking business".  He then reached over to the pole and there was the noise of what seemed to be removing tape from the pole as he narrated, "Are you touching me?"  As she spoke, he said, "Did you just touch me on your own camera?"  The court would note that this does not grammatically make sense to touch someone on your camera, but again Mr. Elkhodary seemed focused to allege that the complainant had touched him, as this was the second time he stated this. 

     

    The complainant explained in testimony that the posters were of hostages and he said, "I don't give a fuck" as he reached over again to get closer to the posters.  He clearly continued to take down the posters in front of the complainant.  He turned away from the complainant to respond to a woman who was to his left, away from the position of the pole.  A female voice then said, he does not have to do that and he asked if she has a problem.  He then said, "What the fuck?", the same swear word he had been using earlier.  He said, “What the fuck is your problem?  Leave.”  He then went back to removing the poster from the pole.  He seemed to be directing that comment towards the woman who was behind some distance.

     

    He turned to continue what he was doing with the pole removing the tape and the posters across from the complainant.  The other two women were not in view.  In response to his actions, the complainant stated, "No, I want these two" referring to the posters of the children.  At the time, the video moved slightly.  This was at the point that the complainant testified that he pushed the complainant, as she narrated in her viva voce testimony in court.  That was evident as the video moved.  Immediately after this point, one other woman moved over to his right as he continued to remove the posters, and the complainant appeared in a position somewhat facing him as the video continued. 

     

    Mr. Elkhodary's girlfriend was already in the area standing next to him as he was across from the complainant.  As pointed out in the testimony of the complainant in court in reference to this video, immediately after he pushed the complainant, one other woman moved into the area.  She moved next to Mr. Elkhodary.  There was no assault captured on the video clip of the other woman towards Mr. Elkhodary.

     

    Mr. Elkhodary made statements that people have assaulted him, which are not captured on the video.  Mr. Elkhodary still continues to go back to the post to continue to remove the poster.  During this time, his girlfriend is standing next to him as he continues to remove the poster. 

     

    The Posters that were removed

     

    Exhibit 3 was a photograph of the two posters that were removed and crumpled up and given back to the complainant after they were taken down.  The first shows, "Raz, 4 years old, Israeli, kidnapped from her home by Hamas".  The second shows, "Aviv, 2 years old, Israeli, kidnapped from her home by Hamas".  Both posters state below the images:

     

    On October 7th, nearly 200 innocent civilians were abducted from Israel into the Gaza Strip.  Their whereabouts remain unknown.  More than 3,000 women, men and children, ranging in age from 3 months to 85 years old were wounded, murdered, beaten, raped and brutally separated from loved ones by Hamas.

     

    And then it stated, "Take a photo of this poster and share it.  Please help bring them...." and then the poster was ripped so you can't read what it says after that. 

     

    Second Video Clip

     

    In the second video, marked Exhibit 2, the court indicated that it was of marginal relevance at the time of the objection to its admissibility by the defence.  It showed the poster that was retrieved from Mr. Elkhodary's girlfriend and returned to the complainant.  This is the poster that was filed as Exhibit 3.  However, although the defence objected to the admissibility of this video clip early on, it appears to show a portion of the subsequent events that relate to Mr. Elkhodary's testimony that the other two women assaulted him. 

     

    The complainant testified that this video was taken seconds after the first video.  The court had to rule upon the admissibility of this video prior to the defence advancing self-defence in this case.  This indirectly made the video more relevant, ironically, even though the defence initially opposed its admissibility in evidence. This video clip is significantly longer, running 5 minutes and 40 seconds.  It was also filmed on the complainant's smartphone. 

     

    It starts off showing Mr. Elkhodary using both of his hands to hold a female who was shorter than him.  As he held on to the female, he pushed her backwards across the sidewalk.  She was alone at the time.  His girlfriend came over to Mr. Elkhodary.  As this point, that female was outnumbered by the two of them, being Mr. Elkhodary and his girlfriend.  Then a second female ran over to the area.  She was also shorter than Mr. Elkhodary and she appeared to use her hands for a few seconds to slap him with an open hand, wearing gloves to hit his arms.  At that point, the first female left the area, as did the second female.

     

    Mr. Elkhodary then followed the second female as she left and the confrontation continued.  The accused’s girlfriend remained beside him.  The four people then appeared to argue as the complainant filmed them.  The accused then exchanged words with the complainant.  At 50 seconds or so, Mr. Elkhodary left the area, walking with his girlfriend.  Then he went back to the area where his glasses had fallen on the sidewalk where he had the physical exchange with the other female.  It appears that the parties were exchanging words as they waited for the police to attend.  As indicated, towards the end of the video, the complainant asked Mr. Elkhodary's girlfriend for the crumpled poster she had been holding in her hand, which was turned over to the complainant and marked later as Exhibit 3. 

     

    Viva Voce Testimony Witnesses

     

    In this case, the court starts with an assessment of the evidence of Mr. Elkhodary.

     

    Omar Ashraf Elkhodary was born in Scarborough.  His mother is Lebanese and his father is Egyptian.  They immigrated to Canada.  He testified that his mother remarried and she is married to a Palestinian who has family members there.  He testified that he disagreed with the posters on a fundamental level.  He stated that he disagreed with spreading hateful propaganda and that the posters were fabricated.  He testified it was a simple protest of misinformation.  He indicated that the complainant argued with him and tried to knock him away from the poster and he said, don’t touch me, leave me alone.

     

    Then Mr. Elkhodary and the complainant got into a verbal argument. He testified that he told her not to touch him and she continued to try to knock his hands away. He said she tried to slap his hand away. He testified that he had told people in the area and that they swarmed. They were hitting. The three of them were surrounding him. The court is summarizing his evidence and the way he gave it, not grammatically changing anything.

     

    He testified he felt pressure behind him. He was pulled in one direction and then some force behind him kicked or was behind him. He told them not to touch him, to leave him. He defended himself. He told them he got them to stop touching him. He testified he pushed them away for self-defence. He testified he did not want a physical altercation, that he did not want to be touched. He agreed that he asked the two women passing by to leave the area.

     

    He did not wish to agree with matters that were clearly factual that might put him in a bad light. For instance, he is 5 feet, 11 inches in height and he was asked if he was taller than the victim. The complainant had just travelled in and out of the courtroom before his testimony, in addition to the extended period of time he saw her during the allegations. He refused to admit that he was taller than the victim, stating, "I do not know how tall she is". The court would note that he was not asked her height, he was simply asked if he was taller than the victim. However, he did admit that the other two women were shorter than him. The court would note, Mr. Elkhodary is clearly taller than the complainant. The court saw that.

     

    He did admit that his girlfriend was in the area, so it is clear that he was not alone. The court would note that initially, Mr. Elkhodary and his girlfriend outnumbered the complainant who was placing the posters, working alone. She did not know the other two females who came along after the fact.

     

    In cross-examination, the Crown suggested that Mr. Elkhodary pushed the complainant away and he responded, he has seen the video and that allegation. The court would note that Mr. Elkhodary did not deny or disagree with the suggestion in responding to the question. He did not agree that she was not hitting or slapping his hands away at the time. His position was that it was self-defence when he pushed her away. Essentially, the court would note that he did admit that he pushed the complainant away but added that it was in self-defence.

     

    He was cross-examined as to whether he felt threatened by the complainant and he responded that he felt trapped and swarmed by three people at the same time. He did not agree that he was taller than the complainant, which the court indicated earlier, but he did admit that he was younger than the complainant.

     

    The court would also note that the evidence in this case reflected what the court finds were three parts to the incident:

     

    First, there was the exchange between the complainant and Mr. Elkhodary culminating in him pushing her. To that point, there was no assault by the complainant evident on the video and the court found that Mr. Elkhodary was narrating things on the video that were not reflected on the video. He admitted he pushed the complainant but argued in his testimony that it was in self-defence.

     

    The second part this court would find was when the other two females joined the group of Mr. Elkhodary, his girlfriend, and the complainant, who was previously alone with the accused and his girlfriend. At this later point, it is evident that there was some pushing and shoving with Mr. Elkhodary and the other two females.

     

    The third part is the second video, where Mr. Elkhodary, moments after the first video, is holding one of the two females as she is alone with him and he is clearly manhandling her by pushing her backwards down the sidewalk. Sometime after that, the second female joined her and tried to strike him.

     

    The whole sequence this court would find as in the video and admitted evidence in this case, was indicative of a push to the complainant, which was followed by the other two females joining the exchange. As evidence proceeded, Mr. Elkhodary clearly wanted a physical confrontation, which he had with the female on the second video as he pushed her backwards.

     

    When asked in cross-examination about the point in time just before he pushed the complainant, he was asked if he could have just stopped doing what he was doing and walk away. The court considers his answer to that question that he was already swarmed at the time and he had no room to move, he could not exercise his freewill to leave. The court would note, that was not believable, based upon what the court could see and has heard in evidence.

     

    However, this portion of the exchange took place after he pushed the complainant, not before or at the same time. He essentially tried to rely upon a potential explanation that he felt threatened by actions that transpired after he pushed the complainant as some sort of rationale as to why he pushed her. He pushed her, this court would find, because he wanted the complainant out of the way, so that he could successfully complete his actions in taking down the posters. The complainant was smaller than him and older than him. He was with his girlfriend. He was not alone.

     

    On a consideration of his evidence, together with the video evidence, the court finds that the motivation for his action at the time that he pushed the complainant was not in acting in fear of the complainant but pursuing his actions of returning to the area of the pole to continue to successfully remove the posters. The court finds that the motivating factor behind him pushing the complainant was to be able to get her out of the area so he could remove the posters. He was not acting in fear of the complainant after he returned to challenge her actions with posting the pictures of the children.

     

    He also testified that at this point he was not physically capable to walk away. The court would find that he had numerous opportunities to walk away from the pole, to leave the posters and leave the area and he clearly chose not to do so. He was on a mission, this court would find, to take down the posters. That was his purpose and his motivation for doing what he did, including pushing the complainant to get her out of the way as he continued to take down the posters.

     

    He was not acting in self-defence. The court rejects his evidence that he was acting in self-defence as a motivating factor when he pushed the complainant. Mr. Elkhodary wanted to make a visual point by taking down the posters in front of the complainant. He was cross-examined as to whether he could have just left and come back later to take down the posters. It was clear to the court that he wanted the audience of the complainant to his actions. He was cross-examined about having no reason to feel threatened or in fear of the complainant as she was not physically bigger than him. He did not answer the question and wanted it to be rephrased.

     

    He continued to state that he was swarmed by three people and did not respond to the scenario put to him when earlier he was dealing with the complainant and whether he had reason to feel threatened or any fear from the complainant. He stated that he was not physically capable to walk away. He stated that he had no room to move, no ability to exercise free will, as people's bodies were in his way. When asked in cross-examination if he stopped taking down the posters whether the people would have stopped swarming him, he stated he did not see the correlation between that because he had asked them to leave him alone.

     

    What this court finds lacking in credibility is his position that there was no correlation between the women in the area, that is to say the additional two women, and his taking down the posters. This defies belief. It is not believable and the court rejects his testimony. It was absolutely clear that the two additional women were concerned about what he was doing, including with the posters. It would be clear to any human being that his behaviour of taking down the posters of the child hostages was causing the women to come over and to try to stop him from doing that action. It was disingenuous for him to state to the court that there was no correlation or connection. The court rejects this testimony.

     

    He also effectively refused to answer the question in cross-examination as to whether he felt fear or threatened by the complainant at the time he pushed her. He turned the question to feeling threatened by three people swarming him.

     

    The court would note that at a later point in time, when there was scuffling between Mr. Elkhodary and the other two women, the court would accept that he might have been potentially acting in self-defence at this later point in time, but not as it related to acts of him pursuing the female on the second video, including pushing her backwards as he held on to her to do that. That would go against any sort of position that he was arguing he was acting in self-defence as he was pushing her backwards, away from the area. While he might have been struck by another female, shortly after he pushed this woman backwards, that was well after the portion of time that he admitted pushing the complainant. The court does not have a reasonable doubt arising from his evidence.

     

    Evidence of Jo Marie Ortega

     

    This witness is the partner of Mr. Elkhodary. She testified she was with him at the time of the allegations. They saw the posters and went to a post to take it down. She walked over, when she saw him in a conversation with the complainant. The two parties then had an argument about the poster. Later, two other women showed up. The court would note there is a clear demarcation of the times within which the two women showed up, after the exchange between the accused and the complainant. Then she said that all three women pulled him and almost swarmed him and tried to stop him from taking down the poster.

     

    The court would note significantly as it relates to this portion of her evidence, the complainant was holding a smartphone recording the initial video and that this does not disclose actions of the complainant in pulling Mr. Elkhodary. She was holding the phone in her hand as this was going on. This witness testified that the women were pulling him, dragging him and grabbing his arms and jacket in the back. This is not what the first video showed at the initial portion of the allegations, at the time just before Mr. Elkhodary pushed the complainant and while that was happening. It may have happened after the first video or after Mr. Elkhodary pushed the complainant. This witness testified that after he pushed the women off of him, she saw that take place.

     

    It is important to consider that her evidence is consistent with the potential self-defence action by Mr. Elkhodary taking place but a notable period of time after he pushed the complainant. It was interesting when the court considered the evidence of Mr. Elkhodary's girlfriend, Jo Marie Ortega, that she made the very clear connection that the women were trying to stop him from taking the down the posters, even if Mr. Elkhodary refused to acknowledge that correlation or connection. She did not relate the acts of her boyfriend immediately after, when he grabbed the smaller female and pushed her backwards as she was alone with him. She just left that out of the narrative she gave in court as it related to the acts with the three women.

     

    The court would also note that her narration of events and positions was contrary to the video as she indicated the female came to her left as she was behind Mr. Elkhodary. The video shows this female, in fact, to his right at the point. Yet she maintained the two women were behind Mr. Elkhodary and the complainant. If this was the position, then based on the evidence of this defence witness, Mr. Elkhodary was clearly free to leave the area. He could have left to his right.

     

    Further, when she described the swarming motion she saw, she related that they were pulling his hands down, grabbing his arms and hands and trying to stop him from taking the poster down. Then later in her cross-examination she testified that there was grabbing, pulling, and pushing in a different direction. But most significantly, this court would note she did not testify as to her boyfriend, Mr. Elkhodary, pushing the complainant, which he clearly admitted. It is also notable that she did admit that the complainant was holding her phone in one hand and using her second hand to grab his arm to stop him from taking down the poster.

     

    Parts of her evidence were lacking in reliability. For instance, she described one female who was shorter than him almost climb over him to grab his arms. That was not evident in the video at all, when the court takes into account the various angles, based upon the phone being held by the complainant.

     

    Overall, the court had real concerns about the credibility of the testimony of Mr. Elkhodary, as indicated earlier. The court has rejected his testimony with respect to the events in question, absent the admission that he pushed the complainant. He maintained through his testimony that, regardless, he was entitled to remove the posters and there was no connection between that act and the physicality that took place.

     

    He maintained that he had been swarmed by three people who did not respond to his commands to leave him alone. The court would add this was all the while he was continuing to take down the posters, an action with which the three women clearly took issue. He was essentially causing a conflict, a confrontation and then seeking to rely on self-defence when he refused to stop taking down the posters. He stated he felt claustrophobic and unable to exercise his free will. He said it was his free will to express himself by taking down the posters. He continued to maintain he had no room to leave the area.

     

    The court finds that he could have left the area before he pushed the complainant, which is the assault allegation being considered by the court in this trial. Although he had maintained he acted in self-defence by pushing the complainant, the court finds that he stayed in the area for a period of time even after the confrontation and did not leave, even though he stated at various points in his testimony that he was unable to leave. The court does not have a reasonable doubt with respect to the assault allegation pertaining to Ms. Moscoe arising from his testimony, especially when considered with the video evidence and the other evidence in the trial.

     

    The court would note that as it relates to the evidence of Ms. Ortega, the court found her evidence to be lacking in reliability and credibility, based upon what was shown in the video clips and also when compared to the evidence of Mr. Elkhodary, including the admission that he had pushed the complainant. She seemed to want to colour the events as her boyfriend being attacked by three women, which was not an accurate statement of what happened in this case relative to the allegation. She also had inconsistencies in her version when the court compared it with Mr. Elkhodary's testimony and the video clips. The court did not believe her evidence and does not have a reasonable doubt arising from her testimony.

     

    Evidence of the Complainant, Vicky Moscoe

     

    The complainant was very clear in her testimony that she was pushed before the two women came over to the area. She testified she was shoved and she felt her forehead being knocked by the defendant, Mr. Elkhodary. She felt the pain of being hit by his hand on her forehead.

     

    The court would note that this was at the point in time, as she relayed it in narrating with the video, that there was movement in the video as she held the phone while she was being pushed. The timing of this push to the complainant, being the assault allegation, was as indicated prior to the other two women arriving in the immediate area and that is significant in this trial.

     

    Ms. Moscoe testified that she was not touching Mr. Elkhodary as he stated on the video. She admitted that as she tried to stop Mr. Elkhodary from removing the posters that she had put on the pole, that her hand might have touched his hand as she tried to protect the posters, but she did not intentionally touch or assault him. She was forthright in her evidence in that regard.

     

    In cross-examination, the complainant maintained that the first person pushed was her and that Mr. Elkhodary had pushed her. She maintained that she did not see Mr. Elkhodary swarmed at all at that point. She was not intentionally touching him before he pushed her. She maintained, as she had throughout, that her fingers or hand might have come in contact with him when he tried to rip down the posters and she tried to protect the posters, but that any touching, if it happened at all, was not intentional.

     

    Ms. Moscoe was also consistent that Mr. Elkhodary was not surrounded by the other two females at the time, but rather that she was standing next to him, and his girlfriend was in the area. It is apparent in reviewing her testimony and the videotape evidence and given the timing she related as to when the camera shook and she was pushed by Mr. Elkhodary, he was not surrounded by the other two females and the complainant at the time. He was not swarmed, to use the term coined by the defence.

     

    Her evidence was consistent and made sense. She was not contradicted. She was forthright and made admissions against interest, including the scenario that her fingers might have come in contact with him as she tried to protect the posters and prevent them from being taken down. The court accepts her testimony as accurate and factual.

     

    FINDINGS OF FACT

     

    The court finds that Ms. Moscoe placed the posters on a post on the public street of child hostages from Israel. After she did so, Mr. Elkhodary approached in a confrontational manner and started tearing down the posters right in front of the complainant.

     

    This started a verbal dispute between the two parties as he swore at the complainant and as he continued to take down the posters. At one point he walked away and then he returned to continue to take down the posters. He was there with his girlfriend.

     

    Ms. Moscoe did not threaten Mr. Elkhodary, nor did she assault him.  While there might have been incidental physical contact between her fingers trying to keep the posters up on the post, as he relentlessly tried to rip them down in front of her, she did not assault him. He then pushed her. He did this, the court would find, for the purpose of getting her out of the way, to effectively intimidate her so that she would remove her hands from protecting the posters. His goal and purpose was laser focused, it was to tear down the posters of the children in front of Ms. Moscoe.

     

    After he pushed her, the other two young women in the area approached him. The court finds that he did not push the complainant in a purpose related to any self-defence, but rather to engage his goal of successfully taking down the posters.

     

    CONSIDERATION OF SELF-DEFENCE

     

    The court will consider the components of Section 34(1) of the Criminal Code, as stated above. The Crown bears the onus of proving beyond a reasonable doubt that the answer to at least one of these questions is no. The court will consider 34(1)(b) first. That can be considered as the motive for the actions, and the question is as follows:

     

    Did the accused do something for the purpose of defending or protecting themselves or another person from that use or threat of force, Section 34 (1) (b)?

     

    The purpose of the accused doing the act is relevant.  It is a subjective inquiry.  If there is no defensive or protective purpose, the rationale for the defence fails:  See paragraph 59 of Khill.

     

    Accordingly, if the acts of the accused are done for the purpose of vigilantism, vengeance, or for some other personal motivation and not for a defensive purpose, self-defence is not available.  While a situation may evolve from a different purpose, such as defense of property, it can transition into a situation of self-defence.

     

    The court would note there is no indication whatsoever that Mr. Elkhodary did anything for the purpose of defending his girlfriend.

     

    Based on the consideration of the evidence of Mr. Elkhodary, together with the evidence of Ms. Ortega and Ms. Moscoe, and the findings of fact, the court finds that Mr. Elkhodary pushed Ms. Moscoe away, not in self-defence, but in an action to further his purpose and motive of taking down the posters.  He wanted Ms. Moscoe out of the way, he wanted her to stop protecting the posters, and he pushed her to assist in this goal.  In that respect, the court finds that his motive in pushing Ms. Moscoe was not an act of self-defence, but rather an act to pursue the goal of taking down the posters. That, in and of itself, would disentitle self-defence in this case.

     

    In this case, the court has found that Mr. Elkhodary acted in pushing the complainant, not for the purpose of self-defence, but rather to get her out of the way so he could successfully take down the posters. The absence of the requisite purpose of Mr. Elkhodary acting in self-defence disentitles him to the defence.

     

    The court will nonetheless also consider the second question, which relates to the catalyst, and whether Mr. Elkhodary believed, on reasonable grounds, that force was being used or threatened against him or another person. Section 34(1)(a), the court needs to consider the accused's state of mind and perception of events that led him to act. For this element, the accused must subjectively believe that force or a threat thereof was being used against him or that of another. In the absence of that subjective belief, self-defence is not available to the accused.

     

    This belief must be held on reasonable grounds, which objective component is based upon the perspective of an ordinary person who shares the attributes, experiences, and circumstances of the accused, where those characteristics and experiences are relevant to their belief and actions. This is a blended or modified objective standard as stated in Khill: paragraph 54.

     

    What would a reasonable person with those relevant characteristics and experiences perceive? The Supreme Court of Canada explained that this requirement operates to shield otherwise criminal acts from punitive consequences, and as a result, the defence cannot depend exclusively on an accused's perception of the need to act. Through the reasonableness requirement, community norms and values are incorporated in weighing the moral blameworthiness of the actions of the accused. Quoting Justice Paciocco, currently a judge on the Court of Appeal for Ontario, in his article, "It is a quality control measure to maintain a standard of conduct that is acceptable, not to the subject, but to society at large".

     

    The court in Khill opined that in assessing the modified objective standard, reasonableness is not to be considered through the eyes of people who are overly fearful, intoxicated, abnormally vigilant, or members of criminal subcultures. Personal prejudices or irrational fears towards an ethnic group or identifiable cultures cannot inform an objectively reasonable perception of a threat as racist beliefs are antithetical to equality and cannot ground a belief held on reasonable grounds. The court also clarified that an honest but mistaken belief can be reasonable and is not a bar to self-defence: paragraph 57.

     

    In conclusion, the Court stated that reasonableness is a matter of judgment. To brand a belief as unreasonable in the context of a self-defence claim is to declare the accused's act as criminally blameworthy: see paragraph 58.

     

    In this case, it is noteworthy that the complainant, Ms. Moscoe, did not assault or threaten to assault Mr. Elkhodary. If there had been any physical touching by the complainant, it was purely, this Court would find, for the purpose of protecting the posters and not any intentional act of touching or assault. It was accidental or coincidental touching if it took place, in the course of protecting the posters, as his hands were in the area trying to take down the posters she was trying to protect.

     

    What is important in this case is whether there is any evidence that Mr. Elkhodary had any belief on reasonable grounds that force was being used or threatened against him or another person by Ms. Moscoe. There is no evidence of any threat by Ms. Moscoe. The court finds that there was no intentional application of force by Ms. Moscoe, prior to her being pushed by Mr. Elkhodary.

     

    The court finds that Mr. Elkhodary did not have any belief on reasonable grounds that force was being used or threatened against him or anyone else by Ms. Moscoe. The court does not have a reasonable doubt in that regard. Mr. Elkhodary cannot take advantage of self-defence, based on the Court's consideration of the criteria in Section 34(1)(a). There is no need for the court to consider further aspects of self-defence as it does not apply in this case.

     

    The Crown has proven beyond a reasonable doubt that self-defence did not apply in this case and that Mr. Elkhodary assaulted Ms. Moscoe by pushing her. The Crown has met its onus of proving guilt beyond a reasonable doubt that Mr. Elkhodary assaulted Ms. Moscoe by pushing her and he is accordingly found guilty of the charge.
