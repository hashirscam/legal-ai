    JUSTICE OF THE PEACE S. THOMAS:

    INTRODUCTION

    [1]         This case arises from a tragic collision on July 26, 2019, on Highway 407 near the Kinsale Road overpass in the city of Pickering in the Region of Durham Ontario. The collision involved a white Dodge Caliber driven by Madison Sprayson Eastbound. This vehicle also contained Johnathan Walker – Clements in the front passenger seat and Riley Wamboldt in the rear driver side seat.

    [2]         The collision occurred at approximately 8:13 p.m. under clear weather conditions. Witness accounts and evidence indicated light to moderate traffic at the time.

    [3]         The Driver of the Caliber was travelling at a high rate of speed and lost control of the vehicle and crossed the median into westbound lanes.

    [4]         While crossing the median the vehicle overturned several times before colliding with a Grey Honda Accord occupied by Mr. and Mrs. Gruben, traveling in the Westbound lanes of the 407 Highway.

    [5]         The collision resulted in the death of Riley Nicholas Wamboldt, a 21-year-old rear-seat passenger in the Dodge Caliber, and significant injuries to Mr. and Mrs. Gruben the occupants of the westbound Honda Accord.

    [6]         On January 14th 2025 Madison Sprayson was found guilty of One (1) Charges of Careless Driving Causing Death under section 130(3) of the Ontario Highway Traffic Act (HTA)

    [7]         Two (2) Charges of Careless Driving Causing Bodily Harm under section 130(3) of the Ontario Highway Traffic Act (HTA)

    [8]         Mr. Riley Wamboldt was in the rear seat of Ms. Madison Sprayson’s Dodge motor vehicle at the time of the collision. Mr. Riley Wamboldt sustained significant blunt force trauma from the collision. Mr. Riley Wamboldt died from the blunt force trauma caused by this collision.

    [9]         The collision involved a second motor vehicle – a Honda. The Honda was being driven by Mr. Keith Gruben. His wife, Ms. Mary-Lou Gruben, was a passenger in the Honda motor vehicle at the time of the collision as well.

    [10]      Mr. Keith Gruben suffered injuries from this collision. These injuries include but are not limited to: broken ribs and a collapsed lung.

    [11]      Ms. Mary-Lou Gruben suffered injuries from this collision. These injuries include, but are not limited to: broken ribs, cervical/vertebrae fractures, 1 lumbar fracture, a broken arm, a broken ankle, a laceration to her pelvic region, PTSD and anxiety. Her cervical fracture required her to wear a neck brace for 3 months 24 hours a day. She was in hospital for 3 months and when she returned home was confined to a wheelchair and hospital bed for several more months.

    (1)         R v. Biggar, 2024 ONCJ 23

    (2)         R v. Henry, 2023 ONCJ 273

    (3)         R v. Ingram, 2023 ONCJ 141

    THE CROWN’S SUBMISSIONS

    [13]      The Crown submits that this is a case that calls for a strong and unequivocal message from this Court a message of denunciation, specific and general deterrence, and, above all, public protection. Seeking a sentence of:

    (1)         18 months imprisonment;

    (2)         A 5-year driving prohibition;

    (3)         24 months of probation upon release; and

    (4)         An order for 250 hours of community service

    [14]      These sanctions are necessary, proportional, and just. This was not a case of momentary inattention or an isolated lapse in judgment. The driving behavior was prolonged, deliberate, and egregious. This accused made a series of high-risk choices: speeding, overtaking multiple vehicles, and continuing to drive recklessly both before and after the incident

    [15]      In the week prior, she was charged with Careless Driving. Post-offence, she was again caught speeding. That shows a pattern, not a one-time mistake. She did not just fail to learn she refused to change.

    [16]      Above and beyond the death of Riley WAMBOLDT there are severely injured victims in this matter, and their suffering is substantial and ongoing.

    [17]      These victims’ pain cannot and should not be overshadowed by the tragic loss of Riley. Each person’s injuries deserve full recognition.

    [18]      We’ve also heard four powerful victim impact statements from the family: Taylor Wambolt, Amber Sloan, Laura Chapman, and Barry Wamboldt. Their grief is profound and permanent. In their words, this tragedy is a "continual sentence" for them

    [19]      What’s deeply troubling here is the offender’s continued denial and lack of accountability.

    [20]      The Pre-Sentence Report paints a concerning picture of narcissistic trait; a victim mentality, and minimization of her own conduct.

    [21]      She said, “I would never have done anything wrong,” and emphasized her fear of losing her licence over the harm she caused.

    [22]      She failed to acknowledge even her basic driving record — a record that clearly shows multiple infractions. She claimed to be a "good driver," even after reviewing her own history. This is someone who, even now, does not accept responsibility. She distances herself from the truth. She has shown no remorse. That makes specific deterrence essential.

    [23]      Unlike other offenders who pled guilty and accepted blame, this individual remains in denial. Ingram, Henry, and Biggar all reinforce the need for a sentence that protects the public from repeat conduct and sends a clear message to those who drive with such disregard for human life.

    [24]      Ms Sprayson is young and there is time ahead of her. But rehabilitation is not the priority in this case. As the Court has heard this is not apples-to-apples with other cases. This offender's mindset sets her apart. Until there is a reckoning with what she did, rehabilitation cannot begin.

    [25]      While incarceration and a driving ban are critical, the Crown is also asking for 250 hours of community service. This follows Biggar’s emphasis on giving back to the community. It allows the offender to begin repaying the harm she has caused in a tangible way.

    [26]      This sentence must reflect the seriousness of what happened, the pain of those affected, and the ongoing risk this offender poses.

    [27]      There is no precedent for a case with this combination of facts. One person deceased. Two other seriously injured victims, a continuing pattern of driving offenses, and an accused who denies it all.

    [28]      The Crown characterizes the driving as prolonged, dangerous conduct involving overtaking vehicles at high speed. It is submitted that this is not a case of momentary distraction, but of calculated risk-taking resulting in foreseeable tragedy.

    [29]      The Crown highlights the accused’s extensive driving record: prior speeding offences, including violations both before and after the collision. Multiple licence suspensions suggest, in the Crown’s view, a pattern of recidivism and lack of respect for road safety.

    [30]      The Crown respectfully submits that 18 months in custody, a 5-year driving prohibition, 24 months’ probation, and 250 hours of community service are the necessary consequences here.

    THE DEFENCE’S SUBMISSIONS

    [32]      Counsel for the Defence submits that the appropriate disposition for Ms. Madison Sprayson, convicted of careless driving causing death and bodily harm under the Highway Traffic Act, is a non-custodial sentence, proportionate to her moral blameworthiness and the regulatory nature of the offence.

    [33]      The Defence respectfully proposes:

    (1)         A fine in the amount of $5,000;

    (2)         A term of two (2) years of probation, with standard conditions;

    (3)         No licence suspension, given her unique personal and professional obligations; or, in the alternative,

    (4)         A custodial sentence not exceeding 10 to 15 days, served intermittently.

    [34]      At the time of the offence, Ms. Sprayson was 21 years old, and she has remained in legal limbo throughout her twenties.

    [35]      She is a single mother to her daughter, Betty. She does however evenly split her parenting duties with the Betty’s father. Their parenting arrangement allows Ms Sprayson to have custody of Betty on a rotating basis and allows Betty to attend her school which is in the town her father lives in.  The imposition of a custodial term or driving suspension would significantly impair her ability to meet Betty’s needs and maintain stability in their lives.

    [36]      Ms. Sprayson is the sole proprietor of a rural animal rehabilitation and rehoming operation. Her business facilitates the care of approximately 500 animals per year, including the use of specialized medical devices for large animals. The business is travel-dependent and not replaceable within her region.

    [37]      She has built this business independently from a young age, beginning with a tack store at 15, followed by a horse barn, and eventually a full animal rehabilitation facility. She is one of the few providers of such services in Northumberland County.

    [38]      The Defence submits that the loss of her driving privileges or a prolonged period of custody would result in the collapse of her business, inability to provide for her child, and likely death or suffering of animals under her care.

    [39]      Ms. Sprayson sustained significant psychological harm from the incident, having lost a close friend, Riley Wamboldt. She continues to mourn the loss, has recurring nightmares, and was not permitted to attend Riley’s funeral, a fact that continues to affect her.

    [40]      She honours Riley’s memory annually, which reflects a form of remorse and emotional processing, albeit not conventionally articulated.

    [41]      Ms. Sprayson has no prior criminal record. While she has previous driving infractions, the Defence notes that many infractions were minor speeding tickets (under 20 km/h over). She has incurred no new driving infractions for over two years.

    [42]      Past licence suspensions were administrative in nature and linked to late fine payments, not to unsafe driving. She paid all fines associated with past offences, demonstrating responsibility and compliance. The Defence submits that these factors weigh against a need for specific deterrence.

    [43]      The Defence emphasizes that careless driving causing death is a regulatory offence, not a criminal one, and is governed by the sentencing principles applicable to strict liability offences.

    [44]      The Court is urged to follow the sentencing guidance set out in:

    •                     R v Miller, 2019 ONCJ 760, at paras 56–63, confirming the primary objectives in HTA sentencing are deterrence, rehabilitation, and proportionality, not punishment;

    •                     R v Defreitas, 2022 ONCJ 313, para 30, indicating that custodial sentences are not mandatory in such matters;

    •                     R v Stupar, 2021 ONCJ 219, paras 39–41, affirming that the fault element for careless driving lies in momentary lapses, not intentional or grossly negligent conduct.

    The principle of parity must also be respected. Comparable cases include.

    •                     R v Miller: 45-day sentence for texting while driving and causing death.

    •                     R v Bertram (2023 ONCJ 236): 14 days’ custody for striking a cyclist.

    •                     R v Ingram: 75 days for crossing a yellow line and causing death with moral blameworthiness far higher than in the present case.

    •                     R v Hiebert, R v Bannister, and others: fines and probation, not custody, imposed for comparable offences.

    [45]      These authorities collectively suggest that sentences for HTA careless driving causing death or bodily harm are measured in days, and custodial terms are rare for first-time offenders with no criminal history.

    Response to Crown Submissions

    [46]      The Defence acknowledges the tragic consequences of the collision. However, it is submitted that the Crown’s reliance on post-incident PSR observations must be viewed with caution. The accused’s emotional affect, while guarded, is consistent with trauma and grief and not, per se, a lack of remorse.

    [47]      There is no evidence of alcohol, substance use, or intentional harm, and while the driving involved speed and passing, there is no indication of prolonged, egregious recklessness akin to the conduct in Ingram or similar cases.

    [48]      The Crown's suggested 18-month custodial sentence is unprecedented in the jurisprudence for HTA matters, particularly given the regulatory nature of the offence, and would constitute a disproportionate response contrary to section 718.1 of the Criminal Code of Canada.

    [49]      Considering the foregoing, the Defence submits that a just, fair, and proportionate sentence would be:

    [50]      A fine of $5,000,

    [51]      Two (2) years’ probation,

    [52]      No driving suspension,

    [53]      Or, alternatively, if the Court deems custody necessary, a short custodial term not exceeding 10–15 days, potentially to be served intermittently.

    [54]      Such a sentence would reflect the principles of denunciation, specific deterrence, rehabilitation, and proportionality, while avoiding the undue collateral consequences that would flow from a more severe sentence.

    THE LAW

    Highway Traffic Act Section 130 Careless driving causing bodily harm or death.

    Penalty

    (4) On conviction under subsection (3), a person is liable to a fine of not less than $2,000 and not more than $50,000 or to imprisonment for a term of not more than two years, or to both, and in addition his or her driver’s licence or permit may be suspended for a period of not more than five years. 2017, c. 26, Sched. 4, s. 17.

    Provincial Offences Act - Probation order

    72 (1) Where a defendant is convicted of an offence in a proceeding commenced by information, the court may, having regard to the age, character and background of the defendant, the nature of the offence and the circumstances surrounding its commission,

    (a) suspend the passing of sentence and direct that the defendant comply with the conditions prescribed in a probation order;

    (b) in addition to fining the defendant or sentencing the defendant to imprisonment, whether in default of payment of a fine or otherwise, direct that the defendant comply with the conditions prescribed in a probation order; or

    (c) where it imposes a sentence of imprisonment on the defendant, whether in default of payment of a fine or otherwise, that does not exceed ninety days, order that the sentence be served intermittently at such times as are specified in the order and direct that the defendant, at all times when he or she is not in confinement pursuant to such order, comply with the conditions prescribed in a probation order.

    Statutory conditions of order

    (2) A probation order shall be deemed to contain the conditions that,

    (a) the defendant not commit the same or any related or similar offence, or any offence under a statute of Canada or Ontario or any other province of Canada that is punishable by imprisonment;

    (b) the defendant appear before the court as and when required; and

    (c) the defendant notify the court of any change in the defendant’s address.

    Conditions imposed by court

    (3) In addition to the conditions set out in subsection (2), the court may prescribe as a condition in a probation order,

    (a) that the defendant satisfy any compensation or restitution that is required or authorized by an Act;

    (b) with the consent of the defendant and where the conviction is of an offence that is punishable by imprisonment, that the defendant perform a community service as set out in the order;

    (c) where the conviction is of an offence punishable by imprisonment, such other conditions relating to the circumstances of the offence and of the defendant that contributed to the commission of the offence as the court considers appropriate to prevent similar unlawful conduct or to contribute to the rehabilitation of the defendant; or

    (d) where considered necessary for the purpose of implementing the conditions of the probation order, that the defendant report to a responsible person designated by the court and, in addition, where the circumstances warrant it, that the defendant be under the supervision of the person to whom he or she is required to report.

They painted a picture of a vibrant individual whose absence now leaves an unfillable void in the lives of those who knew and loved him.

    [57]      Among those who shared their suffering were Keith and Mary-Lou Gruben, the occupants of the second vehicle involved in the collision.     [58]      No words uttered in this courtroom can undo the pain that has been inflicted or restore what has been taken. The courage it took to confront this pain and speak these truths before the court is not lost. On behalf of this institution, I extend heartfelt thanks to each person who bravely voiced their anguish, ensuring that Riley’s life, and the lasting impact of his loss, are fully honoured within these proceedings.

    ANALYSIS

    [73]      When considering the appropriate and just sentence in this matter the court has considered at length the submissions of both counsel. It is clear that the predominant principals being argued are those of parity, restraint, specific and general deterrence and also denunciation.

    Parity

    [74]      The principle of parity mandates that similar offenders who commit similar offences under similar circumstances should receive similar sentences. Parity promotes consistency and fairness across the justice system, fostering public confidence in its administration. While parity requires comparative consistency, it does not necessitate identical sentences; individual factors relating to the offender, or the offence may justify departures.

    [75]      The parity principle does not require that the sentences given to persons who commit the same crime be the same – the parity principle requires that the differences be understandable. R. v. Choquette 2010 ONCA 327 (CanLII), [2010] O.J. No. 1851 (C.A.)

    [76]      the Supreme Court of Canada in R. v. Lacasse, 2015 SCC 64 at paras. 57 and 58 (a sentencing involving criminal driving offences):

    [57]      Tariffs differ from sentencing ranges in that tariff‑based sentencing is theoretically the opposite of sentence individualization, which the ranges allow: Thomas, at p. 8. On the other hand, the principle underlying the two approaches is the same: ensuring that offenders who have committed similar crimes in similar circumstances are given similar sentences. The same is true of the starting‑point approach, which is used mainly in Alberta but sometimes also in other Canadian provinces: R. v. McDonnell, 1997 CanLII 389 (SCC), [1997] 1 S.C.R. 948, at para. 69. Ultimately, whatever mechanism or terminology is used, the principle on which it is based remains the same. Where sentencing ranges are concerned, although they are used mainly to ensure the parity of sentences, they reflect all the principles and objectives of sentencing. Sentencing ranges are nothing more than summaries of the minimum and maximum sentences imposed in the past, which serve in any given case as guides for the application of all the relevant principles and objectives. However, they should not be considered “averages”, let alone straitjackets, but should instead be seen as historical portraits for the use of sentencing judges, who must still exercise their discretion in each case:

      Even when an appellate court has established a range, it may be that a fact pattern will arise, which is sufficiently dissimilar to past decisions that the “range”, as it were, must be expanded. The fundamental point is that a “range” is not a straitjacket to the exercise of discretion of a sentencing judge.

    (R. v. Keepness, 2010 SKCA 69, 359 Sask. R. 34, at para. 24)

    [58]   There will always be situations that call for a sentence outside a particular range: although ensuring parity in sentencing is in itself a desirable objective, the fact that each crime is committed in unique circumstances by an offender with a unique profile cannot be disregarded. The determination of a just and appropriate sentence is a highly individualized exercise that goes beyond a purely mathematical calculation. It involves a variety of factors that are difficult to define with precision. This is why it may happen that a sentence that, on its face, falls outside a particular range, and that may never have been imposed in the past for a similar crime, is not demonstrably unfit.

    [77]      The fact pattern in this case has numerous differences from many of the cases presented to the court by both crown and defence.  The lack of acceptance of responsibility at every turn of the process sets this matter apart from each of the cases presented. Even though Ms. Sprayson’s driving may not appear to be as egregious as some the continued lack of acknowledgement and shifting of blame to the front passenger is an aggravating factor in this case.

    [78]      Despite a finding of guilt Ms. Sprayson continues to blame the passenger in the vehicle for the collision.  Despite her extreme driving record she continues to state she is a good driver.  Despite two prior convictions for careless driving she continues to state she does not put people at risk. One of these careless driving convictions stemmed from an incident on July 12th 2019.   This accident that took the life of Riley Wamboldt and forever changed the lives Mr. and Mrs. Gruben  on July 26th 2019.

    [79]      I am careful in these circumstances when examining the perceived lack of remorse of Ms. Sprayson. The crown touched on this during submission. As outlined in R. v. J.S. 2018 ONCA 675, [2018] O.J. No. 4095). Lack of remorse may be relevant on sentencing regarding the offender’s lack of insight or risk of re-offending. However, lack of remorse is never an aggravating factor on sentence, however it could be acknowledged as an absence of a mitigating factor.

    [80]       In most of the cases filed by both Crown and Defence the expression of remorse and a related guilty plea factored as a mitigating circumstance. Neither of those exist in this case.

    [81]      When examination of  parity is conducted very recent cases can be examined for guidance. , the Court of Appeal very recently in R. v. Perry, 2025 ONCA 241, referring to Lacasse, found that there was no fixed range when it came to length of driving prohibition that could be imposed under the Criminal Code. In Perry, a lifetime prohibition was imposed; offender tried to point to other cases to suggest that aspect of the penalty was outside the normal range:

    [21]      I am not persuaded that these cases establish a fixed range for driving prohibitions or render the sentence unfit. The applicable caselaw reveals a wide range in the length of custodial sentences and driving prohibitions imposed in similar cases. These ranges should be treated as guidelines, and not “hard and fast rules”: Lacasse, at para. 60. This shows the individualized nature of sentencing for each offender and the degree of discretion accorded to the sentencing judge.

    Further in the recent sentencing decision where the defendant was found guilty of Careless Driving Cause Death, R v. Williams-Cordeiro 2025 ONCJ 120, Her Worship examines many of the same cases submitted by counsel. At para 46,47,49 she states the following.

    [46]      This case calls out for a sentence that reflects in particular the principle of general deterrence.  All drivers of motor vehicles need to understand the consequences of their driving behaviour on other persons using the roadway.  They must understand that using roadways in a manner that is inconsiderate and indeed, fool hardy, careless and with out regard for others is unacceptable.  Street racing behaviour cannot be tolerated on our roads.

    [47]      The case law where non – custodial dispositions have resulted are distinguishable from the matter at hand in particular in the most significant aspect, the racing behaviour.  This is particularly aggravating.  Given the aggravating circumstances of this case, and the need for general and specific deterrence, denunciation and despite some mitigating factors regarding Mr. Williams-Cordeiro himself, I have determined that a non-custodial sentence even with a significant period of probation, is not appropriate.

    [49]      Given the facts of the matter, and the public recognition of the need to denounce this type of driving behaviour, I view is that an appropriate sentence is one of 9 months in custody.  Probation for 18 months thereafter with the statutory terms: that you attend court when and as required, that you not commit the same or similar offence that can result in imprisonment and that you notify the court of any change in your address.  During the period of probation you are not to operate a motor vehicle. Your driver’s licence will be suspended for a period of four years beginning today.

    Specific Deterrence

    [82]      Specific deterrence seeks to prevent an individual offender from reoffending. Sentences aimed at achieving this objective are intended to impress upon the offender the personal consequences of their actions, thereby dissuading future misconduct. In crafting such sentences, courts evaluate the offender’s risk of reoffending by considering factors such as personal background, expressions of remorse, and prospects for rehabilitation.

    [83]      In the present case, specific deterrence emerges as a central sentencing consideration. Ms. Sprayson’s driving record is particularly troubling for someone of her age. Although defence counsel has attempted to minimize the seriousness of her record by noting that many of the speeding infractions involved speeds less than 20 kilometres over the limit, the Court does not accept this argument.

    [84]      The Court is particularly concerned by Ms. Sprayson’s convictions for speeding, including two that occurred after the incident in question. Additionally, two licence suspensions following the offence raise further alarm. Of even greater concern are the two prior convictions for careless driving, particularly one which occurred just days before the fatal and life-altering collision at issue. These facts reflect a pattern of behaviour that cannot be overlooked.

    [85]       Ms. Sprayson’s continued denial and distortion of key facts surrounding this incident further underscores the necessity of specific deterrence. Notably, misleading information was presented to both the Court and the probation officer who prepared the pre-sentence report (PSR). It was repeatedly suggested that Ms. Sprayson had been denied the opportunity to grieve her best friend or to say goodbye—an assertion made during trial, in the PSR interview, and during defence submissions.

    [86]      However, it was not until the Crown’s reply to the defence submissions that the Court learned the Wambolt family had, in fact, understood the closeness of the relationship between Riley and Madison. Arrangements had been made for Ms. Sprayson to attend the funeral home in private before the public visitation, with the funeral director's involvement corroborating these efforts. This misrepresentation constitutes yet another example of the distortion of facts in this case, thereby reinforcing the imperative for specific deterrence.

    General Deterrence

    [87]      General deterrence seeks to discourage the public from engaging in similar conduct by signaling that such behaviour will be met with meaningful sanctions. It is particularly important in cases where the offence threatens public safety, public order, or public confidence in the legal system. Sentences focused on general deterrence tend to emphasize the gravity of the offence and the necessity of conveying a strong societal message of condemnation.

    [88]      Both deterrence principles must be balanced with other sentencing objectives, including denunciation, rehabilitation, and the promotion of a sense of responsibility in offenders. Ultimately, the sentence imposed must be proportionate to the gravity of the offence and the degree of responsibility of the offender.

    [89]      Deterrence is the paramount factor in sentencing under public welfare statutes. (R. v. Cotton Felts Ltd. (1982), 1982 CanLII 3695 (ON CA), 2 C.C.C. (3d) 287 (Ont. C.A.).  Punishment must be felt by the Defendant (specific deterrence) while also serving as a warning to others who might be contemplating similar misconduct (general deterrence).  Moreover, a just sentence must be proportionate to the gravity of the offence and responsibility of offender.  In crafting an appropriate sentence, the court must consider the circumstances of the offence and of the offender to assess which factors tend to justify a more lenient sentence and which tend to elevate its severity.

    [90]      In addressing the issue of sentencing, it is necessary to acknowledge the fundamental distinction between regulatory and criminal offences. Regulatory offences, such as those in the Highway Traffic Act are characterized as public welfare offences, do not carry the same degree of moral blameworthiness traditionally associated with criminal conduct.

    [91]      They are enacted to ensure adherence to regulatory schemes intended to safeguard public health, safety, and welfare. The principal objectives of sentencing in the regulatory context are the promotion of compliance, the deterrence of future infractions, and the protection of the public interest. Sentences typically involve the imposition of monetary fines, periods of probation, remedial measures, and, in rare and exceptional circumstances, custodial dispositions. Imprisonment is reserved for the most serious regulatory breaches, particularly where aggravating factors or repeated non-compliance are present.

    [92]      I turn to R. v. Henry para 22-27 to illustrate the forementioned points.

    22  Deterrence as it relates to offences under the Highway Traffic Act reflects the need to both specifically and generally discourage persons from driving behaviors that negatively impact the safety of those using the roadways, as either pedestrians, passengers or drivers. The Highway Traffic Act exists to ensure that the roadways can be used safely by all.

     23   The Highway Traffic Act was amended in 2018 to include s. 130(3) careless causing death and/or bodily harm and associated sentencing provisions of s. 130(4).  These were intended to send a message that driving carelessly could result in significant penalties should the consequences cause death or bodily harm.

     24    In the first session of legislative debate in Hansard (Legislative Assembly of Ontario- Hansard- June 9, 2016, 41st Parliament, 1st Session) with respect to Bill 213- An Act to Amend the Highway Traffic Act:

     Ultimately, the goal is to have penalties in place that discourage careless driving and encourage good choice behind the wheel. But we will also use this opportunity to help promote public awareness of the threat posed by motor vehicles in the hands of the wrong person.

     With Bill 213, both our officers and courts will have the opportunity to send that very clear signal that driving with due care and attention matters.

     25   The principle of proportionality means that the sentence should be proportionate to the gravity of the offence and the degree of responsibility of the offender. The 2018 amendments of the Highway Traffic Act to include escalated penalties for driving without due care and attention reflect the seriousness and often catastrophic consequences of such driving conduct.

     26   It is important to remind myself that it is the conduct of  driving without due care and attention that is the focus of sentencing. In R. v. Grosse, 2017 ONCJ 228:

     However, it is not the consequences that is the focus of the sentence rather, it is the driving conduct itself, lack of due care and attention, along with other mitigating and aggravating factors being taken into consideration (at para 7).

     27   Finally, the principle of restraint guides all sentencing in both Criminal Code and regulatory statutes. I am alive to the need for restraint and that the sentence imposed is not to be more than is required in the particular circumstances having regard to the relevant factors.

    [93]      The court recognizes that sentencing in regulatory matters involving a fatality can be difficult for the public to fully understand. When a life is lost—permanently and irrevocably—the grief experienced by loved ones is immeasurable. In such cases, the public rightly expects accountability commensurate with the gravity of the harm. Yet, this court is bound to reach its decision not on emotion, but through a principled and lawful application of sentencing principles.

    [94]      The court offers its deepest and most heartfelt condolences to the family and loved ones of the deceased, as well as to all those whose lives have been permanently altered by this tragic event. The pain of losing a loved one under such sudden and preventable circumstances is immeasurable. Your grief is compounded by the reality that no sentence, no matter how severe, can ever restore what has been taken or rebuild the future that will never come to pass.

    [95]      This process has undoubtedly been long and emotionally exhausting. The court acknowledges the courage it has taken for you to endure each stage of these proceedings—to sit through evidence, to relive trauma, and to wait for justice under the weight of overwhelming loss. Your dignity, resilience, and unwavering presence in court are a testament to the profound love you had for the person you lost and to your strength as a community of mourners.

    [96]      The court is fully aware that no judicial outcome can truly bring closure to such a devastating loss. The sentencing delivered today does not, and cannot, compensate for a life cut short, nor can it erase the void that remains. But it is the court’s sincere hope that the resolution of this matter offers at least a measure of recognition, accountability, and the beginnings of peace as you continue the long and difficult journey of grief and healing.

    [97]      To each of you affected by this tragedy: your loss is deeply felt, and your voices have been heard. The court honours the memory of the life lost and stands in solemn respect for your suffering.

    [98]      In arriving at an appropriate sentence, the court has considered the magnitude of harm caused, the clear negligence and disregard for legal obligations displayed, the context in which the offence occurred, the offender’s recent and troubling driving history, the enduring and devastating impact on the victim’s family, and the extent to which the offender has taken responsibility or expressed genuine remorse.

    [99]      After careful review of the statutory provisions, the applicable jurisprudence, the aggravating and mitigating factors, and the submissions of both Crown and Defence counsel, the court finds that the sentence imposed must reflect the seriousness of this offence, the permanent loss suffered, the offender’s moral culpability, and the public interest in both specific and general deterrence.

    SENTENCE

    [100]   The sentencing submissions presented to the court have spanned a wide range, reflecting divergent views on culpability and consequence. Having fully considered all relevant factors, the court determines that the following sentence is just and necessary.

    [101]   Please stand. The Prosecution has proposed a sentence of 18 months' incarceration, while Defence has requested a fine of $5,000 or, alternatively, a short custodial sentence of 10–15 days. Given your recent driving suspensions due to unpaid fines and your demonstrated irresponsibility leading to a fatal outcome, this court finds that a fine is entirely inappropriate. Fines are a sanction of leniency, and this is not a case that warrants leniency. Accordingly, you are sentenced to 12 months of custody. This reflexes the median sentence under the amended legislation.

    [102]   On the matter of driving prohibition: while the Defence has requested no suspension and the Crown has sought a 5-year prohibition, the court must prioritize public safety. Your history and conduct demonstrate a disregard for legal and civic obligations. Accordingly, your driver’s licence shall be suspended for a period of 4 years.

    [103]   Both parties have agreed to a probationary term of 24 months following your release. You are hereby placed on probation for a period of two years. The following statutory conditions will apply:

    (a) You shall not commit the same or any similar offence, nor any offence under a statute of Canada, Ontario, or any other province punishable by imprisonment;

    (b) You shall appear before the court when required;

    (c) You shall notify the court promptly of any change to your address.

    [104]   The Crown has sought an order requiring you to perform 250 hours of community service. The Defence has made no submissions on this point. Pursuant to section 72 (3) (b) of the Provincial Offences Act, community service may only be imposed with the defendant’s consent, which has not been provided. The court is therefore not able to make such an order at this time.

    [105]   There has been no expressed consent given in this matter and the court is not able to request such consent nor to I think it necessary or appropriate to request it at this point in proceedings. This is an issue that should have been addresses during submissions for sentence. Accordingly, no community service condition shall be imposed.

    [106]   I will however, pursuant to Section 72 (3) (d) ordered you to report to a probation officer and to remain under that officer’s supervision throughout the duration of your probation order, or until otherwise directed by your supervising officer.

    [107]   Ms. Sprayson, I have previously addressed the victims and their families. Currently I wish to recognize that today marks a significant and painful moment in your life as well. While the consequences of your actions have been devastating, the court does not lose sight of the fact that rehabilitation and accountability are not mutually exclusive. The sentence imposed is not only a reflection of the harm caused, but also an opportunity an obligation for you to reflect, to take responsibility, and to make amends in the ways that remain possible.

    [108]   It is hoped that the time ahead, both in custody and under supervision, will not be wasted, but used purposefully: to understand the full weight of what has occurred, to confront your role in it with honesty, and to begin rebuilding a future grounded in greater care for others, for the law, and for the responsibilities we all carry when we act in society.

    [109]   There is always the capacity for change for growth, for contrition, and for redemption. But that path is yours to walk, and it begins with acknowledgment and continues through sustained effort. The court encourages you to take this sentence not just as a punishment, but as a solemn chance to commit to a future defined not by this tragedy, but by your actions moving forward to honour the life that was lost and to rebuild trust in those around you.

    [110]   You are being held accountable today. What you do with this accountability, and how you carry its weight, will define the legacy of this chapter.
