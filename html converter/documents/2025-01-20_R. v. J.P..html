    Introduction

    [1]         The accused is charged with:

    a)   Sexually Assaulting KJ on July 18, 2023;

    b)   Sexually Interfering with KJ on July 18, 2023;

    c)   Administering a Noxious Substance, Xanax, to KJ on July 18, 2023.

    [2]         The Crown elected to proceed by Indictment.

    [3]         The parties advised at the outset that the following issues were admitted:

    a)   Date;

    b)   Time;

    c)   Jurisdiction;

    d)   Identity;

    e)   The fact that JP and KJ had sexual intercourse on July 18, 2023;

    f)     The fact that KJ was 14 years of age on July 18, 2023;

    g)   The fact that JP was 31 years of age on July 18, 2023.

    [4]         The issues in this case are:

    a)   Whether the sexual intercourse was consensual;

    b)   If the sexual intercourse was not consensual, whether JP had an honest but mistaken belief in communicated consent;

    c)   If the sexual intercourse was consensual, whether KJ’s consent was vitiated by her intoxication;

    d)   If the sexual intercourse was consensual, or if I have a reasonable doubt that KJ did not consent, whether JP took all reasonable steps to ascertain KJ’s age before proceeding to have sexual intercourse with her;

    e)   Whether Xanax is a noxious substance;

    f)     If Xanax is a noxious substance, whether JP administered it to KJ. 

    [5]         These issues require an assessment of the reliability and credibility of the witnesses.

    [6]         On December 9, 2024, I orally advised the parties that, for reasons to be delivered later, I was finding JP guilty of Sexual Assault and Sexual Interference, and finding him not guilty of Administering a Noxious Substance.

    [7]         These are those reasons.

    Analysis

    The Presumption of Innocence and the Burden of Proof

    [318]   Many, who are ill-informed about how our criminal justice system works, believe that it is the responsibility of a Judge to listen to the evidence and decide who they believe.  In their world, if a Judge believes the accused, the accused is not guilty. If, however, the Judge believes the complainant, the accused is guilty. 

    [319]   That is not how it works. 

    [320]   Because the liberty of the accused is always at stake, woven through every stage of the analysis that judges like me conduct every day are safeguards to prevent wrongful conviction. 

    [321]   One of those safeguards is the time-honoured principle that an accused person is always presumed innocent until the state proves his guilt beyond a reasonable doubt. 

    [322]   A second safeguard is the principle that only when the Crown proves that the accused committed the elements of the offence charged beyond a reasonable doubt, is the presumption of innocence overcome.

    [323]   A reasonable doubt is not an imaginary, far-fetched or frivolous doubt.  It is not a doubt based on sympathy for or prejudice against anyone.  It is a doubt that is based on reason and common sense.  It is a doubt that logically arises from the evidence or the absence of evidence.

    [324]   To be satisfied beyond a reasonable doubt, I have to be sure with respect to the accused’s guilt.  If I am not sure, I must find the accused not guilty.  

    [325]   If I don’t know who to believe, I must find the accused not guilty.  Even if I believe that the accused probably committed the offence, that is not a sufficient basis on which to find the accused guilty.  Probable guilt is not proof of guilt beyond a reasonable doubt.

    [326]   The appellate authorities, particularly R. v. W.(D.),  1991 CanLII 93 (SCC), set out the path I must follow in considering whether the Crown has established guilt beyond a reasonable doubt: 

    a)   First, where the accused testifies and I believe him, I must find him not guilty. 

    b)   Second, where the accused testifies and even though I do not believe him, I am left with a reasonable doubt by his evidence, I must find him not guilty.

    c)   Third, even if I do not believe the accused or am not left in doubt by his evidence,  if I am left with a reasonable doubt on the basis of the other evidence I do accept, I must also find the accused not guilty.

    Only if, at the end of this exercise, I am satisfied as to the guilt of the accused beyond a reasonable doubt, must I find him guilty.  

    [327]   I cannot compare the accounts and decide guilt on the basis of which account I like better.

    [328]   In following this path, I rely on my experience, reason and common sense.  I must not, however, engage in stereotypical or mythological reasoning which masquerades as “common sense”.

    The Assessment of the Reliability and Credibility of Witnesses

    [329]   Evidence must be both credible and reliable. 

    [330]   A witness who is credible, is not necessarily reliable.  A witness may be sincere and credible, but their evidence may also demonstrate that their recollections are not reliable. 

    [331]   One way of determining whether a witness is credible and reliable is to gauge their evidence in Court against what they said on previous occasions, such as when they gave a statement to the police. 

    [332]   Not every inconsistency, however, gives rise to the conclusion that a witness is unreliable.  Some inconsistencies are important; others less so.  Where an inconsistency involves something material about which an honest witness is unlikely to be mistaken, the inconsistency may demonstrate a carelessness with the truth which is a cause for concern. 

    [333]   The same issue can arise where a witness “fills in the blanks” with respect to what took place.  A witness may honestly and sincerely believe that a certain aspect of their evidence is true; analysis on the basis of logic, reason and common sense might suggest however, that what the witness says – as honest, sincere and credible as the witness may seem – is not reliable. 

    [334]   Memories fade.  They can also be faulty and frail.  Sincere, honest and credible witnesses can simply be wrong or make assumptions about things because of the passage of time. 

     

     

    The Assessment of Evidence of Persons Who are Not Adults

    [335]   Three of the witnesses in this case, KJ, NL and LP were under 18 when the incident took place and when they testified.  Additionally, KJ is autistic.

    [336]   .  In R. v. W.R. (1992), 1992 CanLII 56 (SCC), 74 CCC (3d) 134 at page 144, Justice McLachlin (as she then was) stated:

    It is neither desirable nor possible to state hard and fast rules as to when a witness's evidence should be assessed by reference to "adult" or "child" standards -- to do so would be to create anew stereotypes potentially as rigid and unjust as those which the recent developments in the law's approach to children's evidence have been designed to dispel. Every person giving testimony in court, of whatever age, is an individual, whose credibility and evidence must be assessed by reference to criteria appropriate to her mental development, understanding and ability to communicate.     The Law With Respect to the Admissibility of Prior Consistent Statements

    [338]   The Crown led the following prior consistent statements:

    a)   The text message between KJ and CC the day after the incident (Exhibit 1);

    b)   Oral evidence from NL with respect to the substance of text messages she received from KJ on the night of the incident;

    c)   Oral evidence from LP with respect to the substance of text messages she received from KJ on the night of the incident.

    [339]   Prior consistent statements are generally not admissible. 

    [340]   One exception to this rule arises where the defence is alleging recent fabrication:  R. v. Ellard 2009 SCC 27.  

    [341]   “Recent fabrication” arises when there is an allegation that a witness has, between the date of the incident complained of and the date that she testifies in trial, lied about the allegations:  R. v. Stirling 2008 SCC 10 at paragraph 5. 

    [342]   The word “recent” in the concept is something of a misnomer.  The fabrication need not be particularly “recent”:  R. v. Stirling, supra, citing with approval R. v. O’Connor 1995 CanLII 255 (Ont. C.A.). 

    [343]   In order to rebut an allegation of recent fabrication, the Crown is entitled to present evidence of the prior consistent statements of the witness.  This is an exception to the general exclusionary rule with respect to prior consistent statements.  The messages are not, however, admissible for the truth of their contents.

    [344]   A second exception to the rule against the admissibility of prior consistent statements relates to narrative. 

    [345]   A prior consistent statement can assist a trial judge in understanding how and when the allegations came to be disclosed and reported.  More generally, it can also assist in assessing the credibility and reliability of the complainant or a witness: R. v. C.(G.) 2006 CanLII 18984 (ON CA), 2006 OJ 2245 (C.A.); R. v. Dinardo 2008 SCC 24; R. v. J.A.T. 2012 ONCA 177; R. v. Khan 2017 ONCA 114; R. v. N.W. 2018 ONSC 774; R. v. Camara 2021 ONCA 79; R. v. Ellis 2022 ONCA 535. 

    [346]   As with the rule respecting recent fabrication, it cannot be admitted for the truth of its contents. 

    [347]   Given that the defence has raised recent fabrication, and given the attack on her credibility, subject to my comments below about the weight that they can be given, the prior consistent statements of KJ are admissible. 

    The Law of Sexual Assault Generally

    [348]   In order to find JP guilty of sexual assault, I must be satisfied beyond a reasonable doubt that:

    a)   JP intentionally applied force to KJ.

    b)   KJ did not consent to the force that JP applied.

    c)   JP knew that KJ did not consent to the force that he applied.

    d)   The force that JP applied took place in circumstances of a sexual nature. 

    [349]   Appellate courts have given trial judges the following instructions in assessing the presence or absence of consent:

    a)   If the sexual assault case involves a different interpretation of essentially the same events, trial judges should consider whether the complainant, in her mind, wanted the sexual touching to occur.  Once she has asserted that she did not consent, the question is one of credibility.  If the trial judge is satisfied beyond a reasonable doubt that she did not consent, the inquiry shifts to the accused’s state of mind:  R. v. Ewanchuk 1999 CanLII 711 at para. 61 (SCC).  

    b)   The accused may then establish that on the basis of her words and conduct, the complainant was consenting:  Ewanchuk, supra, at para. 63.

    c)   The accused’s belief that the complainant was consenting cannot be reckless or willfully blind:  Ewanchuk, supra, at para. 65.

    d)   Silence, passivity or ambiguous conduct is not consent:  Ewanchuk, supra, at para. 51.

    e)   While it is open for a complainant to change her mind, the accused must make certain that she has truly changed her mind before proceeding with further intimacies.  The accused cannot rely on the mere lapse of time or the complainant’s silence or equivocal conduct to indicate that there is a change of heart, nor can he engage in further sexual touching to “test the waters”.  Continuing sexual contact after someone has said “no” is, at a minimum, reckless conduct which is not excusable:  Ewanchuk, supra, at para. 52.

    f)     The complainant must agree to the specific sex act.  Agreement to one form of penetration is not agreement to all forms of penetration.  Agreement to sexual touching on one part of the body is not agreement to all sexual touching:  R. v. Hutchinson 2014 SCC 19 at para. 54.  Consent must be specifically renewed and communicated for each sexual act.  It is not possible to give “broad advance consent”:  R. v. Barton 2019 SCC 33 at paragraph 118.

    g)   Section 273.1 of the Criminal Code requires that the accused must take reasonable steps, in circumstances known to him at the time, to ascertain that the complainant was consenting.  The reasonable steps requirement rejects the outmoded idea that women can be taken to be consenting unless they say “no”.  There is no catalogue of steps that constitute “reasonable steps”.  Silence, passivity or ambiguous conduct is not a reasonable step.  The more invasive the sexual activity and/or the greater the risk posed to the health and safety of those involved, the greater care is necessary in ascertaining consent.  Greater care is also necessary where the parties are unfamiliar with one another and there is the risk of miscommunication, misunderstanding and mistake:  Barton, supra, at paras. 105 to 108.

    h)   Not only does no mean no, but only yes means yes.  Nothing less than positive affirmation is required to establish consent:  R. v. Goldfinch, 2019 SCC 38 at para. 44.

    [350]   The Supreme Court of Canada’s decision in R. v. Hutchinson 2014 SCC 19 makes it clear that where a trial judge finds that the complainant consented, or has a reasonable doubt about the lack of voluntary agreement to the sexual activity, the next step is to determine whether, pursuant to section 265(3) and 273.1, the consent has been vitiated. 

    [351]   Section 273.1 of the Criminal Code states:

    (1)      Consent must be present at the time that the sexual activity in question takes place.

    (2)      For the purposes of subsection (1) no consent is obtained if

    (a)      the complainant is unconscious;

    (b)      the complainant is incapable of consenting to the activity for any reason other than the one referred to in paragraph (a.1);

    (c)        The accused induces the complainant to engage in the activity by abusing a position of trust, power or authority;

    (d)       The complainant expresses, by words or conduct, a lack of agreement to engage in the activity; or

    (e)      The complainant, having consented to engage in sexual activity, expresses, by words or conduct, lack of agreement to continue to engage in the activity.

    Disregarding Myths and Stereotypes

    [352]   For almost 25 years, trial judges like me have been instructed by the Supreme Court that “there is no inviolable rule on how people who are victims of trauma like sexual assault will behave”:  R. v. D.D. 2000 SCC 43 at para. 65. 

    [353]   A complainant’s “failure” to dress modestly is not indicative of consent to sex:  R. v. Kruk 2024 SCC 7 at para. 41. 

    [354]   A complainant’s failure to leave immediately is not indicative of consent to sex:  R. v. Lacombe 2019 ONCA 938 at paras. 43 to 45. 

    [355]   A complainant’s failure to resist or fight back is not indicative of consent to sex:  R. v. Kruk, supra.

    [356]   On the other hand, predicting the sexual behaviour of women based on the fact that at the time of the incident they were menstruating is also “perilous business”.  To do so is “too fraught with the risk of prejudicial judgment and too uncertain to yield appropriate inferences”:  R. v. E.D.J. 2024 ONCA 48 at paragraph 27.

    The Law with Respect to the Capacity to Consent

    [357]   KJ voluntarily consumed alcohol and drugs (as I will discuss below, I find that she voluntarily consumed JP’s Xanax and they both consumed marihuana) before the sexual intercourse took place.  While he disagrees with the extent to which KJ was intoxicated, JP was aware of the extent to which she had consumed both alcohol and drugs. 

    [358]   KJ acknowledged consenting to JP kissing her.  She stated that she did not consent to sexual intercourse.  During the sexual intercourse, she described feeling extremely drowsy and her arms and legs were heavy.  She testified that she essentially let it happen.

    [359]   This raises the spectre of whether KJ was so intoxicated that any consent to have sex with JP was vitiated by incapacity due to intoxication. 

    [360]   A person who is drunk still has the capacity to consent:  R. v. Jensen 1996 CanLII 1237 (Ont. C.A.); R. v. R.(J.) 2006 CanLII 22658 (Ont. S.C.J.).  Alcohol or drug-induced intoxication which results in poor decision-making or loss of inhibition is also still valid consent:  R. v. Merritt [2004] O.J. 1295 (S.C.J.).  While it may be helpful to have expert evidence on this issue, it is not required:  R. v. Hernandez [1997] A.J. No. 955 (C.A.); R. v. Cedeno 2005 ONCJ 91. 

    [361]   In R. v. Al-Rawi 2018 NSCA 10, at paras. 66 to 74, Justice Beverage of the Nova Scotia Court of Appeal helpfully set out how a trial judge should go about assessing the issue of whether the complainant’s intoxication vitiated her consent:

    Therefore, a complainant lacks the requisite capacity to consent if the Crown establishes beyond a reasonable doubt that, for whatever reason, the complainant did not have an operating mind capable of:

    1.                 appreciating the nature and quality of the sexual activity; or

    2.                 knowing the identity of the person or persons wishing to engage in the sexual activity; or

    3.                 understanding she could agree or decline to engage in, or to continue, the sexual activity.

    In cases where consent and capacity to consent are live issues, the trial judge must determine if it has been established beyond a reasonable doubt that the complainant did not consent, or lacked the capacity to consent.  As detailed above, these inquiries are entirely subjective. 

    In R. v. Hutchinson, supra, the majority reasons penned by McLachlin C.J. and Cromwell J. helpfully suggest a two step approach.  First, determine if the complainant consented, or at least, is there a reasonable doubt that she did not.  If there is doubt that she did not consent, determine if her consent was vitiated:

    The Criminal Code sets out a two-step process for analyzing consent to sexual activity. The first step is to determine whether the evidence establishes that there was no “voluntary agreement of the complainant to engage in the sexual activity in question” under s. 273.1(1). If the complainant consented, or her conduct raises a reasonable doubt about the lack of consent, the second step is to consider whether there are any circumstances that may vitiate her apparent consent. Section 265(3) defines a series of conditions under which the law deems an absence of consent, notwithstanding the complainant’s ostensible consent or participation: Ewanchuk, at para. 36. Section 273.1(2) also lists conditions under which no consent is obtained. For example, no consent is obtained in circumstances of coercion (s. 265(3)(a) and (b)), fraud (s. 265(3)(c)), or abuse of trust or authority (ss. 265(3)(d) and 273.1(2)(c)).

    Difficulties present where the complainant, due to the ingestion of drugs or alcohol, truly has little or even no memory of the event.  Absent direct evidence from a complainant that subjectively she did not consent, the judge or jury frequently must rely on circumstantial evidence to determine the absence of consent (see for example:  R. v. J.W.M., [2004] O.J. No. 1295; R. v. Tariq, 2016 ONCJ 614; R. v. Cedeno, 2005 ONCJ 91; R. v. Thurairajah, 2006 CarswellOnt 9699, 2008 ONCA 91; R. v. Anderson, 2010 YKSC 32; R. v. B.S.B., 2008 BCSC 917, aff’d 2009 BCCA 520; R. v. C.P., 2017 ONCJ 277; R. v. J.M., [2003] O.J. No. 3493; R. v. J.R., 2006 CanLII 22658 (ON SC), [2006] O.J. No. 2698, aff’d, 2008 ONCA 200, leave to appeal denied, [2008] S.C.C.A. 189/231; R. v. Kontzamanis, 2011 BCCA 184; R. v. Meikle, 2011 ONSC 650; R. v. Olotu, 2016 SKCA 84, aff’d 2017 SCC 11).

    Where a complainant testifies that she has no memory of the sexual activity in question, the Crown routinely asks: “Would you have consented?”  Despite the potential to discount the typically negative response as speculation, the answer is usually received into evidence, and depending on the reasons, may or may not have a bearing on the determination if consent or capacity to consent were absent (see for example: R. v. J.R., supra; R. v. B.S.B., supra; R. v. Olutu, supra; R. v. Meikle, supra at para. 45; R. v. Tariq, supra at para. 70; R. v. Esau, supra at paras. 4 and 91; R. v. Kontzamanis, supra at para. 31).  For unknown reasons, this question was not put to the complainant.

    In any event, a trial judge is required to consider all of the evidence adduced at trial and direct his or her mind to making the necessary findings of fact or mixed law and fact presented by the issues to be decided.  In doing so, the judge must not start with any presumptions that certain types of witnesses are inherently credible or reliable, nor must the judge employ stereotypical myths or flawed assumptions.

    There is, of course, the legal presumption that any accused is innocent of the accusation that he or she faces.  This legal presumption can only be displaced by reliable and credible evidence that establishes beyond a reasonable doubt all of the essential elements of the offence or offences charged. 

    A trier of fact is not to assess each piece of evidence individually on a standard of proof beyond a reasonable doubt (R. v. Morin, 1988 CanLII 8 (SCC), [1988] 2 S.C.R. 345).  Rather, the trier of fact must take into consideration all of the circumstantial evidence relevant to any particular element. 

    When the evidence is entirely circumstantial, the judge must again consider all of the evidence.  If after considering that evidence, existence of the elements is the only reasonable or rational inference, the trier of fact should draw the inference that the elements, and hence guilt, have been established beyond a reasonable doubt (see R. v. Villaroman, supra at para. 41).  If there are other reasonable or rational explanations inconsistent with guilt, the inference must not be drawn and the accused acquitted.

    The Law with Respect to Mistake of Age

    [363]   Section 150.1(4) of the Criminal Code may have application to this case in the event that I rule that the sexual intercourse between JP and KJ was consensual.  It states:

    It is not a defence to a charge under section 151 or 152, subsection 160(3) or 173(2) or section 271, 272 or 273 that the accused believed that the complainant was 16 years of age or more at the time the offence is alleged to have been committed unless the accused took all reasonable steps to ascertain the age of the complainant.  

    [364]   Mercifully, in R. v. Hason 2024 ONCA 369 at paragraphs 33 to 51, the Court of Appeal has recently set out firm guidance on how to interpret this section, by enunciating the following principles paraphrased below:

                a)         There are two elements to the mistake of age defence provided for in          section 150.1(4):

    i)     the accused honestly believed that the complainant was at least 16 years old;

    ii)   the accused took all reasonable steps to ascertain the complainant’s age.

    b)         If the accused shows an air of reality to both elements, then the Crown must negate the air of reality by proving beyond a reasonable doubt that either element is lacking. 

    c)         In order to disprove the first element, the Crown must prove that the accused did not subjectively accept that the complainant was at least 16 years of age:

    i)     Subjective acceptance as truth requires a higher degree of certainty than suspicion or supposition. 

    ii)   For greater certainty, suspecting, guessing or hoping that the complainant is at least 16 years of age does not rise the standard of subjective acceptance of truth.  This is because inherent in suspecting, guessing or hoping is the fact that there is a risk, even a low one, that the complainant is under 16.  If the accused proceeds with sexual activity in the face of that risk, they are reckless, which is a blameworthy mental state. 

    d)         If the Crown disproves the first element, the entire mistake of age defence is eliminated.  Whether the accused took “all reasonable steps” is irrelevant because he did not subjectively believe that she was at least 16.

    e)         If the Crown has not disproved the first element, subjective acceptance of truth that the complainant is at least 16, then the Court must consider whether the accused took all reasonable steps.  This is a contextual and fact-specific exercise. 

    i)     Establishing all reasonable steps is a high bar.  The use of the word “all” favours requesting adults to take more, not fewer, steps.   This robust analysis protects young people as much as possible. 

    ii)   To establish that the accused took all reasonable steps, the accused must take all steps that a reasonable person would take in circumstances known to him to ascertain the complainant’s age. 

    iii)   Those steps would expect a reasonable person to accept as true that the complainant was at least 16.

    iv)   A reasonable person would demand compelling information that establishes the complainant’s age to a high degree of certainty before accepting as true that the complainant was at least 16 years of age. 

    v)   Even a low risk that the complainant is under 16 is recklessness and precludes belief that the complainant is of legal age.  This is because reasonable persons would want to be sure that they are not running the risk of causing the life-altering and devastating consequences that adult/youth sexual activity inflicts on underage young people, including heightened risk of physical injury, suicide, substance use, unwanted pregnancy. 

    vi)   The information establishing the complainant’s age to a high degree of certainty must be compelling.  If it is not, the accused must take additional steps.  The accused cannot take an approach to ascertaining the complainant’s age where they check a few boxes or that is focused on plausible deniability rather than reasonable belief.  Common scenarios where the accused must take additional steps to ascertain the complainant’s age are:

    1)   If the complainant tells the accused they are underage.

    2)   If the complainant does not respond to the accused inquiries of her age or gives an ambiguous response. 

    3)   If the accused obtains information that merely suggest that the complainant is at least 16, but does not establish this to a high degree of certainty.

    4)   If the accused initially obtains compelling information that the complainant is 16 or older but subsequently learns other information that suggests she is underage. 

    5)    Stereotypical and illogical reasoning is not compelling.  For example,

    (A) An offer to exchange money for sex. 

    (B) A reasonable person appreciates that someone may look 16 but not actually be 16.  A reasonable person would be wary of relying on appearance or jumping to conclusions about age based on, for example, height, weight and presence of some breast development.  Visual observations may be sufficient in some circumstances, but this is rare because it is not a reliable indicator. 

    (C) A reasonable person would be wary of relying on the mere fact that the complainant is drinking, smoking, purchasing or using drugs or sexually active.  Reasonable persons recognize that underage people engage in these activities.  Thus, they will not generally be sufficient to ground a reasonable belief. 

    (D) More reliable indicators include attendance at adult-only social events, graduation from high school, employment or the ability to drive.

    (E) If the accused cannot obtain compelling information right away (such as a situation where the complainant declines identification or cannot provide it promptly), the accused must hold off until they can obtain information that would cause a reasonable person to believe that the complainant is at least 16. 

    f)         Negating mistake of age is necessary but it is not sufficient.  The Crown must also prove that the accused actually believed that the complainant was under age, was willfully blind as to that fact or was reckless. 

    i)     Belief means that the accused accepted as true that the complainant was under age and proceeded anyway.

    ii)   Wilful blindness means that the accused suspected that the complainant was under 18 but suppressed that suspicion and failed to make further inquiries;

    iii)   Recklessness means that the accused appreciated the risk, even if the risk is a low one, that the complainant was under the age of 16 but bailed to make further inquiries.  In essence, the accused appreciated a risk, even a low one, and decided to take the risk.  This can include situations where the accused simply does not turn his mind to the complainant’s age.

    g)         In some rare circumstances, complete inattention to the complainant’s age is not reckless and the accused can be acquitted because the Crown has not proved recklessness.    

    The Law with respect to Administer Noxious Substance

    [365]   Section 245(b) of the Criminal Code makes it an offence to administer a noxious substance.  The section states:

    Every person who administers or causes to be administered to any other person or causes any other person to take poison or any other destructive or noxious thing is guilty

    b)         of an indictable offence and liable to imprisonment for a term of not more than two years or of an offence punishable on summary conviction, if they did so with intent to aggrieve or annoy that person.

    [366]   The Crown is required to prove beyond a reasonable doubt the following elements:

    a)   JP administered, caused to be administered or caused KJ to take a substance;

    b)   The substance is “poison or any other destructive or noxious thing”;

    c)   JP had intent to aggrieve or annoy KJ. 

    [367]   In this case, as I will discuss below, JP essentially says that his Xanax prescription was on the nightstand in his motel room.  He did not see KJ take any, but she could have taken it when he was not looking. 

    [368]   On the other hand, KJ stated that they sat on the bed and he poured two pills from the prescription bottle into her hand and she took it. 

    [369]   There is a distinction in the law between administering a substance to the complainant, causing the substance to be administered to the complainant or causing the complainant to take the substance and the complainant taking the substance voluntarily.  A conviction does not follow where the complainant ingests the substance voluntarily.  There must be the element of subterfuge, deceit, fear, coercion or in the case of a young or intellectually challenged complainant, complete ignorance as to the nature of the substance.

    [370]   Pouring motor oil over the face of the complainant while holding her head back is sufficient to meet the definition of administering:  R. v. Clark 2008 ABCA 271.

    [371]   Surreptitiously applying a chemical which causes rashes, burns or blisters to the complainant’s desk is sufficient to meet the definition of administering:  R. v. Carr 2010 ONCA 290. 

    [372]   Ordering the complainant to drink bleach is sufficient where the complainant is afraid of the accused:  R. v. Getachew 2013 OJ 1674.

    [373]   An uncle who coaxes an 11-year old niece to smoke cocaine but putting the cocaine in a tube, holding it with her and lighting a lighter to allow her to smoke it is sufficient to meet the definition of “administering”, even where the complainant did not feel “forced” to take it:  R. v. DY 2018 ONSC 6764.

    [374]   Surreptitiously placing urine and semen into drinks left by the complainant on a nightstand is sufficient:  R. v. Langdon 2024 ONCJ 183.

    [375]   Is Xanax a “noxious substance”?  The only evidence before me on the character of Xanax comes from the accused himself.  He testified that Xanax can cause drowsiness.  It is particularly potent if mixed with alcohol, particularly in the uninitiated.  In that case it can cause one to black out. 

    [376]   In R. v. Burkholder 1977 ALTASCAD 8 the Alberta Court of Appeal found that a substance is noxious if it is capable of resulting in the consequences described elsewhere in section 245, i.e. the aggrieving or annoying of the person to whom it is administered.  The Court noted with approval the dictionary definition of “noxious”, which included “injurious, hurtful, harmful, unwholesome” (“unwholesome” meaning “not beneficial to mortals”.). 

    [377]   In R. v. Ho-On 2023 ONSC 1010, Schreck, J. acquitted the accused of the charge of administering a noxious substance on the basis that the complainant’s evidence was not sufficiently reliable.  The judgment discusses the effects that the Xanax had on the complainant, including severe drowsiness.  Schreck, J. also found that her ingestion of Xanax is likely to blame for the problems with her memory that resulted in difficulties with her reliability.  The judgment implies that Xanax is a noxious substance. 

    [378]   I am satisfied that a drug that can cause drowsiness to the point of black out when mixed with alcohol is “noxious”.

    k)   KJ uses alcohol and marihuana. 

    3.         Assessment of the Evidence of NL

    [395]   NL was trying her best to give an honest account of her recollections. She is a friend of KJ.  I accept that she was being truthful when she said that other than telling her that she would be a witness, KJ has not spoken with her about these allegations.

    [396]   Unfortunately, I have some difficulties relying on her uncorroborated evidence for the following reasons:

    a)   She was heavily intoxicated on the evening in question to the point that her memory of what took place was “scrambled together”.

    b)   She had no recollection of the police coming to break up the party.  She testified that it essentially ended peaceably.  This cannot be true.  KJ, JP and LP all testified that the party ended because the police came and broke it up. 

    c)   She saw KJ and JP split from the group before arriving at the marina and go down Christie Street.  This cannot be true.  KJ, JP and LP all testified that KJ (and JP) ended up at the marina after the police broke up the party.

    d)   She received a number of text messages from KJ that evening through Snapchat.  Because that software does not save text messages, these messages were not put into evidence and I only have NL’s evidence with respect to the content of them.  I note that KJ did not testify about sending messages to NL. Although I find that her evidence about the Snapchat messages is admissible, it is unsafe to give it any weight given that its content is not available for review and given the other problems I have outlined with NL’s memory and intoxication. 

    [397]   I accept NL’s evidence with respect to the amount of alcohol that KJ consumed and KJ’s state of sobriety. 

    [398]   I also accept her evidence that some of the party-goers were being unkind to KJ, putting her down and calling her names.  

    4.         Assessment of the Evidence of LP

    [399]   Unlike NL, LP admitted to consuming two coolers and she was only “slightly tipsy”.

    [400]   Like NL, although LP and KJ are friends, I accept her evidence that apart from KJ advising her that she would ultimately be a witness, they have not discussed their evidence. 

    [401]   I accept her evidence except for her evidence about the Snapchat texts.  As with similar evidence offered through NL, although this evidence is admissible, I cannot give this evidence much weight because it is not available for review. 

    [402]   I accept that LP was concerned about JP because of his age.  I find that she confronted him about it at the marina.   I note that JP testified that he did speak with LP at that marina, but he could not remember the gist of the conversation.  Although LP omitted to tell the police about being “brushed off” by JP when she confronted him at the marina, I find that this is, in fact, what took place.      

    [403]   I accept that she also confronted KJ about it and KJ “blew her off”.

    5.         Assessment of the Evidence of SW

    [404]   SW is JP’s mother.  She has an obvious interest in the outcome of the proceedings.[2]

    [405]   I accept her evidence on the following points:

    a)   KJ “looked young” to her.  In cross-examination, she described her as “a girl” in bed. 

    b)   When she arrived at the motel and saw KJ, she gave JP “a look”.  In response, JP said, “What?! She’s 20.” 

    c)   That KJ seemed put out by her.  As I will relate below, I believe that KJ was starting to realise what had happened to her.  It is not surprising that she would seem “put out” by the mother of the man who she was starting to realise had taken advantage of her.   I also believe that SW was put out by KJ.  In the final analysis, I believe that SW suspected that KJ was a lot younger than what KJ or JP told her.  As I will now discuss, I also believe that SW suspected that KJ was a lot younger than 18.

    [406]   I do not accept her evidence on the following points:

    a)   That KJ looked 18.  While I accept that to a 60-year-old person, 18 is “young”, the only basis she had for concluding so was the way that KJ was dressed.  There were no other details.

    b)   That KJ looked “definitely 18”.  I do not accept this evidence.  This is a very precise number.  People who estimate the age of someone usually estimate a range of ages.  For SW, there was no range.  KJ looked 18.  Not 17.  Not 19.  18.  Having never met KJ before this incident, it is impossible for her to be so precise and I do not believe it. 

    c)   That she denied being curious as to who “the girl” was.  If she was not curious, she would not have given JP the look that resulted in his immediate exclamation that KJ was 20.   

    d)   That KJ glared at her and said, “What?!  I’m 20,” in the car on the way back to Tim Horton’s.  I find by the time that this car ride took place, KJ was starting to process what happened to her and starting to come to the conclusion that something was not right.  She asked to be dropped off at the Tim Horton’s because she did not want JP to know where she lived.  Earlier that morning, she had taken pictures of JP in the motel room – pictures that he was unaware she had taken – because she wanted proof that they were together.  I do not believe SW when she says that KJ told her that she was 20.

    6.         Findings of Fact based on the Balance of the Evidence

    [407]   I am satisfied beyond a reasonable doubt as to the following facts:

    a)   JP met “two kids” at the Dairy Queen in Pembroke.  They had alcohol.  They invited him to join them at the party.  He walked with them from the Dairy Queen to the location of the party on Eganville Road. 

    b)   KJ knew about a party on Eganville Road.  Her mother did not want her to go.  This upset KJ.  She was also upset because her mother allowed her brother’s girlfriend to stay overnight at the house. She decided to leave and go to the party anyway.

    c)   KJ changed into a black dress and black spandex shorts somewhere after she left her home but before she arrived at the party. 

    d)   KJ met JP at the party.

    e)   Everyone at the party other than JP was between 14 and 18 years of age.

    f)     Some of the people at the party were rude toward KJ, called her names or put her down.

    g)   KJ, LP and JP left the party because the police attended and shut it down.

    h)   KJ was very intoxicated at the party, on the way to the marina and at the marina.  She was stumbling and slurring her words.  She fell. 

    i)     KJ testified that someone saw her with JP and told her not to go with him.  I accept LP’s evidence that she was that person.  I accept KJ’s evidence that she rebuffed that suggestion. I accept LP’s suggestion that she told JP that he should not be walking KJ home and that someone who was “her own age” should walk KJ home.  I note that JP testified that he remembered having a conversation with LP but he did not remember what it was about.  LP also testified that JP said, “I can take you home.” 

    j)     KJ was still very intoxicated as she walked from the marina to the motel. LP testified that at the marina KJ was in no state to walk. KJ testified that she was struggling to walk.  JP admitted that he had his arm around her.  He also testified that a random person passed by, asked about KJ’s state and said that she does not look so good. 

    k)   KJ consumed some Xanax while she was at the motel.  She testified to this effect.  JP testified that he was on prescription Xanax at the time.  I will discuss below whether I am satisfied beyond a reasonable doubt that JP administered the Xanax to her.  

    l)     KJ consented to joining JP in bed. 

    m)  KJ consented to JP kissing her.  She testified to this effect.  JP testified that she consented to him touching her.

    n)   The bed that they were in was a two-person bed.  It was the only bed in the motel room.  

    o)   KJ was wearing a black dress, black shorts, black underwear and a black bra.  JP unzipped her dress.

    p)   KJ’s arms and legs started to feel heavy and she did not feel good.  I accept that this was likely a result of the alcohol, the marihuana, the Xanax or all three. 

    q)   JP proceeded to have sexual intercourse with KJ.

    r)     At some point in the evening, JP cooked a steak on the barbeque outside the room and ate it.  KJ did not have anything to eat.

    s)   At some point in the evening, while at the motel, they consumed marihuana.  KJ had a half an ounce of marihuana in her backpack.

    t)     KJ took the photos of JP.  JP did not realise she was taking his photos.

    u)   At some point in the evening, he told her that he had been in the military and had a 13-year-old son.  He also gave her his name and contact information, including social media.

    v)   At some point in the evening, KJ told JP that she was having a fight with her mother, that she was afraid to go home and that she was afraid her mother would get rid of her cat.

    w)   KJ took photos of JP sitting at a table in the motel room. 

    x)   SW came by the next morning and was suspicious as to KJ’s age.  She gave JP a funny look and he told her that KJ was 20.  KJ and SW were put out by each other.  

    y)   SW and JP drove KJ home the next morning.  They dropped her off at a Tim Horton’s near her home.

    z)   Once she arrived home, KJ went to bed.

    aa)  She got up at one point and asked CC for one of her sleeping pills which CC gave to her. 

    bb) She essentially slept around the clock until approximately 5:00 the next morning when she sent CC an email about what happened.  She figured out that she was taken advantage of.  She was scared and she did not know how CC would react. 

     

    Findings with Respect to Consent

    [408]   I find beyond a reasonable doubt that KJ did not consent to sexual intercourse with JP.  I base this conclusion largely on the following:

    a)   KJ admitted to consenting to JP touching her.

    b)   KJ admitted to going to bed with JP.

    c)   KJ stated that she did not consent to sexual intercourse.  Although there are some serious issues with her credibility and reliability, I believe her on this point for the following reasons:

    i)         KJ took photos of JP after the incident because she wanted some evidence as to who he was and the fact that they were together so he could not deny it.

    ii)         KJ texted her mother.  I have the benefit of a complete record of the text message.  The message indicated that she consented to kissing and “then we had sex”.  While not entirely contemporaneous to what took place, it buttresses her credibility on this point.

    v)            KJ’s evidence that there was really no discussion between her and JP about what happened is remarkably similar to what JP said.  There was a discussion about touching, which she consented to, and then they proceeded with intercourse . JP explained this by stating that one thing “led to another” and they ended up having sex.  That is not consensual because KJ did not consent by words or actions. 

    Findings with Respect to Whether Consent Could Have Been Vitiated by Intoxication

    [409]   Having concluded that KJ did not consent to sexual intercourse, it technically is not necessary for me to consider the balance of the arguments in this case.  In the event that these reasons are reviewed on appeal, however, I will proceed to analyse these issues. 

    [410]   There is ample evidence in this case that KJ was grossly impaired by alcohol before she arrived at JP’s motel room.  Having been with her for most of the evening, JP was very aware of how impaired she was. 

    [411]   Once there, she consumed Xanax and she may have consumed marihuana.  JP was aware that she had consumed these drugs. 

    [412]   KJ testified that she started to feel very drowsy and her arms were very heavy. 

    [413]   Despite this however, she remembered consenting to JP kissing her.  She testified to this effect in Court and she texted her mother to that effect later.  She also remembered the sexual intercourse. 

    [414]   Having regard to the legal principles outlined above, I therefore find that she was not so intoxicated that she was incapable of consenting to sexual intercourse.  Notwithstanding how intoxicated she was, she was capable of consenting.  She did in fact consent to some sexual activity with JP. 

    Findings with Respect to Honest Belief in Communicated Consent

    [415]   I have concluded beyond a reasonable doubt that KJ did not consent to sexual intercourse with JP. 

    [416]   I do not believe JP’s evidence that KJ consented, nor am I left in doubt by his evidence on this issue for the reasons I set out above when I analysed his credibility. 

    [417]   While I accept that KJ consented to kissing JP, I do not believe that JP took reasonable steps to ensure that KJ was consenting to sexual intercourse with him for the reasons I expressed above. 

    [418]   I find that JP took KJ’s consent to kissing as consent to have intercourse.  As he said when he testified, “One thing leads to another.”  Unfortunately for him, legally, one thing does not lead to another.  It was incumbent upon him to specifically ensure that KJ was consenting to sexual intercourse with him.  I find that he did not do so. 

    [419]   I find that she did not consent to sexual intercourse verbally.  Nor did she consent by conduct.  These two people met that night.  They had no familiarity with one another.  It was his responsibility to make sure that he had her consent, either through words or conduct, every step of the way.  He did not do so. 

    Findings with Respect to Mistake of Age

    [420]   Following the Hason rubric, I make the following findings:

    Step 1 – Did JP Honestly Believe that KJ was 16 or older? 

    [421]   JP said that KJ told him at the party that she was 20.  KJ denies this and says that she told him her true age.  I have found that I do not believe KJ’s evidence with respect to this.  She has lied about her age before in order to obtain credit.  She wants to hang out with older people.  She wants to drink and use marihuana which is an adult activity.  Later on, she admitted to consenting to kissing JP who, on her evidence (whether I believe it or not) she thought was 21 years of age.   

    [422]   I therefore find that the Crown has failed to disprove that JP subjectively believed that KJ was 16 or older.

    Step 2 – Did JP Take All Reasonable Steps to Ensure that KJ was 16 or older?

    [423]   Hason instructs me that this element is contextual and fact-specific.  The use of the word “all” imposes a high bar that is robust and protects young people.  JP had to demand compelling information which established KJ’s age with a high degree of certainty.  Even if JP knew that there was a low risk that KJ was underage, he is reckless and has not taken “all reasonable steps”.  “Plausible deniability” is not sufficient. 

    [424]   In this case, although the accused may have obtained compelling information from KJ that she was 20, he was in possession of other information which should have led him to realise otherwise including,

    a)   He followed “two kids” to the party.  His use of the word “kids” is most telling.  It suggests that there was reason to believe that the “kids” were not the age they told JP, assuming that they told him their ages which I do not believe happened. 

    b)   Most of the people at the party were underage.  This should have been clearly evident to him.  It was evident to everyone else I heard from who was present at the party.  As the Crown later submitted, this was a party of high-schoolers. 

    c)   He cannot rely on the fact that other people of the party were drinking or using drugs.  This is stereotypical and illogical reasoning which is impermissible because many underage people engage in this kind of activity. 

    d)   He cannot rely on KJ’s mode of dress.  This, too, is stereotypical and illogical reasoning.  It is natural for underage people to try to dress to look older than they are. 

    e)   He did not have any information that KJ graduated from high school.

    f)     He did not testify with respect to receiving any information with respect to whether KJ was employed.

    g)   He did not testify with respect to receiving any information with respect to whether KJ was licensed to drive. 

    h)   He received information from LP that someone her own age should be walking KJ home and that he was too old for her.  He disregarded this evidence. 

    i)     He received information from KJ that she was in a fight with her mother and was concerned about her pet cat.  As defence counsel pointed out, in this day and age, young persons are remaining under the parental roof longer and it is not uncommon for someone in their 20s to still be living at home, be concerned about fights with their mother or be concerned about their cats.  That’s all true.  But it is, to borrow a phrase used by the Crown, a “red flag” that was deserving of further inquiries – further inquiries which JP did not make. 

    [425]   As to the three categories of blameworthy mental states, I find that JP was reckless as to whether KJ was 16 or older.   He was in possession of other information that should have made him question her more thoroughly.  He chose to ignore the need for those inquiries and take the risk. 

    [426]   I therefore find that, if I am wrong and KJ consented to the sexual activity with JP, JP did not take all reasonable steps to ensure that KJ was of age.

     

     

    Conclusion Re Sexual Assault and Sexual Interference

    [427]   I therefore find the accused guilty on both counts.  The parties may address me on the effect of R. v. Kienapple 1974 CanLII 14 (SCC), [1975] 1 SCR 729.   

    Findings with Respect to Administer Noxious Substance

    [428]   I went back and listened to the record with respect to KJ’s evidence on this point which revealed:

    Crown:           …and when you went into the room, what did [JP] do?

    KJ:                 We sat down at the table and he grabbed a pill bottle off the bedside table and he poured two in my hand and said it was two mg of Xanax so I took them.

    Crown:           Can you describe the pill – the pills for the Court?

    KJ:                  It was like a circle, oval shape.  It was white. 

    Crown:           Was there any marking on it….

    KJ:                  (indiscernible)

    Crown:           Sorry, no marking on it….

    KJ:                  I don’t remember.

    Crown:           And what kind of a bottle did it come out of?

    KJ:                  A prescription bottle.

    Crown:           And you took two of those pills, did [JP] consume any drugs or alcohol when you were in the room?

    KJ:                  Not that I know of.

    Crown:           And after you took the pills, what did you do?

    KJ:                  Uh, sat at the table for a bit.  He got into bed and I got tired and he offered me to come and lie down with him.  So I got into bed with him….

    [429]   Although JP gave her the Xanax pills, there is no evidence that KJ’s ingestion of them was anything but voluntary.  She was old enough and experienced enough to know that one should not take someone else’s prescription medication.  She did anyway.  

    [430]   KJ was not threatened or coerced into taking them.

    [431]   I also find that KJ was not ignorant with respect to Xanax.  In Exhibit 1, her text to CC which is rife with spelling errors and text-ese, “Xanax” is spelled correctly.  She knew what it was and took it anyway.  There was no subterfuge, deceit, fear, coercion or complete ignorance.

    [432]   The accused is therefore acquitted on this count.
