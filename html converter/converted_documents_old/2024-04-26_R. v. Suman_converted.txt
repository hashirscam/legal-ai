The Applicant, Shane Suman, is charged with communicating with K.G., a person under 18 years old for the purpose of facilitating the commission of an offence; obtaining sexual services for consideration and failing to comply with release orders. The police investigation into the Applicant began when K.G.’s parents called York Regional police to report their suspicions that the complainant, who was underage, was engaged in a sexual relationship with an older man. Police seized the complainant’s phone at the invitation of her parents. [ 2 ] Several months later, the complainant provided a statement to police and consented to a search of her phone. The messages retrieved from the search of the phone ultimately led the police to identify, arrest and charge the Applicant with the above offences. [ 3 ] The Applicant submits that he had a reasonable expectation of privacy in the contents of the communications alleged to be between him and K.G., and that the police therefore required prior judicial authorization before searching K.G.’s phone to view and obtain a record of those communications. In the absence of prior judicial authorization, the search and seizure of the conversation between the Applicant and K.G. from the phone violated the Applicant’s right to be free from unreasonable search or seizure protected by s. 8 of the Charter. [ 4 ] The Applicant contends that the text communications were therefore unlawfully obtained and the information gathered by police as a result of this search must be excluded pursuant to s. 24(2) of the Charter. [ 5 ] The Applicant submits, and the Crown agrees, that absent the search of K.G.’s phone, the police would not have obtained a production order for the Uber records which led them to identify the Applicant and the condominium building he resides at. Had the police not obtained the Uber production order, they would not have had the grounds to obtain the production order from the condominium corporation which confirmed the Applicant’s unit. This information was essential in the police securing a warrant to search the Applicant’s residence. Section 8 of the Charter A. Section 8 – Standing and Reasonable Expectation of Privacy [ 53 ] Section 8 of the Charter provides that everyone has the right to be secure against unreasonable search or seizure. The purpose of s. 8 is to protect a biographical core of personal information which individuals in a free and democratic society would wish to maintain and control from dissemination to the state. In interpreting s. 8, courts seek to strike an acceptable balance between privacy and the need for a safe society ( R. v. Mills, 2019 SCC 22, at para. 38 ). [ 54 ] To claim s. 8 protection, a claimant must establish two things. First, the state conduct must amount to a “search” and/or “seizure”. A search involves state action that intrudes upon a person’s reasonable expectation of privacy. A seizure is the taking of a thing from a person by a public authority without that person’s consent ( Hunter et al. v. Southam Inc., 1984 CanLII 33 (SCC), [1984] 2 S.C.R. 145; R. v. Dyment, 1988 CanLII 10 (SCC), [1988] 2 S.C.R. 417, at para. 26 ). [ 55 ]

Second, the claimant must have a reasonable expectation of privacy in the subject matter of the search based on an assessment of the totality of the circumstances ( R. v. Marakah, 2017 SCC 59, at para. 10; R. v. Mills, 2019 SCC 22, at para. 12 ). [ 56 ] In considering the totality of the circumstances, four lines of inquiry guide the court’s analysis: a. What was the subject matter of the search? b. Did the claimant have a direct interest in the subject matter? c. Did the claimant have a subjective expectation of privacy in the subject matter? d. Was the claimant’s subjective expectation of privacy (if any) objectively reasonable? B. Subject Matter of the Search and Direct Interest [ 57 ] The subject matter of a search must be defined using a broad and functional approach. A court identifying the subject matter of a search must not do so narrowly in terms of the physical acts involved or the physical space invaded, but rather by reference to the nature of the privacy interests potentially compromised by the state action. The court should examine the connection between the police investigative technique and the privacy interest at stake as well as the nature of the precise information sought and the nature of the information that it reveals ( Marakah, at para. 15 ). [ 58 ] To determine whether s. 8 protects text messages (and functional equivalents), the subject matter of the search is the electronic conversation between the sender and the recipient(s). A participant in the electronic conversation and the author of the particular text messages have a direct interest in the subject matter ( Marakah, at paras 20-21; Mills, at paras. 14-16 ). [ 59 ] The subject matter of the search in the matter before me is therefore the text messages exchanged between K.G. and the Applicant. The Applicant has a direct interest in these messages. C. Subjective Expectation of Privacy [ 60 ] The Applicant must also have a subjective expectation of privacy in the subject matter of the alleged search for s. 8 to be engaged. This requirement is not a “high hurdle” ( Marakah, at para. 22 ). The Applicant clearly had a subjective expectation that his messages with K.G., wherein they discussed sex in exchange for money meetings, would be kept private. D. Is the subjective expectation of privacy objectively reasonable? [ 61 ] It is only when the answer to this fourth question is “yes” that a claimant will have standing to assert his s. 8 Charter right and argue that the impugned state action was unreasonable. If the answer is “no”, then a claimant has no standing to challenge the constitutionality of the state’s action ( Marakah, at paras. 11-12 ). [ 62 ] The Applicant’s subjective expectation of privacy in the subject matter of the alleged search must be objectively reasonable. Objective reasonableness is assessed in the totality of the circumstances. Several non-exhaustive factors may assist courts in determining whether it was reasonable to expect privacy in different circumstances. These include: - The place where the search occurred; - Whether the subject matter of the search was in public view; - Whether the subject matter of the search was abandoned; - Whether the information was already in possession of third parties; - Whether the claimant was present at the time of the search; - Whether the claimant had possession or control of the property or place searched; - Ownership of the property or place; - Historical use of the property or item; - The ability to regulate access, including the right to admit or exclude others from the place; and - The private nature of the subject matter; i.e. whether the subject matter of the search has the potential to reveal details of the claimant’s lifestyle or information of a biographical nature. [ 63 ] Each factor is only one of several that must be weighed. The question always comes back to what the individual, in all of the circumstances, should reasonably have expected. This is a normative question about when Canadians ought to expect privacy given the applicable considerations. Framed another way, the question is whether it is right that the state should be able to obtain this evidence without a prior judicial authorization ( Marakah, at paras. 24 & 30; R. v. Edwards, 1996 CanLII 255 (SCC), [1996] 1 S.C.R. 128; R. v. Tessling, 2004 SCC 67; Mills, at paras. 13, 20, & 59 ). [ 64 ] The Charter landscape in Canada, specifically as it relates to electronic communications, is now governed by Marakah. In Marakah, McLachlin C.J. discussed, at length, the privacy features of text messaging. She recognized, at paragraph 33, that “[i]ndividuals may even have an acute privacy interest in the fact of their electronic communications” because “[t]he medium of text messaging broadcasts a wealth of personal information capable of revealing personal and core biographical information about the participants in the conversation” (emphasis in the original). [ 65 ] Justice McLachlin emphasized that “[t]he focus is not on the actual contents of the messages the police have seized, but rather on the potential of a given electronic conversation to reveal personal or biographical information ” ( Marakah, at para. 32 ). [ 66 ] I am bound by this principle and must keep in mind that text messaging may reveal an individual’s personal and lifestyle choices, including their daily activities, things “that they would never reveal to the world at large” ( Marakah, at para. 36 ). This might include criminal activities. Communications of this type may also be enveloped in a reasonable expectation of privacy because, “[t]o be meaningful, the s. 8 analysis must be content neutra l” ( Marakah at para. 48 ) (emphasis added). [ 67 ] This approach, that the s. 8 analysis must be content neutral, has been followed for over three decades, since the decision in R. v. Wong, 1990 CanLII 56 (SCC), [1990] 3 S.C.R. 36. [ 68 ]

To be clear, Marakah does not stand for the proposition that text messages always attract s. 8 protections. Each case will depend upon its own facts and a trial judge’s assessment of the totality of the circumstances: The conclusion that a text message conversation can, in some circumstances, attract a reasonable expectation of privacy does not lead inexorably to the conclusion that an exchange of electronic messages will always attract a reasonable expectation of privacy (see

Moldaver J.’s reasons, at paras. 100 and 167-68); whether a reasonable expectation of privacy in such a conversation is present in any particular case must be assessed on those facts by the trial judge. (emphasis in the original) ( Marakah, at paras. 4-5 ). [ 69 ] It is important to highlight that the Supreme Court refers specifically to paragraphs 100 and 167-168 of Justice Moldaver’s dissent when explaining the limits of its conclusion. These paragraphs raised concerns that the proper balance between privacy and law enforcement was being placed at risk by the majority’s decision, particularly in respect of offences that target vulnerable members of society. [ 70 ] Justice Moldaver uses two examples to explain the perceived wide net cast by the majority’s conclusion. Both involve the recipient being victimized by the sender (see R. v. Amdurski, 2022 ONSC 1338, at para. 50 ). [ 71 ] The Chief Justice wrote for herself and three other justices of the Supreme Court. She responded to some of the concerns raised in the dissenting opinion of Justice Moldaver. Justice McLachlin stated that: [46] It is suggested that even if the place of the search, the private nature of the subject matter, and the control over the subject matter support the conclusion that there may be an objectively reasonable expectation of privacy in a given electronic conversation, the Court should not recognize such an expectation because of the impact this would have on law enforcement. The Crown argues, and Moldaver J. concludes, that these considerations should tip the balance against recognition. Respectfully, I disagree. [47] It is argued (see Moldaver J.’s reasons, at paras. 178-88) that if s. 8 may protect the sender’s privacy in a text message after it has been received then the police will either be required to obtain warrants in more situations or will be inclined to do so “out of an abundance of caution”, and that this may impact the ability of police to review messages sent to victims of sexual assault, sexual interference, harassment, child luring, and various other offences without judicial authorization. [48] Moldaver J. rejects any interpretation of s. 8 that would allow sexual predators or abusive partners to retain a reasonable expectation of privacy in text messages that they may send to their victims (para. 169). However, since Hunter, prior judicial authorization has been relied on to preserve our privacy rights under s. 8. In consequence, the fruits of a search cannot be used to justify an unreasonable privacy violation. To be meaningful, the s. 8 analysis must be content neutral. [49] Nor does my position lead inevitably to the conclusion that text messages sent by sexual predators to children or sent by abusive partners to their spouses will not be allowed into evidence. Three scenarios are possible. [50] On the first scenario, the victim, his or her parents, or other intelligence alerts the police to the existence of offensive or threatening text messages on a device. Assuming that s. 8 is engaged when police access text messages volunteered by a third party (see R. v. Orlandis-Habsburgo, 2017 ONCA 649, at paras.21-35 (CanLII)), a breach can be avoided if the police obtain a warrant prior to accessing the text messages. As stated in Cole, “[t]he school board was... legally entitled to inform the police of its discovery of contraband on the laptop” and “[t]his would doubtless have permitted the police to obtain a warrant to search the computer for the contraband” (para. 73). Similarly, victims of cyber abuse are legally entitled to inform the police, which will typically permit the police to obtain a warrant. The police officers will be aware that they should not look at the text messages in question prior to obtaining a warrant. On this scenario, there is no breach of s. 8 and the text messages will be received in evidence. [51]

The second scenario is where the police, for whatever reason, access an offensive or threatening text message without obtaining prior judicial authorization. On this scenario, depending on the totality of the circumstances, the accused may have a reasonable expectation of privacy in the text message and therefore have standing to argue that the text message should be excluded. Standing is merely the opportunity to argue one’s case. It does not follow that the accused’s argument will succeed, or that the search of the text message will be found to violate s. 8. While a warrantless search is presumptively unreasonable under s. 8, it is open to the Crown to establish on a balance of probabilities that the search was authorized by law, the law is reasonable, and the search was carried out in a reasonable manner: see R. v. Collins, 1987 CanLII 84 (SCC), [1987] 1 S.C.R. 265, at p. 278. [52] The third scenario arises where a reasonable expectation of privacy in the text messages and a breach of s. 8 are established under the second scenario. This does not mean that the evidence will be excluded. The Crown can argue that the evidence should be admitted under s. 24(2). [53]

My colleague Moldaver J. “foresee[s]” various other “troubling consequences for law enforcement and the administration of criminal justice” (para. 180). It is suggested that s. 8 challenges will add to the time required to try cases, and may disrupt the “balance” between the state’s interest in effective law enforcement and individuals’ expectations of privacy ( ibid.). If and when such concerns arise, it will be for courts to address them. There is nothing in the record to suggest that the justice system cannot adapt to the challenges of recognizing that some text message conversations may engage s. 8 of the Charter. Nor is it disputed that, where scrutiny of an electronic conversation is concerned, the state’s interest in effective law enforcement is outweighed by “the societal interests in protecting individual dignity, integrity and autonomy ”: Plant, at p. 293. Whatever law enforcement’s interest in enjoying unfettered access to individuals’ text messages, privacy in electronic conversations is worthy of constitutional protection. That protection should not be lightly denied (emphasis added). [ 72 ] Since Marakah, there has been very little appellate consideration of the issues that are directly before me. The Supreme Court did have an opportunity to consider Marakah in R. v. Mills, supra. In Mills, the Court discussed how the nature of the relationship between communicants is also a relevant consideration when determining the reasonable expectation of privacy in text communications. [ 73 ] Mr. Mills engaged in a sexually explicit text conversation with an undercover police officer posing as a 14-year-old girl named Leann. Mills sought to exclude screen captures of these electronic communications by claiming that his s. 8 right was infringed. To support his argument that he had a reasonable expectation of privacy in the messages, he claimed that he retained the ability to choose, selectively, with whom he shared certain communications, even when conversing with a child who is a complete stranger. Justice Brown, writing for the majority, disagreed: This presupposes that there is nothing inherently different between a relationship involving an adult and a child unknown to them, and other relationships, for the purposes of the s. 8 privacy analysis. [ 74 ]

Justice Brown concluded that, on the normative standard of expectations of privacy, adults cannot reasonably expect privacy online with children they do not know ( Mills at paras. 22-26; see also R. v. Campbell, 2022 ONCA 666 (CanLII), at paras. 52 and 63 ). [ 75 ] Deciding whether an expectation of privacy is objectively reasonable is a normative question - it is laden with value judgments ( R. v. Patrick, 2009 SCC 17 at para. 14 ). The Crown argues that it runs contrary to normative standards to find that Mr. Suman's expectation of privacy was objectively reasonable, since he was communicating with a 17-year-old for the purpose of engaging in sex for money. They were “virtual” strangers; K.G. was a minor and a victim of the Applicant’s predatory conduct. As such, the relationship, from a normative perspective, is not worthy of s. 8 protections and the Applicant therefore does not retain an expectation of privacy in the message he sent to K.G. [ 76 ] The Crown takes the position that the nature of the relationship between K.G. and the Applicant was inherently one of exploitation - and the Charter should not protect relationships of such a nature. Moreover, the Crown argues that a finding that the Applicant’s expectation of privacy was objectively reasonable would run afoul of the principle affirmed by the Supreme Court that "enhancing protection to children from becoming victims of sexual offences is vital in a free and democratic society ( Mills, at para. 23 ).” [ 77 ] The Crown relies heavily on the rationale in Mills to support its argument that the Applicant's expectation of privacy is not objectively reasonable. In Mills, police were running a sting operation to catch child predators. They created a fake profile of a 14-year-old girl and posted it online, waiting for the defendant or others to make contact. When Mills initiated an online conversation with the undercover officer and tried to lure her into sex, he thought he was speaking with a 14-year-old. [ 78 ] In Mills, the Supreme Court held that, in applying a normative standard, “adults cannot reasonably expect privacy online with children they do not know ( Mills, at para. 23 ).” For that reason, “the investigative technique did not significantly reduce the sphere of privacy enjoyed by Canadians because the technique permitted the state to know from the outset that the adult accused would be communicating with a child he did not know ( Mills, at para. 20 ).” The Court held, at paragraph 26, that: [26] …Applied to this appeal, and while I have said that many adult-child relationships are also worthy of s. 8’s protection — the relationship between

Mills and “Leann” is not one of them, if expectations of privacy are to reflect a normative (rather than a purely descriptive) standard. The conclusion may or may not apply to other types of relationships, depending on the nature of the relationship in question and the circumstances surrounding it at the time of the alleged search. [ 79 ] The Applicant argues that the YRP had nothing more than a theory of the relationship between K.G. and himself prior to searching K.G.’s phone. This is not the same situation as Mills where the police knew the nature of the relationship to be non-existent from the outset because they had created a fictitious child recipient of the offending communications. [ 80 ] The Applicant argues his relationship - and the conversations he had - with K.G. are clearly distinguishable from the situation in Mills and that the conclusion in Mills does not apply to his circumstances. There was no undercover sting operation. There was no fictitious minor. When the police became involved, K.G. and the Applicant had not only exchanged text messages, they had met and engaged in sex on two separate occasions. The messages included information that went to the Applicant’s and K.G.’s biographical core. In other words, the Applicant and K.G. were not strangers at the time the YRP searched K.G.’s phone and YRP knew this. [ 81 ] In a more recent decision of this Court, my colleague, Justice Konyer addressed some of the interrelationship between Marakah and Mills and subsequent caselaw in the case of R v. C.M., 2022 ONCJ 372, 2022 CarswellOnt 11633. C.M. is a case where an ex-step-father exchanged text messages with his ex-step-daughter through Facebook. [ 82 ] The messages were highly concerning and appeared to make out the offence of child luring. The complainant and her mother reported the messages to investigating officers. The officer asked to see the messages. The complainant and her mother showed the offending messages. The officer formed grounds to believe that an internet luring offence had been committed. With the complainant and her mother’s consent, the officer searched the phone for any additional, previously deleted messages between the complainant and the accused, C.M. [ 83 ] In C.M., the Crown argued that the accused had no standing to challenge the search of the device because, from a normative perspective, the relationship between C.M. and the complainant, was not worthy of s. 8 protection. [ 84 ] In C.M., as with the case before me, the Crown relied upon three other trial court decisions ( R. v. K.A., 2022 ONSC 1241; R. v. Amdurski, 2022 ONSC 1338; and R. v. Morgan, 2020 O.J. No. 2330 ) in support of its argument that a normative analysis of the relationship between the Applicant and K.G. should lead to the conclusion that the relationship is not worthy of s. 8 protection and the Applicant has no standing to challenge the search for and seizure of the messages he sent to K.G. [ 85 ] In each of the three other trial decisions, the defendants were charged with sexual offences against complainants who were either virtual strangers to them or who were acquainted only by virtue of an exploitative relationship. In those circumstances, each of the trial judges concluded that, on a normative analysis, society had no interest in fostering privacy in the relationships. Thus in K.A., Justice Conlan found that a brief relationship between an alleged pimp and his human trafficking victim “was not worthy” of s. 8 protection ( K.A., at para. 54 ). [ 86 ] Justice Molloy in Amdurski held that the accused did not have standing to object to the victim’s mother providing police with her 13-year-old daughter’s phone. Her Honour ruled that the facts in that case did not amount to police seizing the phone ( Amdurski, at paras. 22-23 ). [ 87 ] While highly persuasive, I am bound by Marakah and Mills. As distasteful (and unlawful) as the Applicant’s relationship with K.G. was, the claim to an expectation of privacy must remain content neutral ( Marakah, at para. 48 ). It is not the nature of the conversation between the Applicant and K.G. that determines whether his expectation of privacy from state intrusion in the conversation was objectively reasonable. [ 88 ] Further, the private nature of the information likely to be shared between two people who know one another well enough to regularly communicate online is a factor to consider. Again, this is a content neutral analysis that focusses on the potential for such conversations to reveal intimate details about oneself. “Electronic conversations, in sum, are capable of revealing a great deal of personal information. Preservation of a “zone of silence” in which personal information is free from state intrusion is the very purpose of s. 8 of the Charter ( Marakah, at para. 37 ).” This factor also favours a finding that the Applicant retains an objectively reasonable expectation of privacy in the impugned conversations. [ 89 ] The Crown points to the fact that the communications between K.G. and the Applicant were in fact unlawful and constitute the actus reus of the offences before the Court, namely communicating with a minor for the purpose of prostitution and communication for the purpose. The Crown relies on the trial decision of Justice Bawden in R. v. Patterson, 2018 ONSC 4467 where it was held that Patterson had no direct interest in text messages he sent to the victim because those texts constituted the actus reus of the offence of luring. [ 90 ] Bawden J. found that "the constitutional rights which protect our privacy have never gone so far as to permit an accused to claim privacy in respect of his own criminal offence ( Patterson, at para. 13 ).” [ 91 ] The Applicant takes issue with this finding and asserts that it is wrong in law. He points to the trial decision in R. v. Mootoo, 2022 ONSC 367 as support for this argument. Mootoo was charged with sexual offences, possession of child pornography, child luring and procuring a minor for the purpose of prostitution. Justice Davies found that: [39]

Even if some of Mr. Mootoo’s communications with H.J. were criminal in nature that does not defeat the reasonableness of his expectation of privacy in those communications. The Supreme Court of Canada has repeatedly held that the analysis of whether a reasonable expectation of privacy exists must be content neutral. The analysis turns on the privacy of the location or thing searched, not the legality or illegality of the items sought or the information obtained: Marakah, at para. 48; R. v. Spencer, 2014 SCC 43, [2014] 2 S.C.R. 212, at para. 36; R. v. M(A), 2008 SCC 19, [2008] 1 S.C.R. 569, at para. 72; R. v. Buhay, 2003 SCC 30, [2003] 1 S.C.R. 631, at para. 19; Duarte, at pp. 51-52; and R. v. Wong, 1990 CanLII 56 (SCC), [1990] 3 S.C.R. 36, at p. 50. [ 92 ]

The focus for Justice Davies was upon the privacy of the location or the thing searched and not the legality of the thing seized. Applied to his matter, the Applicant argues that I should be focused upon the thing searched – his messages with K.G. A content neutral analysis requires that I not focus upon the content of the messages themselves. The Applicant points to the Supreme Court of Canada’s decision in Wong, supra, as illustrative of the point. [ 93 ] In Wong, the police received information that people may be using a hotel room for illegal gambling. The police installed a video camera in the room without prior judicial authorization. The Supreme Court held that when assessing whether someone had a reasonable expectation of privacy, the fact they were engaged in illegal activity is irrelevant. The question is not whether someone engaged in illegal activity in a hotel room has a reasonable expectation of privacy. Rather, the question must be framed “in broad and neutral terms”: Wong, at p. 50. In Wong, the question was whether, in a free and democratic society, someone who retires to a hotel room has a reasonable expectation of privacy. [ 94 ] Similarly, the Supreme Court did not rely on the illegal nature of the communication in Mills as a basis to conclude that Mr. Mills had no expectation of privacy in his messages to the fictitious 14-year-old, Leann. The Court was interested in whether and how they knew each other, not whether their relationship involved illegal activity. [ 95 ] In Marakah, the text messages seized by the police were about trafficking firearms. The fact that the communications between Marakah and Winchester were texts related to carrying out a criminal offence did not serve to defeat Marakah’s reasonable expectation of privacy in his messages. The Court reiterated that the content of the messages seized could not justify an unreasonable violation of privacy ( Marakah, at para. 48 ). [ 96 ] In Mootoo, Justice Davies found that Mootoo and the complainant were not strangers. It was not a case comparable to Mills where the target was communicating with a fictitious child that Mills had never met: “The nature of the relationship between

Mr. Mootoo and H.J. supports a finding that he had an objectively reasonable expectation of privacy in their communications ( Moottoo, at para. 43). [ 97 ] In Campbell, supra, Justice Trotter addresses the interrelationship between Marakah and Mills, particularly as it relates to an accused's reasonable expectation of privacy: [60]

Marakah was decided only two years before Mills. It figured prominently in the reasons of both Brown J. and Karakatsanis J., neither of whom attempted to distance themselves from the earlier decision. Indeed, Brown J. described his reasons in the following way, at para. 30: The proposition that I advance is a modest one: to repeat, it is that Mills cannot establish an objectively reasonable expectation of privacy in these particular circumstances, where he conversed with a child online who was a stranger to him and, most importantly, where the police knew this when they created her” (emphasis in original). [61] Similarly, in the course of her reasons, Karakatsanis J. at para. 49 cites Marakah in support of the proposition that, “viewing a text message conversation between two other parties, without their consent, also engages s. 8 of the Charter.”
[63]

In Mills, Brown J. emphasized the importance of the relationship between the two communicants. This led him to say, at para. 54 above, that his conclusion “may or may not apply to other types of relationships, depending on the nature of the relationship in question and the circumstances surrounding it at the time of the alleged search.” [ 98 ] The Crown relies upon Campbell for the proposition that Mills carved out an exception in circumstances where the electronic communications themselves constitute a crime against the recipient - in that case, the victimization of children. [ 99 ] Counsel for the Applicant points out that the comment is obiter and not binding upon me as a trial judge. I agree, specifically in light of Justice Trotter's reasoning when looked at in its entirety. The exception carved out by the majority in Mills relates to the victimization of a fictious 14-year-old girl. [ 100 ] The majority in Mills stated, “Our s. 8 jurisprudence is predicated on police obtaining prior authorization before a potential privacy breach.” However, because the police knew the relationship between the accused and the child from the outset, there was no such potential for a privacy breach. The accused could not have had an objectively reasonable expectation that the state would not intrude in his private conversation with the child because the child was in fact the police. [ 101 ] The situation in Mills was distinguished by the majority from circumstances where police have “a mere theory about a relationship between the conversants.” In other words, the true nature and full extent of the relationship between the parties is not known to the police from the outset. In those circumstances, where the police have a theory of the relationships in question, such a theory would require a thorough examination of the conversation before the true nature of the relationship can be known. [ 102 ] The context of the relationship between the parties, and the circumstances surrounding that relationship at the time of the alleged search, are to be assessed on a case-by-case basis. [ 103 ] I do not read Campbell as setting out a bright line test that any communications between an adult and minor that constitute a crime against the minor can never attract s. 8 scrutiny. Moreover, this is consistent with the three scenarios specifically addressed by Chief Justice McLachlin in Marakah (para. 50 ). An assessment of the relationship between the parties and the circumstances surrounding the search must be undertaken. [ 104 ] The Crown points out, however, that the police had more than a theory of the relationship between the Applicant and K.G. They had been told by K.G. that she had met the accused online and their relationship was an exchange of sex for money. The two were unknown to each other prior to meeting online. They had spent two nights together and exchanged several messages over a two-week period. [ 105 ] That being the case, this was not a police sting. K.G. was not a fictitious, 14-year-old created solely for the purpose of police investigation. When the two first met online, K.G. had listed herself on the website as being either 18 or 19 years old. When he first began texting with K.G., the Applicant would have thought that she was an adult. The police did not know the full extent of the relationship between K.G. and the Applicant when they began searching her phone. Indeed, they were searching the phone to determine the full extent of the relationship. [ 106 ] At the time of the search, YRP had a theory of the relationship between the two. If the assessment of the reasonableness of an expectation of privacy is to be content neutral, without making assumptions about the Applicant’s relationship with K.G. or his knowledge of her underage status, even from a normative perspective, the Applicant’s expectation of privacy in the messages he exchanged with K.G. is deserving of s. 8 protection. [ 107 ] If a relationship that involves exploitation can be the determining factor underlying the expectation of privacy analysis, the content neutral analysis mandated by the Supreme Court would be rendered meaningless. The reason why the nature of the relationship in Mills was the deciding factor for s. 8 purposes is specifically because there was no actual relationship. The parties had never met online or in person. One of the parties was an undercover officer pretending to be a child. That is vastly different from a step-father, step-daughter relationship ( R. v. C.M. ), an adult and a child who had previously met ( R. v. Mootoo ), a sex trafficker and his victim (R. v. K.A.) and innumerable other relationship combinations. [ 108 ] The difficulty in assessing the reasonableness of s. 8 rights by reference to the nature of an actual relationship, particularly if the analysis focuses on whether that relationship is exploitive, is obvious - exploitive relationships, like all relationships, fall on a continuum. For example, a relationship between a drug dealer and their addicts is exploitive, does that mean the dealer and user's communications do not attract an expectation of privacy? Or the relationship between a bookmaker and their clients? Clearly exploitive, but not worthy of privacy protections under the Charter? There are endless examples of relationships that are exploitive that can and will undoubtedly come before the courts. Some trial judges will find they do and some will find they do not attract s. 8 privacy protections. Surely, this cannot be what the Supreme Court of Canada intended with its decisions in Marakah and Mills. It leaves a trial judge with a veritable judicial coin flip. [ 109 ] Although many may find the decision in Marakah unsatisfactory and perhaps even contrary to common sense, it nevertheless remains the law of the land. As Justice Campbell, sitting as a trial judge, found in Knelsen, the principles laid out in Marakah have direct application to scenarios such as the one before His Honour and, by extension, the situation before me. [ 110 ] I could engage in a somewhat strained analysis of the relationship between K.G. and the Applicant to find that the Applicant's expectation of privacy is not objectively reasonable. This would require me to conclude that despite the analysis being content neutral, from a normative perspective, the relationship between the parties was inherently exploitive and only existed for an unlawful purpose. All of this information became known to police after they searched K.G.’s messages with the Applicant. [ 111 ] Moreover, as a trial judge, it is unclear to me just how in-depth my normative assessment of the situation ought to be. By assessing the exploitive relationship, should I have no regard to the fact that K.G., despite being a minor, went onto a website of her own accord, without ever knowing the Applicant, and advertised her services as an adult? [ 112 ] Although undoubtedly exploitive in nature, K.G. was not "pimped" out. Although a victim, she was not sex trafficked. K.G., for reasons unknown and clearly irrelevant to this court, decided to engage in a sex-for-money transactional relationship with the Applicant. Had she been 18, in the Crown's view, would Mills have remained controlling? Does the Applicant’s expectation of privacy depend upon K.G. being a minor or adult without regard to his knowledge of this at the outset of the relationship? [ 113 ] To be clear, the fact that a grown adult is knowingly engaging in sex for money with a minor is morally repugnant. There is nothing justifiable in the Applicant’s actions. But this is not the question I am faced with. The question I am faced with, in light of the Supreme Court of Canada's jurisprudence in Marakah and Mills, absent any appellate court assessment of this scenario, is whether the Applicant’s expectation of privacy was objectively reasonable. [ 114 ] There is a simple and unambiguous way forward. It was addressed by Chief Justice McLachlin. If the police were to treat all messages exchanged between parties who are known to each other as though there is an objectively reasonable expectation of privacy, presuming they had grounds, they can apply for a warrant and search any phone they come into possession of. [ 115 ] Obtaining a warrant, and ensuring judicial oversight, removes the uncertainty involved with an assessment of the relationship between communicating parties. It removes the difficulties arising from a trial judge attempting to ascertain, post facto, whether the relationship is one society deems "worthy" of s. 8 protections. In other words, it provides judges, Crown counsel, defence counsel and, most importantly, police officers with certainty. [ 116 ] In the case before me and with all the other trial level cases discussed above, had police applied for warrants to search the devices they came into possession of, they surely would have had grounds to obtain a warrant. Had officers sought and obtained warrants after they came into possession of the devices or offending messages, there would have been no judicial resources required to assess the issue whether an accused had a reasonable expectation of privacy. Requiring a warrant in these circumstances would serve to eliminate, rather than generate uncertainty. [ 117 ] The fact that K.G. was in fact only 17 and did engage in conversations with the Applicant about sex for money makes it abundantly clear that the police had reasonable and probable grounds to believe an offence had been committed. Given K.G.’s statement to police, they undoubtedly had the requisite grounds to obtain a warrant to search K.G.’s phone. [ 118 ] In the specific circumstances of this case, I find that the Applicant had an expectation of privacy in the text communications he had with K.G. and that expectation was objectively reasonable. The Applicant has standing to assert that the YRP’s warrantless search of K.G.’s phone was a breach of his s. 8 Charter rights. E. Was there a seizure of K.G.’s phone? [ 119 ] Since Marakah and Mills, some trial courts have found that the voluntary provision of text messages from a complainant to police does not constitute a seizure within the meaning of s. 8 of the Charter ( R. v. Ilmi, 2023 ONSC 5602 at paras. 1, 3 and 6-14; R. v. C.T., 2023 ONSC 286 at para. 49; R v. Amdurski, 2022 ONSC 1338 at paras. 21- 44 ). [ 120 ] In the circumstances before me, the police seized a phone without a warrant but did not search it. They waited for several months until the owner of the phone attended the police station and provided a consent before searching it. This is not the same as the investigators' intervention in Mootoo nor is it a scenario where the police were simply "passive recipients" of the phone. [ 121 ] Indeed, during submissions, the Crown fairly conceded that K.G.'s phone was seized (and clearly searched) for the purpose of the s. 8 analysis. I agree. F. Was the search of K.G.’s phone authorized by law? [ 122 ] A search or seizure is presumptively unreasonable in the absence of prior judicial authorization. However, the Crown may establish, on a balance of probabilities, that the police conduct was reasonable in that the search was authorized by law, the law was reasonable, and the manner in which the search was carried out was reasonable. [ 123 ] The Crown argues that in the context of searches and seizures of electronic communications turned over to the police, the valid consent of one of the participants is sufficient for the search to be authorized by law. The Crown further argues that the strict requirements for valid consent that apply to accused persons do not generally apply to others ( Amdurski, at para. 81; R v. K.A., at paras. 68-74; R. v. C.T., at paras. 68-71 ). [ 124 ] C.T. involved allegations of sexual offences against the children of the defendant’s former spouse. One of the children logged on to her Facebook account and permitted the police to download her Facebook messages. The Court dismissed C.T.’s s. 8 Charter application to exclude the messages. Despite ruling that there had been no search or seizure by police, in the event that finding was an error, the Court further found that the search and seizure was reasonable because the complainant provided a valid and unequivocal consent to search her messages ( C.T., at paras. 49 and 64 ). [ 125 ] In R. v. Bridle, the police approached an independent sex worker to ask her about one of her clients who – unbeknownst to her – was under surveillance as part of an unrelated investigation. The witness referred to text communications that she had with the suspect. The police asked if they could take photographs of the text messages and she agreed. [ 126 ] Bridle’s application to exclude the text communications was dismissed. Justice de Sa concluded that the police do not require a search warrant to obtain text communications voluntarily shared by a party to that conversation. The Court reasoned that investigations would be unnecessarily impeded if complainants or cooperating witnesses could not provide their explanation of events to police by reference to their very own text communications with a suspect. In the Court’s view, it would be “ludicrous to suggest that a person could read out verbatim the text communications to police, but the police would require a warrant to take a look.” ( R. v. Bridle, 2023 ONSC 1259, at paras. 71-76 ). [ 127 ] The Applicant submits that these cases are wrongly decided. If he has a reasonable expectation of privacy in the messages between him and K.G., a warrantless search can only be rendered lawful if it is authorized by law – and a third-party consent to search is not the law in Canada. The Applicant points out that many of the cases relied upon by the Crown lose sight of the fact that Canada has no third-party consent doctrine. [ 128 ] As the Supreme Court held in R. v. Cole, 2012 SCC 53 and R. v. Reeves, 2018 SCC 56: “waiver by one rights holder does not constitute waiver for all rights holders (

Reeves, at para. 52 ).” The Applicant argues that in the circumstances of this case, police were only entitled to search the conversation on K.G.’s phone pursuant to a valid warrant. [ 129 ] The Crown stipulates that police review of a recipient’s copy of text communications, voluntarily provided to them, is significantly distinct from the search of an electronic device that an accused has a reasonable expectation of privacy in. [ 130 ] The Crown advances the argument that R. v. Cole and R. v. Reeves were cases that involved a shared computer. The jurisprudence recognizes that computers are unique and present privacy risks that differ from s. 8’s traditional objects. The Crown further argues that these devices store immense amounts of information —some of which is retained automatically unbeknownst to the user— and it can touch the biographical core of personal information. Computers are a repository for an almost limitless universe of information relating to the user ( R. v. Bykovets, 2024 SCC 6, at paras. 54-55 ). [ 131 ] In Bridle and C.T., police were given consent to take copies of text and Facebook messages respectively. This is akin to the police having been shown copies of texts between K.G. and the Applicant while at K.G.’s residence. There is no dispute that the police were entitled to review those texts. However, the YRP did not simply take copies of those texts. That was not the consent they sought from K.G. The consent form K.G. signed, includes the following: “I [K.G.]

Hereby Authorize the York Regional Police to conduct a detailed search for evidence on any and all computer(s), electronic storage media and related electronic devices … I fully understand that an officer trained and qualified in forensic technological investigations will assist with the search and that this officer is capable of viewing electronic evidence and recovering computer files including deleted files and/or password protected files. [ 132 ] Had YRP asked K.G. simply for consent to copy the text messages, the Crown’s argument would be far more compelling. That situation would be akin to what transpired in Bridle and C.T. [ 133 ] While the police only seek to rely upon the text messages that were extracted from K.G.’s phone during the forensic search, it is clear that the consent went far beyond the search for text messages. Modern smartphones, like K.G.’s iPhone 7 Plus, are handheld computers that store immense amounts of information that can touch the biographical core of personal information. Given the scope of the consent and the nature of the thing searched, I find that the warrantless search of K.G.’s phone by the YRP was not authorized by law. [ 134 ] In these circumstances, the YRP’s warrantless search of K.G.’s phone was in violation of the Applicant’s s. 8 Charter rights. What remains to be determined is whether the evidence obtained from: (i) K.G.’s phone; (ii) the Uber production order results; (iii) the condominium production order; (iv) the search warrant pertaining to the accused’s residence; and (v) the TextNow messages, ought to be excluded pursuant to s. 24(2) of the Charter. [ 135 ] Prior to considering 24(2), it is important to note what investigative fruits were derived from the warrantless search of K.G.’s phone. [ 136 ] Without the warrantless search of K.G.’s phone, YRP officers would not have obtained the Applicant’s phone number. They also would not have ascertained any information about the make, model or license plates of the two Uber rides K.G. took from her residence to the Applicant’s on November 19 and 25, 2019. [ 137 ] The Uber information was critical to identifying the Applicant as the target of the investigation. Although police were able to identify the geographic location where the alleged offence had occurred by reviewing the GPS locator data from K.G.’s phone, it was not, in and of itself, sufficient to lead police to focus on the Applicant. [ 138 ] When police attended the condominium complex initially, they only had the name of “Rico”. Condominium staff were unable to identify anyone using the name Rico. It was only once police obtained the records from Uber, via production order that set out the Uber ride details (obtained from the search of K.G.’s phone), that they discovered the Uber account was linked to an email address that included the Applicant’s last name. [ 139 ] It was this discovery that enabled them to determine, upon re-attending the condominium complex, that an individual with the Applicant’s last name resided there. This information, in turn, allowed the police to get a further production order aimed at the condominium and to ultimately obtain the search warrant that led to the Applicant’s arrest and the seizure of his electronic devices. [ 140 ] The vehicle information was the critical information in the chain that led to the Applicant. It could not have been obtained without the warrantless search of the Applicant’s electronic conversation with K.G. The search warrant permitting seizure of the Applicant’s devices would not have issued without the warrantless search of K.G.’s phone. The production order related to the TextNow communications would not have issued without the warrantless search of K.G.’s phone. Absent the warrantless search of K.G.’s phone, the police would not have come into possession of privileged solicitor and client communications. Section 24(2) of the Charter A. Should the texts seized from K.G.’s phone be excluded? [ 141 ] In R. v. Grant, 2009 SCC 32 (CanLII), [2009] 2 S.C.R. 353, the

Supreme Court of Canada set out the test for whether evidence should be admitted or excluded under s. 24(2) of the Charter. A trial judge’s role is to “assess and balance the effect of admitting the evidence on society’s confidence in the justice system” having regard to: (1) the seriousness of the

Charter -infringing state conduct; (2) the impact on the Charter -protected rights of the Applicant; and (3) society’s interest in the adjudication of the case on its merits. I must balance these factors in order to determine whether the evidence should be admitted. B. Seriousness of the Breaches [ 142 ] Police misconduct exists on a spectrum from technical or inadvertent errors committed in good faith to deliberate breaches. I must situate the police conduct here on that spectrum. In Marakah, the Supreme Court held that the search of the electronic conversation between the defendant and his co-conspirator “lacked any reasonable pretext of lawful authority” and that the conduct was “sufficiently serious to favour exclusion of the evidence (para. 66).” [ 143 ] In the matter before me, police seized K.G.’s phone knowing that privacy interests were involved. They turned their mind to K.G.’s privacy but did not consider whether the Applicant had any privacy interest. Officers testified that they were aware of caselaw that said there was a privacy interest in text messages but thought that only existed between two accused people. Officers testified that they had received some training on the issue but could not pinpoint precisely when and the full scope of that training. [ 144 ] At the time of the investigation, Marakah had been the law for two years. The Applicant submits that the YRP were, at best, indifferent to his Charter rights. The Crown submits that the police were acting in good faith when searching K.G.’s phone. They had taken possession of it with consent. They obtained a written consent from K.G., the owner of the phone, to search it. The Crown submits that the law was, and still remains unsettled on the issue of whether the Applicant has an expectation of privacy in the messages. As a result, the Crown argues that the police conduct in seizing the messages was in good faith. [ 145 ] Had the YRP’s Charter- infringing conduct been restricted simply to the search of K.G.’s phone, I would agree with the Crown. However, I must also consider the conduct of Sgt. Dearing in breaching the Applicant’s s. 8 rights with respect to his search of the Applicant’s device and seizing conversations with 10 individuals outside the scope of the search warrant. The Crown concedes this to be a s. 8 violation. [ 146 ] I must also consider the search of K.G.’s phone in the context of the further breach of the Applicant’s s. 8 right regarding the overbroad TextNow production order. The Crown concedes that the production order was overbroad and that the TextNow messages should be excluded. Implicit in that concession is the recognition that Sgt. Dearing’s conduct in reviewing the 42,000 plus files and not disclosing the overbroad return was serious and points towards exclusion on the first branch of the Grant analysis. [ 147 ] A pattern of breaches can be relevant to the seriousness of police conduct with respect to the first branch of Grant. In R. v. Fan, 2017 BCCA 99, Dickson J.A. provides guidance on how to assess the seriousness of Charter breaches: [69]

Several factors are considered when determining the seriousness of the Charter breach in question. For example, whether a breach was inadvertent or technical in nature is relevant to its seriousness, as is whether it was motivated by urgency or to prevent the loss of evidence: Caron at para. 39, citing R. v. Jacoy, [1988] 2 S.C.R. 584 at 559. One of the most important factors for consideration is the relative good or bad faith involved in the breach: Grant at paras. 74-75; R. v. Washington, 2007 BCCA 540 at para. 78. [70]

Where the police act in good faith, it may attenuate the seriousness of a Charter breach and reduce the court’s need to disassociate itself from the conduct. However, ignorance of Charter standards will not be rewarded or encouraged, and good faith will not be found where police conduct is negligent or wilfully blind. For example, while police are not expected to have an immediate understanding of judicial decisions affecting their powers, they do not act in good faith when they know or should know that their actions are unlawful. Deliberate conduct in knowing violation of established Charter standards amounts to bad faith and tends to support exclusion: Grant at para. 75; Caron at para. 41. [71] “Good faith” and “bad faith” are terms of art which connote mental states at the opposite poles of a spectrum. As Frankel J.A. stated in Caron, a finding that a police officer acted in good or bad faith is not a factual finding per se but, rather, a legal characterization of the facts: para. 33. Importantly, the absence of bad faith does not equate to good faith, nor does the absence of good faith equate to bad faith: Caron at para. 38. In R. v. Smith, 2005 BCCA 334, Ryan J.A. explained: [61] … good faith connotes an honest and reasonably held belief. If the belief is honest, but not reasonably held, it cannot be said to constitute good faith. But it does not follow that it is therefore bad faith. To constitute bad faith the actions must be knowingly or intentionally wrong. [72]

The seriousness of a Charter breach is not determined in a vacuum. On the contrary, the cumulative effect of multiple breaches is a relevant consideration. Where a breach is part of a larger pattern of abuse or disregard for Charter rights, this exacerbates its seriousness: R. v. Bohn, 2000 BCCA 239 at paras. 45, 47; R. v. Lauriente, 2010 BCCA 72 at para. 27. Accordingly, when evaluating the seriousness of a breach for s. 24(2) purposes, a court should consider the cumulative effect of multiple breaches: Spence at para. 51. [73 ] A pattern of breaches tends to support exclusion because courts need to distance themselves from the behaviour: Grant at para. 75. However, a finding of cumulative breaches will not necessarily result in the exclusion of evidence under s. 24(2): R. v. Trieu, 2010 BCCA 540 at para. 93. Each case turns on its own facts (Emphasis added). [ 148 ] The Crown initially argued that the TextNow production order was not overly broad and that it was capable of being read as limiting information to conversations between the Applicant and the two complainants’ phone numbers. The Crown argued that the ITO makes it clear that the YRP only sought this subset of messages from TextNow. [ 149 ] I agree that the ITO makes this clear. However, there is no other way to read the Production Order – the only document provided to TextNow. The use of the word “including” can only be interpreted as a clarifying term. This is precisely how TextNow read the face of the Production Order - That they were to produce all messages from the Applicant’s phone number, “including” those between the Applicant’s number and the two complainants. Regardless of the officer’s best intentions, the Production Order on its face was overbroad. As a result, the information produced by TextNow was overbroad and went far beyond what the YRP had grounds to seize. [ 150 ] Sgt. Dearing’s conduct in not disclosing the overly broad seizure of messages resulted in solicitor and client privileged communications being in police possession and accessible for review for more than three years. Had Sgt. Dearing turned his mind to the Applicant’s s. 8 rights and the nature of what he had received, there would be no concern with the seriousness of the state’s conduct. At best, the conduct of the state with respect to the TextNow messages was grossly negligent and therefore very serious. [ 151 ] The search of the Applicant’s phone raises similar concerns about police conduct with respect to Charter standards. Despite his understanding of the warrant and the consent search protocol (that was designed to safeguard solicitor and client privileged communications), when Sgt. Dearing attended the tech crimes unit to review the extraction of the Applicant’s phone for the purpose of directing which conversations were to be included in the extraction report produced to investigators, he conducted a search of the relevant phone numbers that somehow displayed the Applicant’s conversations with at least ten other individuals. [ 152 ] Despite acknowledging that he was aware that these were not conversation with either of the complainants, he directed that they be downloaded regardless. Sgt. Dearing denied that this direction contravened the search protocol. The Crown fairly conceded that Sgt. Dearing’s evidence was problematic in this regard. I agree. [ 153 ] Ultimately, in submissions, the Crown conceded that the information obtained from TextNow, including the messages between the two complainants and the Applicant, were obtained in contravention of the Applicant’s s. 8 rights and should be excluded pursuant to s. 24(2) of the Charter. That was a fair and proper concession. [ 154 ] Given the multiple subsequent breaches of s. 8, after the search of K.G.’s phone, there is no way to characterize the state’s infringing conduct as anything but serious. The production order sent to TextNow was clearly overly broad, making the seizure of the information an unlawful seizure. The manner in which Sgt. Dearing searched the information received from TextNow was a significant disregard for the Applicant’s Charter rights. Moreover, the manner in which Sgt. Dearing searched the Applicant’s devices was clearly in contravention of the consent search protocol which formed the parameters for his search. At a minimum, Sgt. Dearing’s search of the devices and treatment of the TextNow records was grossly negligent and a flagrant disregard for the Applicant’s Charter -protected rights. [ 155 ] As a result, given the multiple and cumulative breaches, the s. 8 breach in relation to the search of K.G.’s phone falls at the serious end of the spectrum and pulls toward exclusion. [ 156 ] In coming to this conclusion, I have not considered the failure of the police to file a Report to a Justice after searching K.G.’s phone. The Applicant makes the further argument that if he has a reasonable expectation of privacy in the messages sent to K.G., then the police had a s. 8 obligation to file a Report to Justice. Given the unsettled state of the law and the consent to search that had been provided by K.G., I do not view this as a separate Charter breach. C. The Impact of the Breach on the Applicant’s Charter -Protected Interests [ 157 ] The second line of inquiry deals with the seriousness of the impact of the Charter violation on the Charter -protected interests of the accused. The impact may range from that resulting from a minor technical breach to that following a profoundly intrusive violation. The more serious the impact on the accused’s constitutional rights, the more the admission of the evidence is likely to bring the administration of justice into disrepute ( R. v. Cote, 2011 SCC 46 at para. 47 ). [ 158 ] The focus here is on “the magnitude or intensity of the individual’s reasonable expectation of privacy, and on whether the search demeaned his or her dignity.” (see R. v. Cole, 2012 SCC 53 at para. 91 ). In Marakah, the Supreme Court held that the search revealed private information going to the defendant’s biological core and that the police conduct “obliterated” his reasonable expectation of privacy. “The impact on Mr. Marakah’s Charter -protected interests was not just substantial; it was total.” (Marakah, at para. 67 ). [ 159 ] Like Mr. Marakah, the Applicant before me had, at most, shared control of the conversation with another person. This serves to diminish - but not extinguish - his expectation of privacy. Nevertheless, the impact on Mr. Marakah’s Charter -protected interests was found to be considerable. Having found the Applicant to have a reasonable expectation of privacy in the messages with K.G., I am bound to find that the impact on his Charter -protected interests was considerable. This factor also pulls toward exclusion of the evidence. D. Society’s Interest in Adjudication on the Merits [ 160 ] The third Grant factor, society’s interest in adjudication on the merits, will typically pull towards inclusion of the evidence. In this case, both complainants are presumably available to testify for the Crown. However, exclusion of the text messages will necessarily lead to exclusion of the Uber production results and other records. In other words, if I exclude the texts from K.G.’s phone, this may effectively gut the Crown’s case against the Applicant. As Justice Cromwell wrote in Cote, at para. 47: The third line of inquiry is concerned with society’s interest in an adjudication on the merits. It asks whether the truth-seeking function of the criminal process would be better served by the admission or exclusion of the evidence. The reliability of the evidence and its importance to the prosecution’s case are key factors. Admitting unreliable evidence will not serve the accused’s fair trial interests nor the public’s desire to uncover the truth. On the other hand, excluding reliable evidence may undermine the truth-seeking function of the justice system and render the trial unfair from the public’s perspective. The importance of the evidence to the Crown’s case is corollary to the inquiry into reliability. Admitting evidence of questionable reliability is more likely to bring the administration of justice into disrepute where it forms the whole of the prosecution’s case, but excluding highly reliable evidence may more negatively affect the truth-seeking function of the criminal law process where the effect is to “gut” the prosecution’s case. [ 161 ] Clearly, the evidence sought to be excluded, the Applicant’s messages to K.G., are highly reliable. Exclusion of the evidence will more negatively affect the truth-seeking function of the trial process than inclusion. This factor favours admission of the text messages. Conclusion on s. 24(2) of the Charter [ 162 ] Balancing the Grant factors, I conclude that the admission of the messages from the Applicant to K.G. that were seized during the search of K.G.’s phone would bring the administration of justice into disrepute. I further find, as the Crown has conceded, that admitting the TextNow messages seized pursuant to the production order would bring the administration of justice into disrepute. The s. 8 Applications with respect to the search of K.G.’s phone, the Uber records, the condominium corporation records and ultimately, the identification of the Applicant and the TextNow messages are therefore granted and the evidence unlawfully obtained from these searches and seizure must be excluded pursuant to s. 24(2) of the Charter. Ruling on Stay of Proceedings under s. 24(1) of the Charter [ 163 ] The Applicant seeks a stay of proceedings as a result of the above breaches. In particular, the Applicant argues that the police conduct in the s. 8 violations related to the: (i) search of the complainant’s phone; (ii) search of the

Applicant’s devices; and (iii) overbreadth of the TextNow production Order, in conjunction with the violation of s. 7 of the Charter in obtaining and maintaining possession of solicitor-client privileged materials, if so abusive as to taint the administration of justice, warranting a stay. [ 164 ] The facts related to each breach have been fully set out above. The sanctity of solicitor-client privilege must be addressed. It is not simply a rule of evidence. Solicitor-client privilege has been recognized as a principle of fundamental justice that “commands a unique status within the legal system ( R. v. Lavallee, Rackel & Heintz, 2002 SCC 61, at para. 49; R. v. McClure, 2001 SCC 14 at para. 31 ).” Given this, breaching the privilege in the context of a criminal investigation engages the interests protected by s. 7 of the Charter. [ 165 ] Solicitor-client privilege must be as close to absolute as possible. The state must demonstrate that it is absolutely necessary to interfere with solicitor-client communications. Solicitor-client privilege must be closely guarded and can only be intruded upon in exceptional circumstances: [S]olicitor-client privilege must be as close to absolute as possible to ensure public confidence and retain relevance. As such, it will only yield in certain clearly defined circumstances, and does not involve a balancing of interests on a case-by-case basis ( McClure, at para. 35 ). [ 166 ] Like confidential informant privilege, solicitor-client privilege is a class privilege that is not determined on a case-by-case basis. Nor is it balanced against other interests, such as the needs of law enforcement. Indeed, the Supreme Court has described the privilege as “all but absolute in recognition of the high public interest in maintaining the confidentiality of the solicitor-client relationship” ( Ontario (Public Safety and Security) v. Criminal Lawyers’ Association, 2010 SCC 23 at para. 53 ). [ 167 ] It is only through the near-absolute protection of solicitor-client privilege that the “interests of a fair, just and efficient law enforcement process” are maintained. In light of its importance, courts must exercise “great caution before trying to circumscribe or create exceptions to that privilege.” ( Lavallee, at paras. 20, 36-37; Descôteaux et al. v. Mierzwinski, 1982 CanLII 22 (SCC), [1982] 1 S.C.R. 860 at p. 890-893; Maranda v. Richer, 2003 SCC 67, at para. 12 ) [ 168 ] Given that the privilege attaching to solicitor-client communications has been recognized as a substantive principle, the criminal law has developed protections commensurate with its importance. The importance of zealously protecting against any breach of solicitor-client privilege cannot be overstated. As the Supreme Court of Canada has made clear, as a class privilege there is a presumption in favour of non-disclosure even if that would impede the search of truth. In Maranda v. Richer, the Court explained that: The ultimate purpose of this privilege is to enable every individual to exercise his or her rights in an informed manner. The protection extends to advice given in both criminal and civil cases, without distinction. The privilege performs the social function of preserving the quality, freedom and confidentiality of information exchanged between a client and his or her lawyer in the context of a legal consultation. It enables all individuals to participate in society with the benefit of the information and advice needed in order to exercise their rights. It is closely associated with access to justice. Accordingly, regardless of the historical origin of the privilege, contemporary imperatives dictate that the same generous approach be taken which led to the recognition of this privilege as a principle of fundamental justice. [ 169 ] The Applicant and Crown both agree that there is a responsibility on the Court and all state actors to guard against unlawful or unnecessary intrusions into the solicitor-client relationship. This includes ensuring that all efforts are made to avoid unlawfully seizing or accessing solicitor-client privileged material. Where the accused establishes a breach of s. 8, this will constitute a prima facie breach of s. 7 ( Lavallee, para. 34). [ 170 ] The YRP unlawfully obtained solicitor-client privileged communications by virtue of an overbroad production order related to TextNow. The seizure was not deliberate nor anticipated – the evidence is clear that the police neither intended to seize solicitor-client communications, nor considered the possibility that the TextNow production order would produce solicitor-client communications – but the fact remains that the production order results captured the communications at issue and placed them in the hands of the YRP. [ 171 ] The material then remained in the possession of the police for over three and a half years without being disclosed to the Crown or defence, resulting in a violation of the Applicant’s ss. 7 and 8 Charter rights. It appears that even the police were not aware of the fact that they were in possession of privileged communications. I accept Sgt. Dearing’s and Det. Gaines’ evidence that neither they nor anyone else at the YRP knew of, let alone reviewed, the privileged communications. [ 172 ] While I have serious concerns with the manner in which Sgt. Dearing reviewed the results of the TextNow production order, including his non-disclosure of the overly broad return, those concerns have been fully addressed in my ruling to exclude evidence under s. 24(2) of the Charter. There can be no doubt that the YRP conduct fell far below acceptable standards and constitutes serious infringements of the Applicant’s Charter - protected rights. The question remains, does this conduct rise to a level so serious as to warrant a stay of proceedings under s. 24(1) of the Charter? [ 173 ] The Supreme Court has repeatedly held that a stay of proceedings is a drastic remedy, as it “permanently halts the prosecution of an accused. In doing so, the truth-seeking function of the trial is frustrated and the public is deprived of the opportunity to see justice done on the merits.” ( R. v. Babos, 2014 SCC 16 at para. 30; R. v. Regan, 2002 SCC 12 at para. 53 ). [ 174 ] A stay is therefore only warranted in the “clearest of cases” where the state conduct either: (1) compromises the fairness of an accused’s trial; or (2) risks undermining the integrity of the judicial process ( R. v. O’Connor, 1995 CanLII 51 (SCC), [1995] 4 S.C.R. 411 at para. 68 ). [ 175 ] In order for a court to stay the proceedings, the prejudice, whether to the fairness of the trial (the “main category”) or to the integrity of the justice system (the “residual category”), must be “manifested, perpetuated or aggravated through the conduct of the trial, or by its outcome”, and there must be no other remedy that can reasonably remove the prejudice ( Canada (Minister of Citizenship and Immigration) v. Tobiass, 1997 CanLII 322 (SCC), [1997] 3 S.C.R. 391 at para. 90 ). [ 176 ] If, after considering these two factors, the Court remains uncertain whether a stay is warranted, then it must balance the importance of “denouncing misconduct and preserving the integrity of the justice system” against society’s interest in a decision on the merits ( Babos, at para. 32 ). [ 177 ] The remedy of a stay of proceedings is a prospective remedy, in that it “aims to prevent the perpetuation of a wrong that, if left alone, will continue to trouble the parties and the community as a whole in the future” ( Canada (Minister of Citizenship and Immigration) v. Tobiass, 1997 CanLII 322 (SCC), [1997] 3 S.C.R. 391 at para. 91 ). Where the conduct is alleged to fall into the residual category, in that it strikes at the integrity of the justice system, the misconduct must be more than an isolated incident ( Tobiass ): 91

The mere fact that the state has treated an individual shabbily in the past is not enough to warrant a stay of proceedings. For a stay of proceedings to be appropriate in a case falling into the residual category, it must appear that the state misconduct is likely to continue in the future or that the carrying forward of the prosecution will offend society’s sense of justice. Ordinarily, the latter condition will not be met unless the former is as well ‑‑ society will not take umbrage at the carrying forward of a prosecution unless it is likely that some form of misconduct will continue. There may be exceptional cases in which the past misconduct is so egregious that the mere fact of going forward in the light of it will be offensive. But such cases should be relatively very rare. [ 178 ] When considering the first criterion of whether the prejudice will be manifested or perpetuated by proceeding, “the question is whether the state has engaged in conduct that is offensive to societal notions of fair play and decency, and whether proceeding with a trial in the face of that conduct would be harmful to the integrity of the justice system.” ( Babos, para. 35 ) [ 179 ] At its core, a stay of proceedings will be warranted under the residual category where to do otherwise would “leave the impression that the justice system condones conduct that offends society’s sense of fair play and decency.” In some cases, the circumstances are such that “proceeding would lend judicial condonation to the impugned conduct.” [ 180 ] While remedies for abuses under the first category of trial fairness must be directed towards restoring the fairness of the trial, the remedy under the residual category must be aimed at ensuring that the justice system adequately disassociates itself from the impugned conduct. [ 181 ] The Applicant seeks a stay of proceedings under the “residual category” in that the YRP’s conduct in this matter undermines the integrity of the justice system. The Applicant argues that stays have been granted in proceedings where: - the state has obtained solicitor-client privileged reports and attempted to utilize those reports in a prosecution ( R. v. Bruce Power Inc. 2009 ONCA 573 ). - the police reviewed four solicitor-client privileged communications in the course of the search of a lawyer’s office for accounting records ( R. v. D.P.F., [2003] N.J. No. 136 (N.L.S.C.T.D.) ); and - the police interviewed the accused’s lawyer in order to obtain evidence against him ( R. v. Rudolph, 2017 NSSC 333 and 2017 NSSC 334 ). [ 182 ] These cases are markedly different from the case before me. In those prosecutions, the state knowingly and actively breach solicitor-client privilege. Here, the YRP did not knowingly do so, rather, they were negligent in the execution of their duties. [ 183 ] I am not satisfied that the YRP’s actions, while very serious, are not an isolated incident in so far as they relate to the TextNow production order and the solicitor-client privileged material. In light of the Crown’s concession and my ruling that the evidence obtained through that production order is excluded under s. 24(2) of the Charter, I do not find that permitting the proceedings to continue would lend judicial condonation to the YRP’s conduct. That conduct has been adequately addressed in my ruling on s. 24(2) of the Charter. This is not one of the clearest of cases that requires the court to stay the proceedings. [ 184 ] As a result, the application for a stay of proceedings under s. 24(1) of the Charter is dismissed. Conclusion [ 185 ] In summary, the evidence obtained from the search of K.G.’s phone is excluded under s. 24(2) of the Charter. Absent this evidence, there would have been no grounds to obtain the Uber production order, the condominium production order, the search warrant pertaining to the Applicant’s residence or devices. As such, all evidence derived from these authorizations must be excluded pursuant to s. 24(2) of the Charter. As conceded by the Crown, the evidence obtained pursuant to the TextNow production order must also be excluded pursuant to s. 24(2) of the Charter. The Application for a stay of proceedings under s. 24(1) of the Charter is denied. Released: April 26, 2024
