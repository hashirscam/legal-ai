I found Mr. Graham guilty with reasons to follow. These are my reasons. C. Charter Issues: Analysis and

Conclusions Sections 8 and 9 of the Charter: General Legal Principles [ 28 ] Section 8 of the Charter protects individuals against unreasonable search and seizure while s. 9 of the Charter protects against arbitrary detention. In this case, those sections of the Charter are engaged by the Applicant’s arrest for mischief and possession of controlled substances, later arrest for possession of controlled substances for the purposes of trafficking, and his search incident to arrest. [ 29 ] If the officers had reasonable grounds to believe that the Applicant committed the offence of mischief, then the arrest was lawful. If not, the arrest and resulting detention violated section 9 of the Charter. If the arrest was unlawful, the police had no authority to search incident to arrest, and any search incident to arrest, including a strip search, would not be authorized by law, and would be in violation of s. 8 of the Charter. [ 30 ] The Applicant has the onus of establishing a breach under s. 9 of the Charter on a balance of probabilities. The Meaning of Reasonable Grounds [ 31 ] Sections 495(1) (a) and (b) of the Criminal Code give police officers the power to arrest anyone they find committing a criminal offence, or that they have reasonable grounds to believe has committed or is about to commit an indictable offence. [3] [ 32 ] The applicable framework for a warrantless arrest was set out in Storrey. A warrantless arrest requires both subjective and objective grounds. The arresting officer must subjectively have reasonable and probable grounds for the arrest, and those grounds must be objectively reasonable. The objective assessment is based on the totality of the circumstances known to the officer at the time of the arrest, including the dynamics of the situation, as seen from the perspective of a reasonable person with comparable knowledge, training, and experience as the arresting officer. [ 33 ] The police are not required to have a prima facie case for conviction before making the arrest. [4] The standard of reasonable grounds to believe does not require proof on a balance of probabilities, much less proof beyond a reasonable doubt. The statutory and constitutional standard is one of credibly based probability. Reasonable grounds is more than suspicion, but less than proof beyond a reasonable doubt, or even a prima facie case. [5] [ 34 ] If a police officer who effects an arrest lacks the required reasonable grounds for the arrest, the arrest will be unlawful and will be an arbitrary arrest, contrary to s. 9 of the Charter. [6] [ 35 ]

In applying the Storrey framework, I am satisfied Mr. Graham’s arrest was lawful. The police had an abundance of grounds to arrest Mr. Graham for mischief. I am satisfied PC Sanderson, PC Taylor, PC Farrow, and Sgt. Milne subjectively formed reasonable grounds to believe Mr. Graham committed mischief in respect of Ms. Arnold’s property and their belief was objectively reasonable. [ 36 ] I accept and find as a fact: • PC Sanderson, PC Taylor, Sgt. Milne, and PC Farrow were dispatched to a 911 call at approximately 5:08 a.m. Dispatch advised the officers that Shevonne Arnold reported that her ex-boyfriend, Rayan Graham, was in her apartment at an address on Jane Street and he was throwing her belongings off the balcony. The officers were advised that Ms. Arnold was calling from the lobby and that Mr. Graham was in her apartment. Dispatch advised the officers that Mr. Graham was cautioned as being violent, armed, and dangerous and may be in possession of a firearm. • When PC Sanderson and PC Farrow arrived at the address, at approximately 5:15 a.m., they located Ms. Arnold coming out of the lobby of the building and walking towards the back of the building. PC Farrow spoke with her briefly. She said her ex-boyfriend, Rayan, was in the apartment and had been throwing her belongings off her balcony. She provided a description of Mr. Graham stating he was a male black, 6’ tall and in his 30s. • At approximately 5:15 a.m., PC Sanderson and PC Farrow followed Ms. Arnold to the back of the building in the police vehicles. As they were making their way to the back of the building, PC Taylor arrived and followed them down the ramp to the back of the apartment building to the shipping and receiving area. Sgt. Milne arrived after PC Taylor. • PC Taylor, PC Sanderson, PC Farrow, and Sgt. Milne, saw paperwork, electronics, and clothing, strewn all over the back of the building in the shipping and receiving area. These observations corroborated information provided by dispatch. Property was even hanging from the trees. The scene was documented by the ICC recording of PC Sanderson, and photographs taken by PC Farrow and marked as Exhibit 4 at trial. I accept and agree with PC Taylor’s evidence that it looked like an “extreme incident”. • As Ms. Arnold began gathering her things, PC Sanderson spoke with her, confirming her identity and gathering information about what happened. Within a short period of time, between 5:18 a.m. and 5:19 a.m, Mr. Graham exited the building “right in front of him”. • Mr. Graham was holding a bottle of Hennessy in one hand and a gift basket in another. He appeared intoxicated. • I accept PC Taylor’s evidence that Ms. Arnold and Mr. Graham began shouting at each other, and he understood from their exchange that the male was the person who had thrown her belongings from the balcony. The ICC recording captures exchanges between Mr. Graham and Ms. Arnold following his arrest. • I accept PC Sanderson’s evidence that the man who came out of the building generally fit the description of the suspect and he asked him if he was Rayan and he said he was. It was the early morning, and no one else was in the area. • At 5:19 a.m., PC Milne informed Mr. Graham he was under arrest for mischief, and PC Taylor and PC Sanderson assisted in effecting the arrest and taking physical custody of Mr. Graham and placing him in handcuffs. PC Taylor also informed Mr. Graham he was under arrest for mischief. [ 37 ] There was an abundance of grounds upon which Sgt. Milne, PC Taylor, PC Sanderson, and PC Farrow subjectively concluded there were reasonable grounds to believe that the man who came out of the building was Rayan Graham and that he committed the offence of mischief. Their conclusion was objectively reasonable. [ 38 ] Even if PC Sanderson had not asked Mr. Graham if he was Rayan, there were reasonable grounds for the officers to believe that Mr. Graham was the person responsible for the mischief. There were no other people in the area, he matched the description of the suspect, and he and the complainant interacted in a way that identified him as being the person responsible for throwing her things from the balcony. The officers arrested Mr. Graham in the presence of Ms. Arnold. She could have immediately alerted the officers if they had the wrong person. [ 39 ] Mr. Graham was not arbitrarily detained. His arrest was lawful. The officers had reasonable grounds to believe that he committed mischief. The Controlled Substances were Located as a Result of a Lawful Search Incident to Arrest [ 40 ] The search of Mr. Graham that led to the seizure of the substances was a lawful search incident to arrest. With respect to section 8 of the Charter, although the onus is generally on an accused to establish breaches of his or her Charter rights on a balance of probabilities, warrantless searches are prima facie unreasonable. As a result, the Crown has the persuasive burden of establishing that the warrantless search was authorized by law. [7] [ 41 ]

To establish that a warrantless search complies with s. 8 of the Charter the Crown must prove on a balance of probabilities that: • The search was authorized by law; • The law is reasonable; and • The search was carried out in a reasonable manner. [8] [ 42 ] The Supreme Court of Canada has long recognized the authority of the police to search incident to arrest. The power to search incident to arrest is a “pragmatic recognition of legitimate state interest and the arrested person’s reduced expectation of privacy. Those state interests include the need to secure the custody of the arrested person, protect those at the scene of the arrest and locate and secure evidence relevant to the arrested person’s guilt or innocence”. While the common law gives the police the powers necessary for the effective and safe enforcement of the law, it also limits the exercise of the power to protect privacy and individual freedom. [9] [ 43 ]

To be valid, a search incident to arrest must meet three conditions: i. The person searched must be lawfully arrested. ii. The search must be "truly incidental" to the arrest in that the search is related to the reason for the arrest and is for a valid law enforcement purpose. iii. The search is conducted reasonably. [10] [ 44 ]

The right to search incident to arrest derives from the fact of the arrest which itself requires reasonable grounds. Since the legality of the search is derived from the legality of arrest, if the arrest is invalid the search will be also. [ 45 ] The requirement that the search must be “truly incidental” to the arrest means that the police must be attempting to achieve some “valid purpose” connected to the arrest including: i. to guarantee the safety of the police, accused and the public; or prevent the prisoner’s escape; ii. to protect evidence from destruction at the hands of the accused or others; and iii. to discover evidence which can be used at the arrestee’s trial. [11] [ 46 ] The police must have one of the purposes for a valid search incident to arrest in mind when the search is conducted and the officer’s belief that this purpose will be served by the search must be a reasonable one. [ 47 ] While the police must have some reason related to the arrest for conducting the search incident to arrest at the time the search was carried out, and that reason must be objectively reasonable
[12], reasonable and probable grounds is not required. In Caslake, the Supreme Court of Canada explained as follows: To give an example, a reasonable and probable grounds standard would require a police officer to demonstrate a reasonable belief that an arrested person was armed with a particular weapon before searching the person. By contrast, under the standard that applies here, the police would be entitled to search an arrested person for a weapon if under the circumstances it seemed reasonable to check whether the person might be armed. Obviously, there is a significant difference in the two standards. The police have considerable leeway in the circumstances of an arrest which they do not have in other situations. At the same time, in keeping with the criteria in Cloutier, there must be a “valid objective” served by the search. An objective cannot be valid if it is not reasonable to pursue it in the circumstances of the arrest. [13] [ 48 ] In this case the police conducted a lawful search incident to arrest. After Mr. Graham was arrested, PC Sanderson’s In-car Camera recording system, (ICC system) captured the search incident to arrest. Almost all the officer’s interactions with Mr. Graham were captured on video by the ICC. However, only audio from within the police car is fully captured. Although some external sound is captured, most of what took place outside the police car is inaudible. [14] [ 49 ] I find as a fact that Mr. Graham was arrested almost immediately after he came out of the apartment building. I accept PC Sanderson’s and PC Taylor’s evidence that their ICC systems were not yet engaged because they did not expect to encounter Mr. Graham behind the building. They had information he was in the apartment. [ 50 ] I am satisfied that the time on the ICC is out by exactly 5 hours. The ICC recording starts at 10:19:28 a.m. I find as a fact that the actual time was 5:19:28 a.m. The ICC recording was started within a very short time of when Mr. Graham came out of the apartment building and was placed under arrest. PC Sanderson explained he activated his ICC as soon as Mr. Graham was placed under arrest and the video recording captures video 30 seconds prior to activation. [15] [ 51 ] I accept the evidence of PC Taylor, PC Sanderson, and PC Farrow and find as a fact that the officers searched Mr. Graham incident to arrest for officer and public safety reasons and to discover evidence related to the offences for which Mr. Graham was arrested. The search was truly incidental to arrest and was for a valid objective. [ 52 ] I accept the evidence of PC Taylor, PC Sanderson, and PC Farrow that dispatch advised them over the radio that Mr. Graham was cautioned as being violent, armed, and dangerous and that he may be in possession of a firearm. PC Farrow confirmed this information on CPIC. His CPIC search also indicated that Mr. Graham was known to be violent, armed, and dangerous, was known to carry a firearm, and had outstanding charges. [ 53 ] Following his arrest, Mr. Graham was going to be placed into custody in the back of a police cruiser. In these circumstances, the officers reasonably believed it was necessary for officer and public safety reasons to search Mr. Graham to locate any weapons. [ 54 ] Within moments of the arrest and commencement of the search incident to arrest, PC Sanderson located a plastic Ziploc bag and removed it from Mr. Graham’s pocket. The ICC recording shows PC Sanderson handling a Ziploc bag which appears to contain more than one bag. At approximately 54 seconds on the ICC recording, PC Sanderson turns his flashlight onto the Ziploc bag on the hood of the cruiser and the presence of the green strip on at least one of the Ziploc bags can be seen. PC Sanderson testified that the bag contained a white powdery substance he believed was cocaine. After looking at the bag with his flashlight directly in front of Mr. Graham, he took it into the cruiser and looked at the bag more closely. After doing so, at 5:20 a.m. he informed the other officers and Mr. Graham that Mr. Graham was under arrest for “possession as well”. This was picked up by the internal audio of the ICC camera. [ 55 ] PC Sanderson and the other officers had reasonable grounds to arrest Mr. Graham for possession of a controlled substance. As expressed by PC Farrow, at this point, in addition to conducting a safety search for weapons, the officers were also searching for evidence connected to the reasons for the arrest, mischief and possession of a controlled substance. It was appropriate and reasonable for the officers to search for additional controlled substances and evidence of the intended use of the controlled substances. [16] [ 56 ] After PC Sanderson located the Ziploc bag, PC Farrow located and seized several items. This is captured by the ICC recording. I find as a fact that PC Farrow located two cellphones and the victim’s keys. In Mr. Graham’s front left pocket, he located a small scale with a dragon emblem, which he thought could be used for weighing drugs. He moved to Mr. Graham’s right side and found a purse that belonged to the victim in the centre pocket of Mr. Graham’s hoodie. He also located a small spherical ball-shaped baggie containing a dark substance in the centre pocket of Mr. Graham’s sweater. He put on gloves to remove the baggie, which he reasonably believed contained fentanyl. [ 57 ] While on Mr. Graham’s right side, PC Farrow noticed Mr. Graham was wearing two pairs of pants. He went back to Mr. Graham’s left side and checked his inner pant pocket where he located two additional baggies of a white substance in clear sandwich bags that were tied shut. He believed these bags contained crack cocaine or cocaine. PC Farrow also located a bag of cannabis. PC Farrow put the bags containing what he believed was fentanyl and cocaine on the hood of the cruiser together with other items seized. This was captured by the ICC recording system. [17] [ 58 ] Mr. Graham was lawfully arrested for mischief when the search incident to arrest commenced and, moments later, was lawfully arrested for possession of a substance. The search incident to arrest was truly incidental to the arrest and was for a valid law enforcement purpose. Given the information the police received from dispatch about the nature of the alleged offence and the caution that he was armed and dangerous and may be in possession of a firearm, together with their observations at the apartment building, it was necessary and reasonable for the officers to search Mr. Graham for officer and public safety and to discover evidence. [ 59 ] The ICC recording and officer’s evidence establish that the search incident to arrest at the scene was conducted in a reasonable manner. The ICC recording shows that it lasted approximately six minutes, between 5:19 a.m. and 5:26 a.m. It took six minutes because Mr. Graham was wearing multiple layers of clothing and because on at least one occasion during the search Mr. Graham was going limp and the officers had to lift him to a standing position. During the search Mr. Graham told the officers he was in pain because of a prior injury to his arm. The officers used two sets of handcuffs to limit his discomfort. Governing Legal Principles – Strip Searches [ 60 ] Strip searches have a significant impact on the privacy, dignity, and integrity of the individual. As such, the common law authorizes strip searches incident to a lawful arrest in more limited circumstances than the general power to search incident to arrest. The police may only conduct a strip search where there are reasonable grounds justifying the strip search, in addition to reasonable and probable grounds justifying the arrest. [18] The police must have case specific reasonable grounds to believe that a strip search is necessary for the purpose of discovering weapons or objects that could be used to threaten the safety of the accused, the arresting officers, or other individuals or to secure evidence related to the reason for the arrest. [19] [ 61 ] A strip search must be conducted reasonably, in a manner that “interferes with the privacy and dignity of the person being searched as little as possible.”
[20]

In Golden, the Supreme Court of Canada provided guidelines regarding the manner of execution of strip searches to ensure that they interfere with the privacy and dignity of the person being searched as little as possible. A strip search should: • Take place in a police station except in exigent circumstances; • Be conducted in a manner that protects the health and safety of all involved; • Be authorized by an officer acting in a supervisory capacity; • Be conducted by an officer of the same gender; • The number of officers involved in the search should be no more than reasonably necessary; • Should involve the minimum force necessary; • Should be carried out in a private area so that no persons other than those engaged in the search can observe the search; • Should be carried out as quickly as possible; • The arrestee should be given the opportunity to remove their own clothing and the search should be carried out in a manner so that the detainee is not completely undressed; • Should involve a visual inspection only of the genital and anal areas without physical contact. Where the circumstances of a search require the seizure of material located in or near a body cavity (not including the mouth), the individual should be given the opportunity to remove the material himself or the advice or assistance of a trained medical professional should be sought to ensure the material can be safely removed; and • A proper record should be kept of the reasons for and the manner in which the strip search was conducted. [21] [ 62 ] Because strip searches impact the privacy, dignity, and integrity of the detainee they are prima facie unreasonable. Where the reasonableness of a strip search is challenged, it is the Crown that bears the onus of proving its legality. [22] Custodial Safety Searches [ 63 ] The common law permits strip searches of individuals entering a custodial setting which includes persons being held for a show cause hearing. A custodial strip search is animated by concerns related to the safety and well-being of the prison population. Where individuals are entering into a prison environment, there is "a greater need to ensure that they are not concealing weapons or illegal drugs on their person prior to their entry."
[23] The risk can arise from any prisoner, regardless of why they were arrested. Hence, unlike strip searches incident to arrest, custodial searches are not limited by the "purpose of the arrest."
[24] There is a distinction between strip searches incident to arrest and strip searches related to safety issues in a custodial setting. The former must be limited to searching for weapons or other evidence related to the offence, the latter is not. [25] The Strip Search was Lawful and Necessary for Mr. Graham’s Safety, Officer Safety, and Public Safety [ 64 ] I accept the evidence of PC Sanderson regarding the reason for and manner of execution of the strip search. For privacy reasons, the strip search itself was not captured on surveillance video. However, the video surveillance of the booking area, Exhibit 2, captures the events leading up to and following the strip search, the length of the strip search, and the circumstances surrounding the strip search. [ 75 ] In my view, the grounds for a strip search were compelling and clearer than those that existed in Golden, Tim, Francis, or Tonkin. Mr. Graham had multiple bags of substances hidden on his person in different locations, including substances believed to be fentanyl and cocaine. [ 76 ] In Golden, the appellant was arrested for trafficking after the police saw him engage in two transactions involving what they believed was a narcotic. A narcotic was found under the table where the appellant was arrested, and there was evidence the appellant was seen crushing a substance that looked like crack cocaine between his fingers during the arrest. The officer testified that he was aware from past experience of persons having concealed drugs in their groin area. At issue in Golden was the egregious breaches of Mr. Golden’s privacy as a result of the manner of execution. [ 77 ] In Tim, the Supreme Court of Canada found case specific reasonable grounds existed to justify a strip search when bullets fell from the accused’s pants during his arrest. [29] [ 78 ] In Francis, the Ontario Court of Appeal concluded that “the discovery of a significant quantity of drugs and a loaded handgun in the appellant’s car” created a lawful basis for the police to conduct a strip search for weapons or evidence. [30] [ 79 ] In Tonkin, the Court was satisfied that there were reasonable grounds for a strip search. The accused was arrested in his vehicle after intercepted calls alerted officers to the fact that a drug transaction would take place in a parking lot. Surveillance officers saw him meet with the target vehicle and observed a transaction take place. After the arrest, a police officer patted him down at the roadside. Nothing was found on his person. A vacuum sealed brick of cocaine and a knotted baggie of white powder was found in the front passenger footwell of his car. In respect of the manner of the execution of the search, I am satisfied on a balance of probabilities that the search was conducted reasonably: • The search was conducted at a police station after it was authorized by a senior officer. • The search was conducted in circumstances of privacy. PC Sanderson testified, and the booking video surveillance shows Mr. Graham was taken into a private room for the strip search. The only persons present during the strip search were PC Sanderson, and PC Trichilo, officers of the same gender as Mr. Graham. • There was no audio or video recording in the private room. In respect of Ms. Mirza’s submission that the strip search was not conducted in a private location because the door to the room was open, the private room was a short distance down a private hallway leading off the booking area. The door to the private room was open outwards towards the booking area. This blocked the view of anyone in the booking area looking towards the hallway and private room. In addition, access to the booking area was controlled. PC Sanderson testified that they waited to bring Mr. Graham into the booking area until another detainee completed a drug recognition examination because they did not allow two accused in the booking area at the same time. The booking video shows during the period of the search no one passed by the hallway and no other person was in the hallway leading to or near the private room. Had anyone walked past the doorway, it would have been difficult for them to see into the room because PC Trichilo was standing in the doorway. There was no evidence from Mr. Graham to support the assertion that he believed there was insufficient privacy. • The number of officers present was no more than was reasonably necessary. Only PC Sanderson went into the room with Mr. Graham. PC Trichilo stood in the doorway. I am satisfied that it was reasonable for a second officer to be present to ensure the safety of PC Sanderson and Mr. Graham and to serve as a witness to ensure nothing improper took place during PC Sanderson’s search. • The search was completed quickly. PC Sanderson and Mr. Graham entered the room at 6:11:06 a.m. and exited the room at 6:14:26. The strip search lasted less than 4 minutes. • I accept the uncontradicted evidence of PC Sanderson that he asked Mr. Graham to remove pieces of clothing himself, one item at a time. I accept PC Sanderson’s evidence that once he searched a piece of clothing, he returned it to Mr. Graham so that Mr. Graham was never completely undressed. PC Sanderson testified that he did not make physical contact with Mr. Graham and the search involved a visual inspection only. I accept his evidence that he uses the same process each time he conducts a strip search. • I am satisfied that nothing about the manner of execution compromised the health or safety of Mr. Graham. Nothing was located or seized during the search. • Although the video surveillance does not show the strip search itself, it shows that in his dealings with Mr. Graham, PC Sanderson was polite, professional, and sought to respect the dignity of Mr. Graham. Mr. Graham appeared unfazed when he came out of the room where the strip search took place. [ 82 ] PC Sanderson made notes indicating that a strip search was conducted but did not record details of the manner of execution. Well after the fact, PC Hartwick completed a York Regional Police form regarding the strip search on behalf of PC Sanderson, in consultation with PC Sanderson. PC Trichilo either did not make notes or lost his notes. His evidence explaining the absence of his notes was unclear. [ 83 ] PC Sanderson and PC Trichilo should have made detailed notes. However, I am satisfied that having regard to all the circumstances, the failure of PC Sanderson and PC Trichilo to make detailed notes did not render the search unreasonable. The video surveillance provided a record of the reason for the search, and the circumstances surrounding the execution of the search. I accept PC Sanderson’s evidence about the manner of execution of the search. The guidelines in Golden are important factors to consider in determining whether a search was conducted reasonably. However, non-compliance with one guideline does automatically lead to a finding that a search was unreasonable. As indicated in Atule, the goal always is to determine whether the search maintained the privacy and dignity of the detainee, not to complete a check list in a mechanical fashion. In this case the officer’s failure to follow the note taking guideline did not impact Mr. Graham’s dignity, nor did it frustrate my review of the execution of the search. [32]

In this case the officer’s failure to make detailed notes did not render the search unreasonable. Governing Legal Principles – Section 10(a) of the Charter [ 88 ] Section 10(a) of the Charter provides: “Everyone has the right on arrest or detention (a) to be informed promptly of the reasons therefor.” The purpose of the provision is to ensure that a person “understands generally the jeopardy” in which he or she finds himself or herself so they can make an informed choice about submitting to arrest or detention and exercising their right to counsel as guaranteed by s. 10(b) of the Charter in a meaningful way. An accused must be provided with sufficient information to be able to appreciate the extent of his or her jeopardy, to make meaningful choices regarding the rights to counsel and silence, and to instruct counsel. [33] [ 89 ] The Applicant has not established a breach of s. 10(a) of the Charter on a balance of probabilities. I am satisfied that Mr. Graham was promptly informed of the reason for his arrest and detention. [ 90 ] In assessing compliance with s. 10(a), the police do not need to employ specific or formalistic language in conveying the reason for the arrest or the detention. The police also do not need to provide a full description of the charges the accused is facing, nor do they need to describe the elements of the offence. Section 10(a) does not require that a detainee be told of the technical charges they may ultimately face. A person will be properly advised of the reason for their detention if they are given information that is sufficiently clear and simple to enable them to understand the reason for their detention and the extent of their jeopardy. [34]

No Breach of Section 10(a) [ 91 ] Immediately upon Mr. Graham’s arrest at 5:19 a.m. he was advised he was under arrest for mischief. Immediately after PC Sanderson seized a Ziploc bag from Mr. Graham’s pocket and looked at the contents, PC Sanderson informed the other officers, in the presence of Mr. Graham, that he was also under arrest for possession of a substance. He was feet away from Mr. Graham and the other officers when he said that Mr. Graham was also under arrest for possession of a substance. Prior to doing so he looked at the plastic Ziploc bags with the flashlight in front of Mr. Graham. I am satisfied that in these circumstance, Mr. Graham clearly understood he was also under arrest for possession of the substances that PC Sanderson had just removed from his pocket. [ 92 ] I accept that at 5:21 a.m., PC Taylor informed Mr. Graham he was under arrest for mischief and possession of a substance when he advised Mr. Graham of his right to counsel from memory during the search incident to arrest and Mr. Graham did not respond. [ 93 ] At approximately 5:27 a.m., PC Sanderson again advised Mr. Graham he was under arrest for mischief and possession of a substance. At 5:30 a.m., he arrested Mr. Graham for possession for the purpose of trafficking and reread Mr. Graham his rights to counsel. He advised him he was under arrest for mischief and possession of a controlled substance for the purpose of trafficking to ensure he understood the extent of his jeopardy. [ 94 ] There was no delay. PC Sanderson informed Mr. Graham of the more serious charge as soon as he realized that the quantity of substances and other items seized, including a scale and money, supported a charge of possession for the purposes of trafficking. General Principles s. 10(b) of the Charter [ 96 ] In Bartle, the Supreme Court of Canada made clear that upon making an arrest or effecting a detention s. 10(b) requires the police: i. To inform the detainee of his or her right to retain and instruct counsel without delay and of the existence and availability of legal aid and duty counsel; ii. If a detainee has indicated a desire to exercise this right to provide the detainee with a reasonable opportunity to exercise the right; and iii. To refrain from eliciting evidence from the detainee until he or she has had that reasonable opportunity. [35] Informational Duties [ 97 ] The first duty is the informational component of the right to counsel. The second and third duties are the implementational components of the right to counsel which are triggered when a detainee indicates a desire to exercise his or her right to counsel. [36] Failure to comply with either of the components of the right to counsel frustrates the purpose of s. 10(b) and results in a breach of the detainee’s rights. [ 98 ] The police must inform the detainee of the right to counsel immediately upon arrest or detention, subject to concerns for officer or public safety. [37] The duty is immediate because a person who is detained is vulnerable to the exercise of state power and in a position of legal jeopardy. A detainee is in immediate need of legal advice to protect his or her right against self-incrimination and to assist him or her in regaining their liberty. [38] The provisions aim to ensure that a detainee has an opportunity to be informed of their rights and obligations under the law, and to obtain advice on how to exercise those rights and perform those obligations. [39] The right to counsel also provides an important psychological “lifeline” through which a detained person will obtain not only legal advice and guidance about the procedures to which they will be subjected, but also “the sense that they are not entirely at the mercy of the police while detained.”
[40] [ 99 ] While the police have a duty to inform a detainee of his or her right to retain and instruct counsel immediately upon detention, and a duty to facilitate that right, it is subject to concerns for officer or public safety, and such limitations as prescribed by law and justified under s. 1 of the Charter. [41] Implementational Duties [ 100 ] The duty to facilitate access to a lawyer arises immediately upon the detainee’s request to speak to a lawyer. If a detainee requests to speak to a lawyer, the police have a duty to facilitate access “without delay”. [42] In Taylor, the Supreme Court of Canada held if an accused has requested to speak to a lawyer, the police have a constitutional obligation to facilitate contact with counsel at the first reasonably available opportunity and to refrain from eliciting evidence from the detainee until that time. [43] [ 101 ] There may be practical impediments to the police’s ability to facilitate access to counsel, such as a lack of means to contact counsel, or lack of privacy. Justifications for delay may be premised on a case specific risk such as the destruction of evidence, public safety, police safety, medical concerns, or some other urgent or dangerous circumstance. However, any delays must be reasonably necessary. [44] [ 102 ] Where there has been a delay in facilitating the requested access to a lawyer, the Crown bears the burden of proving the delay was reasonable in the circumstances. Whether a delay in facilitating access to counsel is reasonable is a factual and highly contextual inquiry. [45] Conclusions – No Breach of the Informational Component of s. 10(b) [ 103 ] In respect of the informational component of the right to counsel, after Mr. Graham was arrested a search incident to arrest was necessary for officer and public safety. She and Mr. Graham exchanged words before he was taken to the police station. He could be seen doing so in the ICC recording. I am satisfied that, at approximately 5:21 a.m., PC Taylor informed Mr. Graham of the reason for his arrest and provided Mr. Graham with his rights to counsel. In doing so he provided Mr. Graham his rights to counsel, “immediately”, in accordance with s. 10(b) of the Charter. The timing and content of the rights to counsel provided by PC Sanderson was captured by the ICC audio recording. After confirming Mr. Graham’s identity, at 5:27 a.m. PC Sanderson immediately informed Mr. Graham he was under arrest for mischief and possession of controlled substances, and read him the rights to counsel from his notebook. Conclusions – No Breach of the Section 10(b) Implementational Duties [ 110 ] In respect of the implementation of the rights to counsel, the first reasonably available opportunity the police had to implement the rights to counsel was after Mr. Graham was taken to the police station. To exercise a meaningful right of consultation with counsel, Mr. Graham required a telephone and sufficient privacy to protect the confidentiality of any communications. This was not possible at the scene. [ 111 ] I accept that the police did not have a phone available to put Mr. Graham in contact with a lawyer and could not afford Mr. Graham privacy at the roadside. PC Sanderson turned his mind to implementation of the rights to counsel at the roadside. He testified that he could not put Mr. Graham in contact with his lawyer from the scene because he did not have a police-issued cellular phone and could not ensure privacy for Mr. Graham when speaking with his lawyer because everything was being audio and video recorded. He testified that while he had his personal cell phone, officers are advised not to use their cellular phones for this purpose. He did not believe that any of the other officers had a police issued cellular phone. [ 112 ] In Taylor, and Brunelle, the Supreme Court of Canada accepted, for privacy and safety concerns, police officers are under no duty to provide their personal cellphones to a detainee to contact counsel. The Supreme Court in Brunelle also found that the police are not yet obligated to have inexpensive devices on hand to facilitate the right to counsel. [46] [ 113 ] I also accept PC Sanderson’s evidence that allowing Mr. Graham to use his cell phone could compromise the ongoing drug investigation. He expected the investigation of the controlled substances would continue. [ 114 ] As in Fareed, there was no breach of s. 10(b) because Mr. Graham was not given an opportunity to attempt to speak with counsel at the scene. There was no phone available, and no ability for Mr. Graham to speak to a lawyer with any degree of privacy. [47] In addition, Ms. Arnold, the complainant, was a few feet away in front of the police cruiser gathering her belongings. Mr. Graham was agitated and exchanged words with her at one point from the back of the police cruiser. There were multiple police officers on scene. It was not an appropriate location for a private confidential discussion with counsel. [ 115 ] I am satisfied that the delay associated with the time necessary to transport Mr. Graham to the police station, and for him to be paraded, booked, searched for safety reasons, and lodged in the cell was necessary and reasonable. [ 116 ] Once at the police station, it was necessary for the police to “book” Mr. Graham and complete the searches required for officer and public safety before putting him in contact with counsel. There was a delay of 15 minutes in taking Mr. Graham before the booking sergeant because a drug recognition test was being conducted with another accused. PC Sanderson explained that, for privacy and confidentiality, the police do not bring two arrestees into the booking area at the same time. PC Sanderson testified that he could not put Mr. Graham in contact with counsel in the sally port during the 15 minutes the officers waited for the booking sergeant to become available because the officers could not give Mr. Graham the necessary privacy in the sally port, everything was being recorded, and like at the scene, he did not have a police issued cellular phone available to him. [ 117 ] It was important, necessary, and reasonable that Mr. Graham be properly searched, including a strip search for officer safety and public safety reasons, given the significant safety concerns flowing from his possession of fentanyl. The potential that he may be in possession of additional fentanyl justified the delay in implementation of the right to consult counsel until the strip search was complete. [ 118 ] Where, as in this case, the police have case specific reasonable grounds to believe that a person in custody may be in possession of fentanyl it raises obvious and significant safety concerns for the police to put the detainee, especially a detainee who shows signs of emotional upset, into an unmonitored private room where the individual could ingest fentanyl in their possession or access it to potentially expose others to it. [ 119 ] I agree with the Court’s observations in Tonkin that a detainee could take advantage of an unattended moment to retrieve a hidden sample of drugs and ingest them or a hidden supply of fentanyl could be used as a weapon against police by an angry or distraught detainee. [48] In addition to the obvious concerns raised for the wellbeing of the detainee and others, the police have a responsibility for persons in their custody and would face liability if a person suffered harm where there were reasonable grounds to believe the person may have fentanyl in their possession. Where the police have case specific reasonable grounds to believe that a person in custody is in possession of fentanyl, it cannot be that they are mandated to put the individual into the cells or into a private room to contact counsel, thereby providing them an opportunity to access fentanyl without detection, prior to a strip search taking place. [ 120 ] The police concern about relinquishing physical control of a detainee prior to conducting a strip search is legitimate in these circumstances. It justified the police delaying putting Mr. Graham in a private room to contact counsel until all searches of his person were completed, including the strip search, to ensure neither Mr. Graham, or any other person, suffered harm from fentanyl. [ 121 ] In Tonkin, after emphasizing the significant safety concerns posed by fentanyl, the Court nonetheless found a s. 10(b) violation because the police did not provide the accused with access to counsel prior to a strip search. The trial judge concluded he was bound by the Ontario Court of Appeal’s ruling in McGuffie which he found required the police to provide access to counsel, prior to a strip search taking place. [49] [ 122 ]

In Tonkin the Court identified important reasons why a detainee ought to be able to obtain legal advice prior to being strip searched including: • Counsel can advise the detainee of the guidelines set out in Golden to ensure the search is conducted in a reasonable manner; • Counsel can advise the detainee to challenge the grounds for the strip search and request the authorizing officer provide a full account of his or her reasons on the booking hall video; • As detainee who speaks to counsel may elect to disclose the presence of hidden contraband which could obviate the need to conduct a strip search; and • A detainee who is hiding drugs in a body cavity may disclose that fact to counsel within a privileged conversation and obtain advice as to how to protect his or her right to challenge the strip search while ensuring the hidden drugs are extracted with minimal health risks. [50] [ 123 ] Despite the importance of advice from counsel, I am not satisfied that McGuffie establishes that in every case access to counsel must be provided prior to a strip search. This is inconsistent with the Supreme Court of Canada’s and the Ontario Court of Appeal’s recognition that delay in the implementation of the right to counsel may be justified by officer and public safety concerns. [ 124 ] In Suberu, the Supreme Court of Canada held the police have a duty to inform a detainee of his or her right to retain and instruct counsel, and a duty to facilitate that right immediately upon detention, subject to concerns for officer and public safety. [51] [ 125 ] In Rover, Justice Doherty, who also wrote for the Court of Appeal in McGuffie, accepted that concerns for public safety, police safety, or some other urgent or dangerous circumstance could justify delaying access to counsel. The Court in Rover accepted that s. 10(b) jurisprudence has always recognized that specific circumstances may justify some delay in providing a detainee access to counsel. [52] The Court acknowledged the tension between the seriousness of suspending an accused person’s access to counsel – a psychological “lifeline” for those who are detained, that guards against the risk of involuntary self-incrimination – and the critical importance of protecting the safety of the public and law enforcement, as well as preserving evidence. While accepting that the law permits a delay in the facilitation of the right to counsel, the Court found it is only where the police have turned their minds to the specific circumstances of the case and concluded “on some reasonable basis, that police or public safety, or the need to preserve evidence justifies some delay in granting access to counsel”. [53]

Justice Doherty explained the test as follows: To fall within the exception to the requirement that an arrested person be allowed to speak to counsel without delay, the police must actually turn their mind to the specific circumstances of the case, and they must have reasonable grounds to justify the delay. The justification may be premised on the risk of the destruction of evidence, public safety, police safety, or some other urgent or dangerous circumstance. [54] [ 126 ] More recently, in Leonard
[55], the Ontario Court of Appeal confirmed that a reasonable delay in accessing counsel justified by case-specific concerns for officer safety does not violate s. 10(b). Whether a delay in facilitating access to counsel is reasonable must be a factual and context specific inquiry. [56] [ 127 ] The factual circumstances and legal findings in McGuffie are distinguishable. The issue before the Court in McGuffie was not whether the police are required to provide access to counsel before a strip search in every case. Rather, the issue in McGuffie was whether the trial judge erred in admitting evidence of controlled substances seized under s. 24(2) of the Charter following violations of ss. 8 and 9, and 10(b) of the Charter. [ 128 ] In McGuffie the police were called to a bar after security reported five men passing a gun around. When the investigating officer arrived, Mr. McGuffie was identified by security as one of the men who had passed around the gun. When Mr. McGuffie left the bar, the officer placed him under investigative detention and conducted a lawful safety search incident to the investigative detention. Nothing was located. However, instead of releasing Mr. McGuffie, the officer, without grounds to arrest, unlawfully detained him in the back of a police car for 30 minutes. During this period, Mr. McGuffie was not informed of his rights to counsel. When the officer returned, he conducted a second search of Mr. McGuffie, found to violate s. 8 of the Charter, and located a package containing white powder, later determined to be cocaine. He arrested Mr. McGuffie for possession of cocaine, and informed him of the right to counsel for the first time, 40 minutes after he was first detained. [ 129 ] At the police station, the officer informed the booking sergeant that he wanted to strip search Mr. McGuffie for a handgun and to look for more drugs. This was despite the fact the officer knew the firearm from the bar had been found. Against this factual backdrop, Justice Doherty, writing for the Court, stated “Nothing was said about giving the accused an opportunity to speak to a lawyer before the search, despite PC Greenwood knowing he wanted to speak to a lawyer. Nor did any of the officers allude to the possibility that the police should refrain from conducting the strip search until the appellant had an opportunity to speak with counsel.” [ 130 ] There were serious violations during the execution of the strip search. Three officers were present during the strip search, the door to the room remained open, and when the accused resisted, the investigating officer stood on his ankles for the purpose of causing him pain. This conduct was characterized as a “purely gratuitous act”. The police found a package of cocaine sewn into the accused’s underwear and a package of cocaine concealed in his buttocks. After the strip search was complete, the accused was put in contact with his lawyer, approximately 90 minutes after he was first detained. [ 131 ] In McGuffie, the trial judge held that while the initial investigative detention and safety search incident to the investigative detention were lawful, thereafter the police “repeatedly violated the appellant’s constitutional rights”. The Court of Appeal agreed that the accused’s rights under sections 8, 9, and 10(b) were violated. In respect of s. 10(b), the Court concluded the accused’s rights were violated because he was not provided with rights to counsel when first placed in the cruiser, and the police failed to give him an opportunity to speak to his lawyer for an hour after he requested to do so. [ 132 ] The strip search was found to be unreasonable and in violation of s. 8 of the Charter because it was executed unreasonably due to the inappropriate force used, and due to the failure to respect the accused’s privacy because the door was open during the search which took place in the presence of police officers who were unnecessary for the execution of the search. [57] [ 133 ] The issue on appeal was whether the trial judge erred in his or her application of the exclusionary rule under s. 24(2) by admitting the evidence. While assessing the seriousness of the breach, Justice Doherty stated the trial judge erred in finding there was no institutional misconduct because when the accused got to the station “his superiors did not express any concern… about whether the appellant had been given the opportunity to speak to his lawyer before undergoing the proposed strip search” and seemed “unconcerned about the excessive use of force by Constable Greenwood and the failure to minimize the intrusiveness and humiliation inherent in that kind of search”. [58] The appeal did not consider whether officer or public safety issues may justify delaying access to counsel until after a strip search is completed. [ 134 ] Notably, in Golden the Supreme Court of Canada did not include in the manner of execution guidelines a requirement that the accused be provided with an opportunity to consult with counsel. [ 135 ] In Taylor and Brunelle, the Supreme Court established that where there is a delay in the implementation of the right to counsel, the Crown has the burden of establishing the delay is reasonable. This involves a fact specific and contextual assessment. Clearly, where an accused has asked to speak to a lawyer the police must facilitate access prior to a strip search unless compelling safety reasons justify the delay. In many instances delay in providing access to counsel to conduct a strip search will be unreasonable, such as where police are searching for evidence that does not pose an immediate danger to the accused or police. However, in my view, in cases where there are case specific reasonable grounds to believe that an accused is in possession of a dangerous drug like fentanyl that may pose an immediate and serious risk to the health or safety of the accused and others, a brief delay in providing access to counsel will be justified to conduct a strip search. [ 136 ] The need to delay access to counsel to ensure an accused does not cause harm to him or herself or others by accessing fentanyl, is equally or more compelling than the circumstances where officer safety concerns justify a delay in access to counsel for execution of a search warrant. [59] [ 137 ] Given the case specific circumstances that justified the strip search in this case, the delay in providing access to counsel until after the strip search was complete was necessary to ensure that Mr. Graham did not have further drugs, including fentanyl, in his possession that could cause serious harm to himself, the police, or other persons at the police station. It was unsafe to put him in a private location until the officers confirmed he did not have any further fentanyl in his possession. The evidence of PC Sanderson, together with the content of the booking video, makes clear that this immediate danger was something the police turned their minds to and was the reason the strip search was conducted. [ 138 ] In reaching my conclusion that the delay in implementing rights to counsel was justified in this case such that there was no violation of s. 10(b) of the Charter, I have considered the brief duration of the search, which was less than four minutes. Once the strip search was completed, after Mr. Graham was placed in the cells, PC Sanderson immediately contacted Mr. Graham’s lawyer. [ 139 ] Unlike McGuffie, this was not a circumstance where PC Sanderson or Sgt. Kozak were indifferent to or unconcerned about Mr. Graham’s right to counsel. Throughout his dealings with Mr. Graham, PC Sanderson sought to ensure that Mr. Graham understood the reasons for his arrest, the nature of his jeopardy, and that he had a right to speak to a lawyer. Once the safety concerns were addressed, following the strip search, he immediately called Mr. Graham’s counsel of choice. [ 140 ] I am satisfied that this was the first reasonably available opportunity and that the delay in implementation of the right to counsel was necessary to ensure the safety of Mr. Graham, the police, and other prisoners. The officers complied with their implementational duties under s. 10(b) of the Charter. Section 24(2) of the Charter
[60] [ 141 ] If I have erred in finding there was no violation of section 8, related to the execution of the strip search, or s. 10(b) of the Charter, related to the implementation of the right to counsel, I would nonetheless find that the Applicant has not established the admission of the evidence would bring the administration of justice into disrepute. Obtained in A Manner [ 142 ] Section 24(2) of the Charter does not create an automatic rule of exclusion for all unconstitutionally obtained evidence. Rather, under s. 24(2), where a court concludes that evidence was “ obtained in a manner ” that infringed or denied any rights or freedoms guaranteed by the Charter, the evidence must be excluded if the Applicant establishes that having regard to all the circumstances, the admission of the evidence in the proceedings would bring the administration of justice into disrepute. [ 143 ] I am satisfied that even though the controlled substances were seized prior to the breaches, if the alleged s. 8 and s. 10(b) breaches occurred, the “obtained in a manner” requirement is met. Since Pino, Courts have taken a “very broad reading” and generous view of the phrase “obtained in a manner”. The jurisprudence has accepted that courts should consider the entire “chain of events” between the accused and the police. The requirement may be met where the evidence and the Charter breach are part of the same transaction or course of conduct. The connection between the evidence and the breach may be causal, temporal, or contextual, or any combination of the three, but the connection cannot be “too tenuous or too remote”. [61] [ 144 ] Recently in Griffiths, similar to this case, the evidence was located incident to arrest, and the s. 10(b) breach occurred more than two hours later. Nonetheless, the Court of Appeal found the “obtained in a manner” criterion was satisfied because the discovery of the evidence and the s. 10(b) breach all arose from the same transaction, the appellant’s arrest, and the connection was temporal because the discovery of the evidence was relatively close in time to the s. 10(b) breach and was part of the continuum of events arising from the appellant’s arrest. The connection was “contextual” because the discovery of the evidence arose out of the same events flowing from the appellant’s arrest that led to the s. 10(b) breach. For the same reasons, although there is no causal connection, I am satisfied the nexus is sufficient in this case to engage s. 24(2). Assessment of the Grant Factors [ 145 ] According to the Supreme Court of Canada decision in Grant
[62], there is a three-pronged test that must be applied when considering the admissibility of evidence under s. 24(2) of the

Charter. The court must assess and balance the effect of admitting the evidence on society’s confidence in the justice system having regard to the following factors: (1) the seriousness of the Charter -infringing state conduct; (2) the impact of the breach on the Charter -protected interests of the accused; and (3) society’s interest in the adjudication of the case on its merits. [63] [ 146 ] This requires attention to whether the overall repute of the justice system, when viewed in the long term and objectively speaking, will be negatively affected by the admission of the evidence, taking all the circumstances into account. [64] Section 24(2) is not aimed at punishing the police or providing compensation to the accused, but rather at systemic concerns. The focus is societal and is on the broad impact of admission of the evidence on the long-term repute of the justice system. [65]

Seriousness of the Breach [ 147 ] In assessing the seriousness of a breach, Grant requires an examination of the police conduct and a determination of where it fits on a spectrum from mere technical breach at one end of the spectrum to bad faith violation at the other. In Beaver the court explained as follows: The first line of inquiry under s. 24(2) considers whether the Charter -infringing state conduct is so serious that the court needs to dissociate itself from it. This inquiry requires the court to situate the Charter -infringing conduct on a scale of culpability. At one end of the scale is conduct that constitutes a wilful or reckless disregard of Charter rights, a systemic pattern of Charter -infringing conduct, or a major departure from Charter standards. At the other end of the scale are less serious Charter breaches, including breaches that are inadvertent, technical, or minor or those that reflect an understandable mistake. The more severe the state’s Charter -infringing conduct, the greater the need for courts to dissociate themselves from it. (citations omitted). [66] [ 148 ] In assessing where on the spectrum the breach lies in terms of seriousness, and the impact of the breach, the facts in this case are very different than cases such as Jarrett
[67] and Rover, cases where courts found that delay in provision or implementation of the rights to counsel justified the exclusion of evidence despite the absence of a causal connection between the evidence and the breach. [ 149 ] In Jarrett, the accused was not provided with an opportunity to speak with counsel for 30 hours after requesting to do so. Jarrett was arrested for assaulting a police officer, possession for the purposes of trafficking, and other offences. The police used significant force in executing the arrest, including use of tasers. Following the arrest, the accused was informed of the right to counsel and requested to speak to a specific lawyer before he was taken to the hospital where he remained for 20 hours handcuffed to a bed, during which time he was not provided access to counsel. [ 150 ] Once Mr. Jarrett was released from hospital and taken to the police station, he waited another 10 hours to speak with counsel. The only step taken by the police to facilitate contact with counsel was a single voicemail message left for his lawyer approximately 1.5 hours after arrest. After the single phone call there was no further follow-up by the police. [ 151 ] The trial Judge held the accused’s s. 10(b) rights were violated because of the failure of the police to make further efforts to put him in contact with counsel but admitted the evidence, a fanny pack containing illicit drugs, after finding the breach was inadvertent, the impact limited, and society’s interest in determination of the case on the merits was significant. [ 152 ] The Court of Appeal held the trial Judge erred in his s. 24(2) analysis and found the breach to be serious because of the 30-hour delay and the fact that over the lengthy period nothing was done to follow up the single call or to inform the accused of what had been done to put him in contact with his lawyer. [ 153 ] In Rover, the accused was arrested prior to a search warrant being obtained and executed on his home. At the time of arrest, when provided the rights to counsel, he asked to speak to a lawyer. The police put him in the cells and delayed contacting his lawyer for six hours while they applied for, obtained, and executed a search warrant. The court found that it was routine practice for the police to delay the implementation of the right to counsel for an indeterminate period when executing warrants to search a place for drugs believed to be connected to the accused person. The decision to delay access to counsel occurred without the officers turning their mind to the specific circumstances of the case. During the period of delay, the accused was not told why he was not being allowed to speak to counsel or when he would be allowed to speak to counsel. [ 154 ] The Court in Rover made clear that constitutional breaches that are the direct result of systemic or institutional police practices render the police conduct more serious for the purposes of s. 24(2). [ 155 ] The seriousness of the police conduct in Jarrett and Rover was of a different nature than what occurred in this case. Firstly, the delay in this case is of a different magnitude than the delays in Jarrett, 30 hours, and Rover, six hours. The total delay from when Mr. Graham first advised that he wanted to speak to a lawyer, until PC Sanderson took steps to put him in touch with a lawyer, was approximately one hour. If there was an error by the police in implementing the right to counsel, it was the failure of one of the officers to place a call to Mr. Levine as soon as Mr. Graham arrived at the police station, so that Mr. Graham could potentially speak to Mr. Levine as soon as the booking process was complete. One of the officers could have placed a call to Mr. Levine at approximately 5:40 a.m., approximately 50 minutes earlier. The 4-minute strip search did not significantly delay the call to counsel. [ 156 ] The more significant issue in assessing the seriousness if the police were required to provide access to counsel prior to the strip search being conducted is conduct that deprived the accused of the opportunity to received advice prior to the execution of a strip search which has a significant impact on the privacy and integrity of the accused. In the circumstances of this case, the breach was not serious because the police had case specific reasonable grounds to believe Mr. Graham was in possession of fentanyl, and after turning their mind to the specific circumstances, conducted a strip search for the safety of Mr. Graham, the police, and others. If McGuffie establishes that the officers were required to provide access to counsel prior to conducting the strip search, in my view the law was unclear because of the conflicting authorities that permit delay in providing access to counsel for legitimate safety reasons. The police were acting in good faith to protect not only themselves, but also Mr. Graham. [ 157 ] This is not a case where PC Sanderson was indifferent to Mr. Graham’s contact with counsel. When Mr. Graham did not respond to Sgt. Kozak’s question about whether he wanted to speak to a lawyer, he informed her that he had provided the name of a lawyer. He contacted Mr. Levine immediately after the strip search was complete and when Mr. Levine did not call back, PC Sanderson placed a second call to counsel and as a result Mr. Graham spoke to counsel at approximately 7:27 a.m. [ 158 ] Another important difference between this case and Rover is that the systemic and institutional considerations present in Rover are absent. There was no evidence that the delay in providing access to counsel in this case occurred due to a customary or routine practice that “sacrifices a detainee’s constitutional right to immediate access to counsel in favour of the police interest in the efficient use of its resources”. If there was a s. 10(b) violation, it was situation specific. [68] [ 159 ] In respect of the execution of the strip search, if there was a violation associated with insufficient record keeping and failure to close the door to the room, these violations were more of a result of carelessness than a wilful disregard of Mr. Graham’s Charter -protected interests. [ 160 ] In my view, if there were violations associated to the strip search and delay of the rights to counsel, the police were acting in good faith and the violations fall towards the minor end of the spectrum. While many cases have characterized delay in providing access to counsel as a serious breach, those cases generally involved delays of a much longer duration. The Charter -offending conduct in this case did not constitute a wilful or reckless disregard of Mr. Graham’s Charter rights, nor was it part of a systemic pattern of Charter -infringing conduct. It did not represent a major departure from Charter standards. [ 161 ] Having regard to all of the circumstances, an assessment of the seriousness of the Charter -infringing conduct supports admission rather than exclusion of the evidence. Impact of the Breach [ 162 ] I accept there was a psychological impact on Mr. Graham caused by the delay in waiting to speak with a lawyer especially in the context of a strip search taking place prior to access to counsel. He was not able to get the advice that may have benefited him in the ways described above prior to the strip search. In addition, I accept, even though the area was a controlled private area with no one other than the two officers engaged in the search present, the door being partially open may have had some impact on Mr. Graham. [ 163 ] However, a significant factor attenuating the impact of the violation in this case is the absence of any causal relationship between the violation and the finding of the controlled substances. The controlled substances were located during a lawful search incident to arrest at the scene, prior to any alleged s. 10(b) violation, and not as a result of the strip search. While the impact of a breach can be serious even without a causal connection, the complete absence of a causal connection between the evidence and the breach will generally mitigate the impact of the breach on the accused’s Charter -protected interests. [69] [ 164 ]

In Beaver, Justice Jamal, writing for a majority of the Supreme Court explained as follows: …In appropriate cases, the lack of a causal connection between the breaches and the obtaining of the impugned evidence may mitigate the impact of the breach on the accused's Charter -protected interests ( Grant, at para. 122; R. v. Mian, 2014 SCC 54, [2014] 2 S.C.R. 689, at para. 87; R. v. Rover, 2018 ONCA 745, 143 O.R. (3d) 135, at para. 43; R. v. Pileggi, 2021 ONCA 4, 153 O.R. (3d) 561, at para. 120 ). As this Court explained in Grant, the strength of the causal connection between the Charter infringement and the impugned evidence plays "a useful role... in assessing the actual impact of the breach on the protected interests of the accused" (para. 122). Here, no such causal connection exists….The lack of a causal connection between the Charter breaches and Beaver's confession mitigates the actual impact of the breaches on his Charter -protected interests. [70] [ 165 ] Recently, in Hamouth, the defence sought to exclude a gun seized during the execution of a search warrant. The gun was seized prior to serious s. 10(b) violations occurring. Despite the serious nature of the s. 10(b) breaches, Fairburn A.C.J., writing for the Ontario Court of Appeal, upheld the trial judge’s decision to admit the gun. In doing so Justice Fairburn confirmed that the lack of a causal connection is a well established and proper factor for consideration in assessing the impact of a breach and one that may mitigate the impact of the breach on an individual’s Charter -protected interests, weighing against exclusion. [71] [ 166 ] In this case the controlled substances were found before the breach occurred. The recovery of the drugs had no connection whatsoever to the alleged s. 8 and s. 10(b) Charter breaches. The evidence was discovered during a lawful search incident to arrest. The evidence was in police custody before the breach took place. The controlled substances would have been discovered irrespective of any Charter violation. [ 167 ] Section 10(b) is meant to assist detainees to regain their liberty and guard against the risk of involuntary self-incrimination. There is no evidence that the police delayed access strategically or attempted to elicit inculpatory statements from the Applicant during the delay. There is no indication that the Applicant would have regained his liberty earlier had there been no delay in accessing counsel. [72] [ 168 ] For these reasons, I find that any s. 10(b) or s. 8 violation had a minimal impact on Mr. Graham’s Charter -protected interests and the second line of inquiry also favours the admission of the evidence. [73] [ 169 ] The third line of inquiry, society’s interest on the adjudication of the case on its merits, “considers societal concerns and asks whether the truth-seeking function of the criminal trial process would be better served by the admission or exclusion of the evidence. Relevant factors under this inquiry include the reliability of the evidence, the importance of the evidence to the prosecution’s case, and the seriousness of the offence at issue.”
[74] [ 170 ] The controlled substances are reliable real evidence, and the evidence is essential to the Crown’s case. The exclusion of the controlled substances would effectively end the prosecution and preclude any consideration of the merits of the case. The alleged Charter violation in no way undermined the reliability of the evidence. Excluding reliable evidence critical to the Crown’s case, such as the controlled substances, can also undermine the truth-seeking function of the justice system and render the trial unfair from the public’s perspective, thus bringing the administration of justice into disrepute. [ 171 ] Society has a strong public interest in controlling the possession and distribution of harmful substances such as cocaine and especially fentanyl, which poses a grave danger to the community. There is a significant public interest in such cases being determined on the merits. [ 172 ] The truth-seeking function of the criminal process, and the societal interest in a criminal trial on the merits would be seriously undermined if such highly reliable and critical evidence were excluded. [ 173 ] This third prong of the analytical framework under 24(2) of the Charter strongly favours admission of the evidence. [75]

Conclusion 24(2) [ 174 ] In my view, all three prongs of the Grant test support the admission of the evidence: the breach was not serious, had a minimal impact on the Applicant, and there is a strong societal interest on the adjudication of the case on the merits. [ 175 ] In reaching my findings I have also considered the findings of the Ontario Court of Appeal in Hobeika
[76], where the accused was charged with possession for the purpose of trafficking and possession of proceeds of crime. The accused, following arrest, asked to speak with a specific lawyer. He was taken to the police station, strip searched and placed in the cells. No steps were taken to put the accused in contact with his counsel of choice for a period of 4 hours and no explanation for the failure to do so was provided. There was no evidence of any other breaches, and no evidence that the breach was systemic. The Court held that despite the serious nature of the s. 10(b) breach, the evidence of the drugs and money seized was admissible under s. 24(2). [ 176 ] The Applicant has not established that admission of the controlled substances would bring the administration of justice into disrepute. The evidence of the controlled substances will be admitted. D. Substantive Issues on the Trial Proper [ 177 ] The issues on the trial proper were focussed. Ms. Mirza made reasonable admissions including admitting the date, time, jurisdiction, and the identity of Mr. Graham. The defence also conceded that if the Crown established that Mr. Graham was in possession of controlled substances, that his possession was for the purposes of trafficking. This was a reasonable concession given the quantity and different types of substances, and his possession of potential cutting agents, small baggies, and a scale. [ 178 ] At issue is whether the Crown has proven the nature and quantity of the substances beyond a reasonable doubt. [ 179 ] In terms of the controlled substances seized, as noted above, the defence submits the Crown failed to prove Mr. Graham was in possession of the controlled substances beyond a reasonable doubt due to gaps in the evidence establishing continuity of the items seized and the nature and quantity of the substances. The defence submits PC Panacci did not submit a sample from both Ziploc bags with the green strip, containing what was believed to be cocaine and, as a result, I cannot be satisfied beyond a reasonable doubt that both bags contained cocaine, especially given that a sample from another Ziploc bag containing a white substance was not cocaine. [ 180 ] The Crown submits the essential elements of possession for the purposes of trafficking have been proven beyond a reasonable doubt. The Crown submits it has established continuity and the integrity of the items seized, specifically that what was analyzed by Health Canada was from the substances seized from Mr. Graham. The Crown further submits that although only one sample was taken from the two Ziploc bags with the green strip containing a substance believed to be cocaine, the only reasonable inference is that both bags contained cocaine. [ 181 ] In respect of continuity, the Crown must establish beyond a reasonable doubt that the samples tested by Health Canada, and found to be controlled substances, originated from, and represent the substances seized during the investigation from Mr. Graham. Continuity of an exhibit goes to weight and not admissibility. Gaps in continuity are not fatal unless they raise a reasonable doubt as to the integrity of the exhibit. [ 182 ] I am satisfied that all of the substances seized, and the samples submitted to Health Canada originated from the samples seized during the search incident to arrest of Mr. Graham. The substances seized were in his personal possession. The ICC recording captured the officers locating the substances. The items seized by PC Farrow included a dark substance he believed to be fentanyl, which he located in Mr. Graham’s front hoodie pocket. It was a small see-through cylindrical bag that looked like a ball containing individual pieces. It looked purple. [ 185 ] PC Farrow also located two additional tied clear baggies containing a white substance he believed to be cocaine. They were in Mr. Graham’s left inner pants’ pocket. PC Farrow also located a bag containing cannabis on Mr. Graham’s right side. [ 186 ] As is evident in the ICC recording, the substances located by PC Farrow were also placed on the hood of PC Sanderson’s cruiser. [ 187 ] I accept that all the drugs that were initially placed on the hood of the cruiser were subsequently placed inside PC Sanderson’s cruiser by PC Sanderson and PC Farrow because of the wind. I also find as a fact that PC Farrow, at PC Sanderson’s request, subsequently moved all the controlled substances from inside the cruiser, into a brown paper bag in the rear of PC Sanderson’s cruiser. The paper bag in the rear of the cruiser also contained other items seized from Mr. Graham, such as a purse and car key. The booking surveillance video captured PC Taylor removing the seized items including the substances from the brown paper bag at the booking desk in front of Sgt. Kozak shortly after 5:36 a.m. [ 190 ] I accept PC Taylor and PC Panacci’s evidence that PC Taylor turned the controlled substances over to PC Panacci at approximately 6:25 a.m. PC Panacci subsequently processed the drugs and I accept her evidence about the substances she processed and the process she used. Photos documented the substances she processed before and during the processing. These photos became Exhibits 5, 6, 8, and 10. I find as a fact the substances PC Panacci processed originated from Mr. Graham. [ 191 ] PC Panacci processed the substance seized in the cylindrical bag by PC Farrow believed to be fentanyl. PC Panacci removed a.28-gram sample and put the.28-gram sample into Health Canada submission bag number ON0157380. The remaining bulk fentanyl was secured in another property bag. It weighed 9.43 grams. [ 192 ] I accept PC Panacci’s evidence that after preparing the samples for submission to Health Canada, PC Panacci put the bags into the 4 District drug locker. No one had access to the drug locker except for the personnel responsible for the property room and submission of the samples. [ 193 ] The Health Canada Certificates of Analyst establish that the samples arrived at Health Canada sealed and unopened. The sample numbers identified by PC Panacci correspond with the sample numbers on the Health Canada certificates. The certificates include the York Regional Police Occurrence number and PC Panacci’s name and badge number. [ 194 ] I am satisfied that the Certificates in Exhibits 7, 9 and 11 accurately set out the results of the samples taken by PC Panacci, which originated from the substances seized from Mr. Graham. [ 195 ] The Health Canada Certificate of Analyst, Exhibit 7, pertains to the.28-gram sample from the substance seized by PC Farrow that was believed to be fentanyl. The Health Canada Certificate establishes that sample ON0157380 is fentanyl mixed with benzodiazepines, namely flualprazolam and etizolam that are also controlled substances. The sample also tested for the presence of etonitazene, another controlled substance. The agreed statement of fact indicates that etonitazene is a member of the benzimidazole opioid family. It is a synthetic opioid used for testing on animals that is 1000-1500 times more potent than morphine. The agreed statement of fact indicates the presence of these drugs made the fentanyl more potent and created greater risk to the user. [ 196 ] I am satisfied beyond a reasonable doubt that the sample described by Exhibit 7 originated from the cylindrical bag seized by PC Farrow that he believed to be fentanyl. I am satisfied that Mr. Graham was in personal possession of 9.71 grams of fentanyl which also contained etizolam, flualparzolam, and etonitazene. His possession was for the purposes of trafficking. [ 197 ] After processing the substance believed to be fentanyl, PC Panacci processed the substances in the two Ziploc bags with the green strips, believed to be cocaine. The two bags are depicted in Exhibit 8. PC Panacci removed a sample of.25 grams from one of the two Ziploc bags. The sample was placed into Health Canada submission bag, ONO157382. PC Panacci testified that the substances in both bags looked similar. After the removal of the sample, the remaining bulk amount of suspected cocaine from the two Ziploc bags with the green strip was 45.09 grams. The remainder was placed in a property bag with tag number A15172135. The total weight of the white substance from the two Ziploc bags was 46.34 grams. [ 198 ] The Certificate of Analyst, Exhibit 9, establishes that the.25-gram sample ONO157382 was examined and analyzed and was found to contain cocaine. I am satisfied beyond a reasonable doubt that the source of the sample, which originated from one of the Ziploc bags with a green strip seized from Mr. Graham, was cocaine. [ 199 ] The last item processed by PC Panacci was the large clear Ziploc bag depicted in Exhibit 10. The total weight of the white powdery substance in the bag was 74.62 grams. A sample of.29 grams was removed and submitted to Health Canada in bag ONO157381. The remaining bulk substance was 74.33 grams. [77] [ 200 ] The Health Canada Certificate of Analyst for sample ONO157381 established that the analyst was not able to identify a substance within the meaning of the Controlled Drugs and Substances Act. The white substance taken from the large clear Ziploc bag did not test as cocaine. [ 201 ] PC Panacci testified that the contents of the two Ziploc bags with a green strip, looked identical. She believed they were the same substance because they had the same packaging and appearance. Given the similar appearance of contents of the two Ziploc bags with the green strip, and the fact they were packaged the same, both Ziploc bags with the green strip probably contained cocaine. However, that is not the only reasonable inference. There is no evidence that the substance in the Ziploc bags with the green strip looked any different than the white substance contained in the large clear Ziploc bag that did not test as cocaine. [ 202 ] As a result, I am not satisfied that the Crown has proven beyond a reasonable doubt that both Ziploc bags with the green strip contained cocaine. The bag not sampled could have contained the same substance as the large Ziploc bag that did not contain cocaine. [ 203 ] I am satisfied beyond a reasonable doubt that the Ziploc bag with the green strip from which the sample was taken was cocaine. Because PC Pinacci did not weigh the contents of the two bags separately, I do not know the precise amount contained within the bag from which the sample was taken. However, it was approximately 20 grams of cocaine. Both Ziploc bags with the green strip appeared to contain a similar amount of white substance and the total weight of the two bags was 46.34 grams. [78] [ 204 ] I am satisfied that on the evidence, including the agreed statement of fact, the only reasonable inference is that the controlled substances, including the 9.71 grams of fentanyl and approximately 20 grams of cocaine, were possessed by

Mr. Graham for the purposes of trafficking. The fact that the Crown proved that Mr. Graham was in possession of approximately 20 grams of cocaine rather than 46.34 grams of cocaine, does not impact the proof beyond a reasonable doubt that Mr. Graham possessed the cocaine for the purposes of trafficking having regard to the amount and the circumstances of the possession, including the other items seized such as the scale, baggies, cutting agent and fentanyl. According to the agreed statement of facts, 20 grams of cocaine (.7 ounces) has a value of between $950 and $1200. In addition, in paragraph 1 of the agreed statement of fact, the parties agreed the substances located on Mr. Graham’s person were possessed for the purposes of trafficking. [ 205 ] I am satisfied that the Crown has established the essential elements of the two counts of possession for the purposes of trafficking beyond a reasonable doubt. As a result of my findings there will be findings of guilt to Counts 1 and 2. Released: October 28, 2024
