                This is a case under the Youth Criminal Justice Act and is subject to subsections 110(1) and 111(1) and section 129 of the Act.  These provisions read as follows:

    110.  IDENTITY OF OFFENDER NOT TO BE PUBLISHED —(1)  Subject to this section, no person shall publish the name of a young person, or any other information related to a young person, if it would identify the young person as a young person dealt with under this Act.

    .   .   .

    111.  IDENTITY OF VICTIM OR WITNESS NOT TO BE PUBLISHED —(1)  Subject to this section, no person shall publish the name of a child or young person, or any other information related to a child or a young person, if it would identify the child or young person as having been a victim of, or as having appeared as a witness in connection with, an offence committed or alleged to have been committed by a young person.

    .   .   .

    129.  NO SUBSEQUENT DISCLOSURE —  No person who is given access to a record or to whom information is disclosed under this Act shall disclose that information to any person unless the disclosure is authorized under this Act.

    Subsection 138(1) of the Youth Criminal Justice Act, which deals with the consequences of failure to comply with these provisions, states as follows:

    138.  OFFENCES —  Every person who contravenes subsection 110(1) (identity of offender not to be published), 111(1) (identity of victim or witness not to be published)  . . .  or section 129 (no subsequent disclosure)  . . .

       (a)   is guilty of an indictable offence and liable to imprisonment for a term not exceeding two years; or

       (b)   is guilty of an offence punishable on summary conviction.

    Before Justice C.K. Assié

    Heard on July 17, 2025

    Reasons for Judgment released on September 2, 2025

     

     

    Sébastien Gerdun.............................................................................. counsel for the Crown

    Harpreet Saini........................................................................ counsel for the accused Z.D.

    Lakhwinder Sandhu............................................................. counsel for the accused S.S.

     

    ASSIÉ J.:

    Introduction

    [1]         Four young persons were charged with two different home invasions to facilitate stealing vehicles. The Crown is prosecuting them jointly. Two of the defendants apply to have their charges stayed because of unreasonable delay in getting their matter to trial. The other two defendants are effectively the cause of the delay.

    [2]         The total delay from the swearing of the Information on June 9, 2024, to the anticipated end of the trial on December 19, 2025, is 18 months and 11 days.

    [3]         This application raises one question: Was the delay in this joint prosecution reasonable?

    Facts Underlying the Charges

    [4]         In the early hours of June 9th, 2025, five suspects smashed their way into a home on Meadowridge Court in Brampton. One of the suspects pointed a gun at the homeowner and demanded the keys to a vehicle. They dragged the homeowner outside, made him start his car, and then drove off with the car. Later that same morning, the same parties went to a home on Sacramento Road in Brampton where they smashed their way in and stole a second vehicle.

    [5]         Approximately forty minutes later, the vehicle from the second address was located by the police. It went off the road, mounted a curb, and drove across the grass. It was found extensively damaged and unoccupied. A search of the area was conducted with the assistance of a K9-unit. Items from the first address were found in the vicinity of the damaged vehicle.

    [6]         The police learned that the suspects fled into nearby backyards and eventually four of the suspects smashed their way into a third home on Oakview Road. One suspect was arrested outside of the home. The others were found hiding in a closet inside the home.

    Facts Underlying the s. 11(b) Application

    [7]         Z.D. and S.S. are applying for stays pursuant to ss. 11(b) and 24(1) of the Charter. R.I. and T.B. have not participated in the Application, though T.B.’s counsel advised he would re-evaluate his participation in the event the Application was granted.

    [8]         The following are the facts relevant to the Application:

    (1)         June 9, 2024: The Information was sworn.

    (2)         October 7, 2024: A judicial pre-trial was held. The time estimated for trial was determined to be ten days with seven civilian witnesses and ten police witnesses anticipated to testify.

    (3)         October 24, 2024: Initial trial dates were secured on this date.

    (4)         November 12-December 4, 2025: These were the earliest dates offered by the court. The dates were available for S.S. and T.B.’s counsel. The dates were not available Z.D. and R.I.’s counsel.   

    (5)         March 2-13, 2026: These were the initial trial dates agreeable to all parties.

    [9]         The anticipated delay from the swearing of the Information to the end of the initial trial was 21 months and 4 days. Had all defence counsel been available for the earliest dates offered by the court the delay would have been 17 months and 25 days.

    [10]      On the day the March 2026 trial dates were set, counsel advised that delay was a concern and scheduled a date for hearing an 11(b) Application in November of 2025.

    [11]      The Crown responded to the concerns over delay by bringing the matter forward in an attempt to find earlier trial dates. The following are additional facts relevant to the Application:

    (1)         January 9, 2025: The Crown sent an email to all counsel suggesting meeting to select earlier dates.

    (2)         January 15, 2025: The parties appeared in court to select earlier dates.

    (3)         February 24-March 10, 2025: These dates were the earliest dates offered by the court to counsel. The Crown was available. However, some of the counsel were not available.

    (4)         November 3-7, December 15-18, 2025: These are the current trial dates selected during the January 15, 2025, appearance.   

    [12]      The anticipated delay from the swearing of the Information to the end of the new trial dates is 18 months and 11 days.

    [13]      During the January 15, 2025, appearance, Z.D.’s counsel advised he was unavailable during the following weeks, which were offered by the court and agreeable to the Crown:

    (1)         March 3-10, 2025.

    (2)         September 15-19, 2025.

    (3)         September 22-26, 2025.

    (4)         November 17-21, 2025.

    (5)         November 24-28, 2025.

    (6)         December 1-5, 2025.

    (7)         December 8-12, 2025.

    [14]      S.S.’ counsel advised he was unavailable only during the week of November 24-28, 2025.

    [15]      Looking at the complete record, regardless of the unavailability of Z.D. and S.S’ lawyers during specific weeks, the delay was principally caused by the unavailability of R.I.’s lawyer.

    [16]      During the January 15, 2025, scheduling conference in court, Z.D.’s counsel suggested that R.I. be severed. Ultimately, the Crown never severed any of the four co-accused and none of the defence counsel brought severance applications. In submissions, Z.D.’s counsel advised the reason for not applying for severance was that he estimated the application would have a very low chance of success.

    Section 11(b) of the Charter

    [17]      The Supreme Court’s decision in Jordan established the current framework for assessing allegations of unreasonable delay under s. 11(b) of the Charter.[1] The framework requires the court to examine the total delay from the swearing of the Information to the anticipated end of the trial. From that delay, the court is to deduct any delay explicitly or implicitly waived by the defence or caused by the defence (either through frivolous applications or periods of time where the Crown and the court were ready to proceed but the defence was not).[2]

    [18]      The next step in the framework is for the court to determine if any ‘exceptional circumstances’ existed. Exceptional circumstances are circumstances that fall outside the Crown’s control in the sense that they are reasonably unforeseen or reasonably unavoidable and that the Crown cannot reasonably remedy the delay that arises from those circumstances. Events do not have to be rare or uncommon to be classified as ‘exceptional circumstances.’[3]

    [19]      Exceptional circumstances fall into two categories: 1) discrete events, or 2) complex cases. Discrete events include instances where a participant falls sick, or a witness recants requiring additional applications, or the parties underestimated the amount of time needed for the trial.[4]

    [20]      After subtracting any defence delay and the delay caused by discrete events, the court determines if the delay is above or below the presumptive ceilings (generally 18 months for matters proceeding in the Ontario Court of Justice and 30 months for matters proceeding in the Superior Court of Justice).[5]

    [21]      If the delay is above the presumptive ceiling, the Crown may rebut the presumption that the delay is unreasonable by showing the presence of exceptional circumstances.[6]

    [22]      The Crown cannot simply point to past exceptional circumstances to excuse delay but must also show that it took reasonable steps to avoid the delay before the presumptive ceiling was reached. The Crown does not have to be successful at those steps. It only has to show that it was being proactive in trying to minimize the delay.[7]

    [23]      Complexity of a case is assessed by the nature of the evidence or the nature of the issues that must be litigated and not simply the nature of the charge.[8]

    [24]      Furthermore, in Jordan, the Supreme Court stated, “Proceedings jointly against multiple co-accused, so long as it is in the interest of justice to do so, may also impact the complexity of the case.”[9] (emphasis added)

    [25]      In Cody the Supreme Court clarified that delay caused by ‘discrete events’ are to be deducted mathematically from the overall delay, whereas the ‘complexity’ of a case is more of a qualitative assessment that affects what constitutes reasonable delay in any given case.[10]

    [26]      In Gopie, the Court of Appeal rejected the Crown’s argument that delay by one defendant in a multi-defendant prosecution should be attributed to all. The Court concluded that the weight of jurisprudence favoured an ‘individualized approach’ in assessing delay. In other words, when assessing delay in multi-defendant trials, the court must look at the delay caused by the actual defendant and refuse to impute delay caused by other co-defendants.[11]

    [27]      However, what constitutes ‘reasonable delay’ in a complex case is not rigidly governed by the presumptive ceilings.[12] What is reasonable delay, “…falls well within the expertise of a trial judge.”[13] A holistic approach must be taken.

    Application of the Law to the Facts

    [28]      The defence takes the position that there were no exceptional circumstances in this case. I disagree. In Jordan the Supreme Court used multi-defendant prosecutions as an example of exceptional circumstances.[14]

    [29]      In this case, the original trial dates offered by the court were November through to December 2025. That would have meant a delay of 17 months and 25 days – just under the presumptive ceiling. Though S.S.’ counsel was available, Z.D.’s counsel was unavailable until March of 2026. Z.D.’s counsel’s unavailability, combined with the unavailability of R.I.’s counsel caused the anticipated delay to grow to just over 21 months. Faced with this delay, the Crown acted proactively by prioritizing the matter and attempting to secure earlier dates. The Crown and the court offered February and March 2025 dates – a full year before the original trial dates. Had all the defence accepted those dates the total delay from the swearing of the Information to the end of the trial would have been 9 months. However, the four defence counsel were not all available until November and December of 2025, thus leading to a delay of 18 months and 11 days.  

    [30]      This is not a case in which the Crown or the court as an institution failed to appreciate the delay. They moved proactively before the delay to provide a 10-day trial within 9 months of the defendants’ arrest. The Crown had no control over who the defendants hired. The fact that one of the defendant’s lawyers had a very busy schedule and was unwilling to change his commitments to prioritize this matter is outside of the Crown’s power.  

    [31]      Counsel for Z.D. and S.S. concede they did not apply for severance because they knew it would not be granted. Implicit in their concession is the admission that the Crown’s decision to prosecute the defendants jointly was in the interest of justice.

    [32]      The Supreme Court said in Vassell, “[i]n many cases, delay caused by proceeding against multiple co-accused must be accepted as a fact of life and must be considered in deciding what constitutes a reasonable time for trial.”[15] Justice Fairburn, as she then was, stated it aptly in Ny, “[T]he concept of “reasonable time” within s. 11(b) may fluctuate depending on whether accused are standing alone or together. Provided it is in the interest of justice to proceed jointly, delay above the ceiling may reflect the realities of life in a joint trial and transform an otherwise unreasonable delay into a reasonable one.”[16]

    [33]      In cases where the delay is caused because of the complexity of the case, there is no obligation for the court to deduct periods to bring the matter under the presumptive ceilings. In Gopie, after deducting the delay attributable to the defence and to discrete events, the Court of Appeal upheld a refusal to stay proceedings that exceeded the presumptive ceilings by 5.5 and 2.5 months for each co-accused after appropriate deductions had been made.[17]

    [34]      In this case, I find the Crown was proactive in attempting to secure earlier dates, and that it was in the interest of justice to proceed jointly. I conclude that the delay of 18 months and 11 days to be reasonable in the circumstances because of the complexity in prosecuting multiple co-defendants and accommodating the schedules of their counsel.  

    [35]      I find that the Applicants have not shown that their section 11(b) Charter rights have been violated and dismiss the Application.
