    Ruling on Long-Term Offender Application and Sentencing Disposition

    WARNING:

    This ruling involves reference to matters governed by a court ordered publication ban pursuant to s.486.5 of the Criminal Code.

    Crown: Patricia Garcia

    Defence: Joel Prajs

    Libman J.: -

    Overview

    [1] The accused has entered guilty pleas to the following offences: two counts of possessing child pornography; two counts of accessing child pornography, failing to comply with a release order, and failing to comply with a probation order.

    [2] The Crown has applied under s.753.1 of the Criminal Code for a finding that the accused be declared a long-term offender, and placed on a 10-year long-term supervision order. She additionally seeks that he be sentenced to 8 years’ imprisonment, plus ancillary orders, including: a lifetime prohibition order under s.161, a weapons prohibition order under s.109 for life, a DNA order (primary) and lifetime SOIRA order.

    [3] Defence counsel opposes the imposition of the long-term supervision order, and submits that Mr. Green is in a time-served position, due to the considerable length of his pre-trial custody, and the circumstances in which he has served it. He also takes issue with the length and breadth of the s.161 prohibition order. The defence submits that the defendant’s behaviour in the community can be adequately managed by a probation order, and that any additional restriction on his liberty is not warranted.

    Circumstances of Offences and Offender

    [4] Mr. Green was born on […], 1980. He will shortly be 45 years old. He is a Canadian citizen.

    [5] Mr. Green has been previously convicted and sentenced for child pornography offences.

    [6] His first conviction occurred when he was 37 years old. He had no previous criminal record at the time. This sentencing hearing took place before Justice Borenstein at the Old City Hall court in Toronto, and concluded at College Park. I have been provided with transcripts of these proceedings.

    [7] On May 5, 2017, he entered pleas of guilty before Justice Borenstein to possessing child pornography and making available child pornography, as well as possession of crystal methamphetamine. The offence dates for the pornography offences spanned May 2012 to August 2016.

    [8] The majority of the child pornography in Mr. Green’s home was described as being “hardcore in nature”. It involved mostly male children under the age of 12 years. The running time of all of the unique child pornography videos in his collection was at least 123 hours in length. There were 2,662 unique child pornography videos in total. His laptop contained 953 unique images.

    [9] Sentencing was imposed on February 5, 2018. An expert psychiatric report from Dr. Hy Bloom, written in 2017, was put before the court. Dr. Bloom opined that the principal risk factors for Mr. Green were his attraction to a youthful male physique and his drug use. The Doctor commented on the link between the use of crystal methamphetamine and offenders who possess child pornography. Mr. Green had been found in bed with crystal methamphetamine at the time of his arrest. Dr. Bloom was of the opinion that the accused represented a low risk to re-offend if he engaged in sex offender and drug counselling and abstained from using crystal methamphetamine and other street drugs.

    [10] Justice Borenstein imposed concurrent sentences of 15 months in jail in addition to the 7 days of pretrial custody served by Mr. Green on the child pornography charges. A fine of $500.00 was imposed for the drug offence. The Court also placed him on the maximum period of 3 years probation. Ancillary orders included: DNA order, a lifetime SOIRA order. The Court declined to make an order under s.161. Justice Borenstein also made a recommendation that Mr. Green serve his sentence at Brampton OCI, where he subsequently did serve his sentence before being released on December 5, 2018, and placed on probation for three years.

    [11] Thereafter, Mr. Green was arrested on the charges, which come before me, on June 29, 2021. He has been incarcerated at the Toronto South Detention Centre (TSDC) since that date. As of today’s date (April 17, 2025), that is a total of 1,374 days, or 45.8 months, being 3.8 years. Applying the pre-trial custody credit of 1.5:1 under s.719(3.1), Mr. Green is credited with 2,061 days of pre-trial custody, or 68.7 months, being 5.7 years. His counsel submits that he is entitled to enhanced pre-trial credit in addition to that; the Crown differs as to what that amount, if any, should be.

    [12] On July 28, 2020, a search warrant had been executed at the defendant’s home (Mega.nz investigation), which resulted in 867 images of child pornography being located. Mega is a cloud storage and file hosting service offered by Mega Limited, an Auckland, New Zealand-based company. Mega can be used to share files with others by the use of links and adding other Mega users to a user’s contact list for easy drop and drag sharing. Mr. Green created a Mega user account on June 13, 2019.

    [13] There were recovered on 3 of Mr. Green’s electronic devices: an Apple ipad (51 total images), an Apple iphone (272 images), and HP Laptop (536 total images). The images included both prepubescent females and males. The majority of the images captured were male children, between 5-12 years old. The dominant focus of the images were the genitals of the child. 3 images included babies involved in sex acts with adults. Child pornography videos were also recovered on these three electronic devices. The majority of these videos captured male children, being predominantly 5-12 years old. There were 78 videos in total. Mr. Green was arrested for these offences in July 2020, and released on bail the following day. According to the Information, the defendant appeared in court on July 28, 2020; on July 29, 2020, he was released on bail in the amount of $5,000.00, with two sureties.

    [14] A second search of a device in Mr. Green’s possession (Google investigation) was subsequently executed, in June, 2021. A total of 472 images were categorized as child pornography on an Apple iphone. The images were of both prepubescent females and males, the majority of the images captured male children, who were predominantly between 5-12 years old. 3 images involved babies involved in sex acts with adults, and 3 images involved images taken while the child appeared to be sleeping. There were also 233 videos of child pornography. The majority of the videos captured male children. The children were predominantly between 5 and 12 years old. The videos included children masturbating, sexual acts between children, and sexual acts with adults, such as an adult placing his penis in the mouth of a child. Upon being arrested for these offences, Mr. Green has remained detained in custody, awaiting sentencing and the outcome of these proceedings.

    [15] The images and videos, in common on both arrests, depicted children masturbating, vaginal and anal penetration by adult males of a child, sexual acts between children, and adult fondling and digitally penetrating a child anally/vaginally.

    [16] A representative sample of the contents of these two sets of charges may be found in the Report of Detective Alexa (Exhibit 13).

    [17] The summary of the 2020 arrest is: Image Categorization Report (Exhibit 9); Video Categorization Report (Exhibit 10).

    [18] The summary of the 2021 arrest is: Image Categorization Report (Exhibit 11); Video categorization Report (Exhibit 12).

    [19] Detective Alexa could not speak of the contents of the entire collection of images and videos seized from Mr. Green, but did comment that other sexual acts with adults were not in the collection.

    [20] Mr. Green entered guilty pleas to 6 indictable offences before me on February 21, 2023. The child pornography offences were: possession of child pornography and accessing child pornography, the offence dates spanning July 14 2019 to July 28,2020. The breach of probation count was for accessing pornographic websites while bound by Justice Borenstein’s 3-year probation order, imposed on February 5, 2018. A second set of possession child pornography and accessing child pornography charges contained offence dates of June 13, 2020 to June 29, 2021. The breach of release offence covered this period, the breach being not to possess images or videos of persons under the age of 18-years unless they were family photos or displayed in your home that did not display nudity.

    [21] On September 3, 2024, the Crown tendered written notice of its intention, to have Mr. Green declared a long-term offender, and detaining him in a penitentiary for a determinate period of incarceration, followed by a long-term supervision order, pursuant to s.753.1 of the Criminal Code. The hearing of evidence commenced on September 13, 2024, and took place over four days, concluding with counsels’ submissions on February 28, 2025.

    Evidence on Application

    [22] In addition to Detective Alexa, the officer-in-charge, the Crown called witnesses from the provincial and federal correctional systems to give evidence, along with Dr. Iosif, who provided a psychiatric assessment of Mr. Green.

    [23] Brad Tamcsu gave evidence on behalf of the Ontario Ministry of the Solicitor General. Exhibit 6 is an Overview of Institutional Services and Community Correctional Services for Dangerous Offender/Long-Term Offender Hearings. Mr. Tamcsu has been employed with the Ministry of Solicitor General for 30 years, and has been Superintendent at the Ontario Correctional Institute.

    [24] Provincial programs are in place to provide programing for sexual offenders. These programs (the Ontario Correctional Institute in Brampton and St Lawrence Valley Correctional and Treatment Centre in Ottawa) include treatment designed for offenders who commit sexual offences with children. A number of considerations are taken into account when considering whether to admit offenders into such programs, including classification requirements, motivation level and sentence length. Generally a sentence of at least 9 months is required for admission to OCI. Space is also quite limited at these institutions. As of October, 2024, there were 68 beds at OCI. In order to gain admission to these programs where counselling is provided, the offender must take accountability for their actions, display motivation for receiving treatment, and have clinical and institutional support for their application. Stop sexually offending is one such program specifically offered by the Ontario Correctional Services as a rehabilitative or treatment program.

    [25] These provincial programs are aimed for offenders who are serving sentences within provincial reformatories. They are not geared for preparing inmates to serve sentences in the federal penitentiary, although it would give them a sense as to what to expect. Neither are there specific provincial programs specifically designed for offenders who are placed on long-term supervision orders. Once the offender is within the community, the offender is supervised by a probation and parole officer. Core rehabilitative services include the target area of sexual offending. However, this would not include supervision of an offender who is placed on a long-term supervision order, as that form of release is overseen by Correctional Services Canada, within the federal system. Hence, Mr. Tamcsu explained that a probation officer could not supervise a condition imposed in a long-term supervision order, such as a residency condition, or monitor access and use of narcotics or the internet. Probation officers also have their own caseloads to monitor, which range between 20 to 40 cases, and vary from intense to basic streams. He did agree, though, with the defence suggestion that if a person in a provincial institution was receiving treatment for substance use, this would at least help prepare them for such a term being imposed in a long-term supervision order.

    [26] The witness who testified on behalf of the federal Correctional Service Canada (CSC) was Erin Brennan, a parole officer supervisor and employee of CSC for 20 years. She has supervised offenders on long-term supervision orders and worked out of the Keele Correctional Centre where offenders are also supervised. CSC administers post-sentence supervision of offenders with long-term supervision orders for up to 10 years. For high-risk offenders in the federal penitentiary system, which requires a minimum sentence of 2-years, a correctional plan is formulated by the assessing parole officer, which commences upon the person’s intake assessment. An offender who receives a sentence in the provincial system of less than 2 years and is placed on a long-term supervision offender would be supervised by conditions imposed by the parole board, but the intake process would be compressed, as the offender had not entered the federal system previously.

    [27] The federal system offers programs for sexual offenders. This includes a specialized sex offender assessment. These programs are described in Exhibit 8 CSC Community Supervision Information Package. Programing is offered based on high intensity or moderate intensity, once the offender is placed within their federal institution, which may be in Ontario, or anywhere across Canada. No distinction is made based on whether the offences were committed “hands on” or through accessing sexually explicit materials. Community programming is also offered to offenders who are placed on long-term supervision orders once they are released into the community. In Toronto, this includes programing through the Centre for Addiction and Mental Health (CAMH). Substance use programing is offered in the same way. There is a community correctional centre in Toronto, located on Keele Street.

    [28] Dr. Alina Iosif is a forensic psychiatrist and lecturer at the University of Toronto. She graduated from medical school in 2001 and is currently a staff psychiatrist at CAMH. Her Curriculum Vitae (CV) is Exhibit 3. Her expertise was admitted on consent. Dr. Iosif was the first witness called by the Crown. She spent six hours interviewing Mr. Green via videoconference, on January 15 and 22, 2024. Her expert report is Exhibit 4 in the proceedings. It is dated March 25, 2024. In preparing her report, she also reviewed disclosure materials consisting of the defendant’s criminal record and transcripts, custodial records, community supervision records, medical records, psychiatric and treatment records, CRA and ODSP records, and education records. She additionally spoke to Mr. Green’s mother, Collen Comer, who lives in Huntsville. Mr. Green lived with her while he was awaiting sentencing between 2016 and 2018, for a period of 18 months.

    [29] Dr. Iosif testified that Mr. Green had a diagnosis as a child of attention deficit hyperactivity disorder (ADHD). His mother told her that he was easily bored and hyper as a child. He also suffers from substance use, which was not an issue when he lived with his mother, but resumed upon his return to Toronto. According to Dr. Tania Stirpe, who interviewed Mr. Green at the request of the defence (and whose report is dated September 8, 2024, although not marked as an exhibit in the proceedings), Ms. Comer told Dr. Stirpe that she no longer has room for the defendant to live with her on a long-term basis. However, they remain in contact and have a good, close relationship.

    [30] The defendant completed high school and college and obtained employment. However, he has been under disability payments since 2017. This resulted from Mr. Green suffering a stroke due to head injuries when he was the victim of a home invasion. His education and work history demonstrate, the doctor testified, that he can occupy his time in a constructive fashion and structured activity, which is risk reducing from a psychiatric perspective.

    [31] Mr. Green has had several intimate relationships. They have all been with men. His most significant relationship was with Robin Swalm, with whom he was in a relationship since 2008. It ended when he was recently incarcerated. Mr. Swalm is 8 years younger; he is slim and tall, without body hair. This is the body type favoured by Mr. Green. The two of them engaged in substance use together; they also watched pornography together, although not the same type of pornography that is the relevant to the doctor’s assessment. While this relationship is a protective factor, it also enabled his offending behaviour. The two of them would use substances together for long periods of time, would be intoxicated for long periods of time, and would engage in this type of hypersexual and pursuit of different types of pornography together for extended periods of time.

    [32] The defendant told Dr. Iosif that he was bisexual, however this was not borne out by his history. He had only exclusively homosexual relationships. He had an overwhelmingly androphilic interest as manifested over his lifetime, hence women who interested him were bodybuilders, who were similar in body type to males. He characterized his sex drive as low unless he was using substances; he would engage in masturbation mostly when he was intoxicated and for long periods of time. He would stay sleepless for days on end, during which he would watch pornography and masturbate almost the entire time. He estimated this to be for approximately 20 hours daily. Mr. Green was adamant that he did not have a sexual interest in children or adolescents. However, he admitted to being interested in images of adolescent boys in the pornography he was watching. This consisted of, in his words, “a tall, smooth, youthful, blonde hair, blue-eyed young man”. By file information, his report upon intake at OCI, he began using crystal meth and watching child pornography in 2012. At that time, he was introduced to child pornography by his drug dealer. To Dr. Iosif, he said this started in 2014. The former would indicate that Mr. Green’s interest in child pornography began before his stroke, so it is tied more to an organic preference, as opposed to somehow originating from a catastrophic brain event.

    [33] Mr. Green told Dr. Iosif that as part of his sharing software he was also involved with chats. He stated that overwhelmingly he was interested in boys and mostly adolescent boys, that he was not interested in younger children and girls, although the child pornography he was watching pertained to both sexes and ages from infancy to adolescence. He informed Dr. Iosif that he was intoxicated with stimulants, specifically crystal meth, when he was looking at these images, and that the images consisted of children having sexual contacts with adults or other children. His interest, however, was in adolescent boys. The doctor also stated that clearly his level of interest that prompted him to keep such materials, and retrieve them, occurred when he was no longer intoxicated.

    [34] The defendant did understand that these children were victimized and he did not have their consent to view the materials. He understood about the victimization of children. However, his understanding and empathy and pro-social makeup was not enough to prevent him from accessing such materials. This is very relevant to risk assessment: it speaks to the strength of his interest and draw to these images, even in the face of the legal sanctions in place and his being in a relationship with a man. Mr. Green told the doctor collecting child pornography was “almost compulsive for him”.

    [35] At the time the defendant committed the offences before the court he was on bail. He also had a probation officer. In addition, he had been referred to CAMH for the sexual behaviour clinic and also for substance relapse prevention programing. He was also seeing Dr. Pallandi at the Riverdale Clinic for psychiatric care. Despite access to all these resources, the defendant committed the offences for which he is now incarcerated. Mr. Green told the doctor that he needed more programing to abstain from substances; he believe that being sober or abstinent from illicit substances would automatically take care of the issue of child pornography. Dr. Iosif disagreed, however, that recovery from addiction would be sufficient. Previous attendance at substance relapse programming at CAMH revealed he was removed from the group due to seldom attending. He therefore required risk management in a more structured way,

    [36] Dr. Iosif had access to Dr. Bloom’s 2017 assessment which was put before Justice Borenstein. Dr. Bloom had found Mr. Green to be evasive. His phallometric testing was inconclusive, and revealed a contra sign. The results of his response to men being equally strong as his response to women was not compatible with his history.

    [37] Upon his admission to OCI, the defendant was given ADHD medications, and did well on them. Dr. Iosif commented that methamphetamines are themselves stimulants. An individual with a history of methamphetamines would not therefore be given ADHD medications, as this would lead to similar symptoms and a high that is produced from consuming crystal meth. In the defendant’s case, though, the ADHD was so severe and impairing that treatment with ADHD medications could not be avoided.

    [38] Dr. Pallandi, who had been treating Mr. Green following his release from OCI, believed his ADHD was severe and needed treatment. He was also available for psychiatric care. Mr. Green stated to Dr. Iosif that he would be willing to participate in psychological programing related to sex offending and substance abuse, although he was hoping not to be sentenced to any additional period of imprisonment. He recognized that he needed supports so that he would not engage in viewing child pornography again. He was confident that he could control himself, and find future employment. He was also willing to go to an aftercare group at CAMH, and attend counselling at the Sherbourne Centre.

    [39] With respect to the offences before the Court, the defendant described a pattern of use where he would be abstinent from substances and child pornography for approximately three days, and then he would use crystal meth and child pornography for four days; sometimes he would do this for as long as seven days at a time. His use of material and masturbation would comprise 20 hours out of 24. He was intoxicated with crystal meth and could not sleep because of it. He stayed awake for three, four days at a time. During this time he almost exclusively engaged in sexual activity, much of which had to do with watching child pornography. Dr. Iosif believed the size of his collection was directly proportionate to his interest in such images. This was not a situation where he happened upon some images; his collection spanned over a hundred hours and showed videos and pictures of children from infancy to adolescence engaged in various explicit sexual acts with other children or adults, often of a violent nature. She added, viewing violent child pornography material is concerning from a risk management perspective: it is not just an issue of sexual deviance, but also one of violence and the degree to which the children exposed to these kind of actions are harmed and violated during their production.

    [40] Mr. Green admitted to Dr. Iosif that his attraction was to adolescent boys. However, she noted that pedophilia is rarely a preference that is so acutely honed to just one age group; it tends to be a lot more diffuse or amorphous in terms of the ages and the genders that one has an interest in. Hence, one cannot rest on the defendant’s expressed preference for adolescent boys and say that all other children are safe otherwise. Ultimately, the materials that the defendant was watching and collecting included other ages and other genders. As he committed these offences while on probation and bail, this indicated that he could not help himself now or in the future without further intervention. In other words, he cannot control himself. While he knows crystal meth is bad for him, and it leads to bad things, and watching child pornography is also bad for him, as it will lead to being incarcerated and losing his relationship and employment, the pedophilic preference, and his addiction issues, are such that he cannot overcome them on his own. His risk in the future will therefore be driven by these two variables.

    [41] The defendant was released from OCI in December of 2018. He returned to Toronto at this point. By June of 2019 he was using substances and watching child pornography again. He admitted to his probation officer in August of 2020 that he had been using crystal meth for several months. Despite the supports in place for him, he had reverted to his usual behaviours within a period of approximately six months.

    [42] Phallometric testing was done on the defendant at CAMH on February 8, 2024. This testing indicates sexual preference. His responses were similar to his responses in 2017 at the time of Dr. Bloom’s report, which indicated a contra sign test interference. Stated simply, Mr. Green cannot have the same interest in women as he does in men, as this is contrary to his own stated preference. In addition, the phallometric testing does not replicate his use of methamphetamines, as it is not conducted with any chemical aids. A not indicative finding does not mean that a person does not have pedophilia. A phallometric testing does not rule out the presence of a paraphilia; it can only rule in the presence of a paraphilia. A non-indicative test simply means that a test is inconclusive.

    [43] In the case of Mr. Green, while one cannot make a formal diagnosis of pedophilia in this case because of certain information that was lacking, such as phallometric testing and the defendant’s stated preferences, there was no other reasonable medical or psychiatric explanation for his behaviour with regards to child pornography than the diagnosis of pedophilia. That is, there was no alternative reason why he would behave the way that he does. What fuels and prompts Mr. Green’s behaviour is not just substance use, but his underlying sexual preference, which is one for children.

    [44] With respect to risk assessment, Dr. Iosif stated that for offenders who do not have hands-on offences, only online child exploitation material offences, the only actuarial risk tool is the psychopathy checklist revised (the PCL-R). The psychopathy checklist revised is comprise of 20 items, each one of which is scored from 0 to 2. The defendant scored a total of 14 points, which is a low moderate score. Such a score is in keeping with his generally pro-social lifestyle and personality and behavioural controls in other ways. This score generates a prediction of risk that Mr. Green would do well with supervision. That said, his history was such that he actually, while being supervised, went behind those supports and offended surreptitiously without their knowledge.

    [45] A clinical checklist of risk factors for a person, HCR-20 version 3, (historical, clinical and risk management components) was also employed. It is more of a checklist of things that would drive risk; it does not produce a score. In the defendant’s case. Historical items of relevance included substance use problems and prior supervision failure; clinical items of concern included impulsivity and unresponsiveness to treatment. If he was released into the community without further intervention, he is likely to have further problems. His risk on the instrument was therefore assessed as moderate. Protective factors, such as support from his mother, was not very extensive in his case. If he had fewer supports, less empathy, if something happened to his cognitive function, his risk would go up.

    [46] Dr. Iosif used the CPORT, which is a child pornography offender risk tool. It measures the degree to which someone has a pedophilic preference and the risk associated with it based on the materials they are viewing. The defendant scored a three out of a possible seven points on this instrument, which suggested a moderate likelihood of any sexual recidivism. However, this tool does not take into account the offender’s drug use, nor does it relate exclusively to child pornography offending in particular. This tool also does not take into account the risk of re-offence if the offender were to relapse.

    [47] CASIC, another tool, was employed. It measures behaviours and correlates them to admitted sexual interest in children. The defendant’s child pornography included videos but not stories; his interest in child pornography spanned a decade.

    [48] Having regard to the defendant’s overall risk for recidivism, the doctor opined that his risk for recidivism is low generally, moving to moderate for child pornography offences. Moreover, if he were to be released and relapsed to stimulant use by using methamphetamines, his risk for reoffending by accessing or possessing child sexual abuse material would be high, and very likely to occur. The likelihood of relapse on the part of the defendant without intensive treatment being offered and a structured environment outside of a custodial setting would be high.

    [49] Dr. Iosif also addressed the factor of burnout. Known as an aging factor, it has been noted to occur for sex offenders after the fourth decade of life. There is a drop in the frequency of sex offending for such individuals. However, it does not happen to everyone, and it happens less in individuals who suffer from pedophilia as opposed to other deviant sexual preferences. It is even less relevant to child pornography offences as they do not necessitate any type of physical strength or stamina, to engage in. Therefore, individuals like Mr. Green, who is currently 44 years old, and who offend only by way of child pornography, are less likely to experience burnout than other sex offenders.

    [50] Consequently, if he were released into the community, aged 44, with no treatment of any necessary intensity in a custodial setting and simply put on probation, and even had access to a cell phone with wifi, the risk of reoffending by consuming child pornography would be very high. This would be a situation that is very similar to what has happened in the past.

    [51] Dr. Iosif also addressed criminogenic variables, specifically deficient coping mechanisms. She explained that for many individuals, when they encounter a stressful situation, they may resort to coping mechanisms that are maladaptive, such as the use of illicit substances. This is what the defendant has done. When he is bored or has nothing to do, he will revert to using substances and engaging in sexual behaviour, including watching child pornography. Conversely, having work and being abstinent from substances and being in a relationship that does not trigger such issues, would be  coping mechanisms that would be to his advantage.

    [52] Risk management for Mr. Green, would therefore target criminogenic variables specifically for him. Most importantly, he needs to have programming directed specifically ay his sexual behaviour and underlying sexual preference for children. He would also have to have programming directed at managing and leading to abstinence from all illicit substances, as well as programming that develops his coping mechanisms, his ability to control himself, to manage these emotions. It would be important for him to engage in structured activities, such as working or volunteering, and to consider who he is in a relationship with, and whether these people have children. It was apparent that he also needed external supervision, given that the supervision he had in the past, probation and bail and psychiatric treatment, was not sufficient to manage his risk, or even to catch him while he engaged in his offending behaviour. The supervision he required, moving forward, must be more intense, more stringent and more intrusive, such as, for example, taking urine samples during home visits to determine if he was using substances. In short, he required “something above and beyond what was offered by probation and bail”. Given that he reoffended while on probation, this would not be suitable for such supervision in the future. Mr. Green was planning to return to Toronto, although there were no specific plans as to where he would be staying or what he would be doing. While he remained in a custodial setting, higher intensity programming would be required.

    [53] Dr. Iosif also addressed the use of pharmacological options for Mr. Green. While there are sex drive reducing medications that are available, the value of such medications for one who has only online offending was questionable, in her opinion. Sex drive reducing medications have more of a physiological effect on a person; it does not remove the interest in children. What it does remove is the physical ability to be able to perform in sexual situations to pursue, for example, children in the community. This, however, was not Mr. Green’s case. She did agree, though, with Dr. Stirpe’s recommendation that he should be placed in a high-intensity substance use program.

    [54] The recommendations of Dr. Iosif were that Mr. Green engage in psychotherapy, specifically designed for child pornography offenders and sexual behaviour relapse. He could receive such high-intensity programming through Correctional Services Canada. She opined that if he participated in such programming and was thereafter released to a structured setting and supervised under a long-term supervision order where substance use could be monitored, intimate partners could be notified of his history, access to electronic devices and access to children monitored as well, all of which could be accomplished in a residential setting at a community correctional centre or approved residence by CSC, such a scenario would likely address his likelihood of recidivism, and make him a potentially successful candidate for therapy and for programming.

    [55] Given the likelihood of a pedophilia diagnosis, there will never be a time when Mr. Green will not have a pedophilic sexual interest.     Analysis

    A Denunciatory Penitentiary Sentence is Warranted

    [87] Child pornography offences are “extremely serious and are met with sentences that emphasize the sentencing principles of denunciation (Criminal Code, s.718(a)) and general deterrence (Criminal Code, s.718(b))”: R v McCaw, para. 19.

    [88] This approach to sentencing offenders like Mr. Green must therefore inform the disposition I impose.

    [89] In the Supreme Court of Canada’s seminal decision in Friesen, the Court observed:

    Protecting children from wrongful exploitation and harm is the overarching objective of the legislative scheme of sexual offences against children in the Criminal Code. (Friesen, para. 42)

    [90] The fact that the victim is a child, in other words, increases the degree of responsibility of the offender: Friesen, para. 90.

    [91] Parliament has additionally made it clear through its enactment of s.718.01 of the Criminal Code that emphasis is to be given to the sentencing principles of denunciation and deterrence in the case of offences involving child abuse: see Friesen, para. 101.

    [92] Applying the “message” from Friesen, para. 114, then, mid-single digit penitentiary terms for sexual offences against children are “normal”; upper-single digit and double-digit penitentiary terms are neither “rare or exceptional circumstances”.

    [93] In R v Pike, 2024 ONCA 608, Chief Justice Tulloch, on behalf of the unanimous court, applied the child-centred approach from R v Friesen to child pornography offences. The Court itemized the distinct wrongs and harms which flow from the possession offence. This includes consideration that individuals who possess child pornography violate the dignity of children: Pike, para. 147.

    [94] Perpetrators like Mr. Green who possess images of abused and exploited children “directly invade children’s privacy”: Pike, para. 148. This inflicts severe emotional harm on children: Pike, para. 149. It also creates and fuels a market for child exploitation (Pike, para, 151), and incites others to facilitate and commit other sexual offences against children: Pike, para. 152. Finally, people who possess child pornography inflict “pernicious messages that attack children’s humanity and equality”: Pike, para. 154.

    [95] In sum, possession of child pornography is “a grave offence”: Pike, para. 157. It calls for an increase in sentencing by raising the 3.5 to 4 year upper end of the range for possession of child pornography to 5 years. This sentencing range is warranted, as it empowers sentencing judges to respond to the “’staggering and systemic nature’ of the wrongs and harms that people who possess child pornography can cause in cases involving numerous victims”: Pike, para. 177.

    [96] It follows that for a repeat offender like Mr. Green, penitentiary terms for his 2020 and 2021 child pornography set of charges should each attract respective sentences in the 5-year range. Indeed, I consider such a sentence to be at the lower end of the range. Mr. Green had previously served a 15-month sentence for the same type of offence, and was on both probation and bail at the time of the commission of these further child pornography offences. The size of his collection was not insignificant; the duration of the offences was several years. The number of victims, in the hundreds; the harm and damage inflicted on them, incalculable.

    [97] In all of the circumstances, I am of the respectful opinion that a total sentence of 12 years’ imprisonment is warranted – prior to considering the principle of totality, and apportioning credit for Mr. Green’s period of pre-trial custody at TSDC.

    [98] I arrive at the quantum of 12 years’ imprisonment as follows:

    - possession of child pornography (July 14, 2019 – July 28, 2020): 5 years’ imprisonment;

    - accessing child pornography (July 14, 2019 – July 28, 2020): 5 years’ imprisonment, concurrent;

    - possession of child pornography (June 13, 2020 – June 29, 2021): 5 years’ imprisonment, consecutive;

    - accessing child pornography (June 13, 2020) – June 29, 2021): 5 years’ imprisonment, concurrent;

    - breach of probation order: 1 year consecutive;

    - breach of release order: 1 year consecutive.

    Applying the Principle of Totality

    [99] While I have imposed concurrent sentences on the set of possession and accessing child pornography offences that cover the same time period, this reflects the fact that the delict for these offences contains an element of overlap, in that both offences occurred over the same time period, and were committed at the same time as one another. It is for this reason that concurrent sentences were imposed in respect of two of the six counts to which Mr. Green has entered guilty pleas.

    [100] What I have yet to consider, however, is the principle of totality, which requires as a sentencing principle that where consecutive sentences are imposed, the combined sentence should not be “unduly long or harsh”: Criminal Code, s.718.2(c).

    [101] According to this totality principle, a trial judge who imposes consecutive sentences for multiple offences is to ensure that the cumulative sentence does not exceed the overall culpability of the offender: see R v M. (C.A.), 1996 CanLII 230 (SCC), [1996] 1 S.C.R. 500.

    [102] In this case, it is important to understand, as pointed out by the Supreme Court of Canada, in R. v M. (L.), 2008 SCC 31, at para. 31, the “close relationship” between the offences, the “overall situation they gave rise to and the need to impose a global sentence suited to that situation”. In M. (L.), the offence of sexual assault was closely connected with three other offences: making, possessing and distributing child pornography. As LeBel J. explained, “each aspect of the offender’s conduct could be considered only in light of all of these charges, viewed as a whole”: M. (L.), para. 31.

    [103] Put another way, then, although the totality principle must always be considered when sentencing an offender for multiple offences, the sentence being imposed “cannot thereby become inadequate to properly recognize the overall gravity of this conduct that victimizes children in the most horrendous way”: see R. v F. (D.G.), 2010 ONCA  27, at para. 27.

    [104] Applying the principle of totality to Mr. Green’s offences, I am the respective opinion that a total sentence of 12 years’ imprisonment would be unduly long. I say this, notwithstanding that the two separate counts of child pornography offences attracting 5-year sentences each is well within the increased sentencing range mandated by Friesen and Pike, respectively.

    [105] Consecutive sentences are also warranted on the fail to comply with probation and release order charges, as these are highly aggravating factors. Mr. Green was bound by a probation order while he committed the first set of child pornography offences; he was bound by both the probation order and release order when he committed the second set of child pornography offences. Concurrent sentences are not warranted for these breach offences. There must not be a “free-ride” for these aggravating factors of committing further – and identical – offences while subject to court orders, designed to prevent the commission of same.

    [106] In order to give effect to the totality principle, while at the same time imposing a sentence that reflects the fundamental principle of proportionality pursuant to s.718.1 of the Criminal Code, I sentence Mr. Green to a total sentence of 8 years’ imprisonment. I do so by imposing sentences as follows:

    - possession of child pornography (July 14, 2019 – July 28, 2020): 3.5 years’ imprisonment;

    - accessing child pornography (July 14, 2019 – July 28, 2020): 3.5 years’ imprisonment, concurrent;

    - possession of child pornography (June 13, 2020 – June 29, 2021): 3.5 years’ imprisonment, consecutive;

    - accessing child pornography (June 13, 2020) – June 29, 2021): 3.5 years’ imprisonment, concurrent;

    - breach of probation order: 6 months’ imprisonment, consecutive;

    - breach of release order: 6 months’ imprisonment, consecutive.

    Credit for Pre-Trial Custody

    [107] The remaining consideration in determining the sentence to be imposed is the amount of pre-trial custody to apportion in this case, and to then reduce it from the period of 8-years’ imprisonment that I have determined is a fit and just sentence for the defendant.

    [108] Section 719(3.1) of the Criminal Code is the starting point of the analysis. It authorizes the granting of credit of 1.5 days for each day spent in custody.

    [109] Accordingly, Mr. Green is entitled to credit on at least this basis, the loss of early release, without anything more: R v Summers, 2014 SCC 26.

    [110] As noted earlier, he has been in custody from the time of his arrest on June 29, 2021, to today’s date, April 17, 2025, a total of 1,374 days or 45.8 months (3.8 years). Applying the pre-trial custody credit of 1.5:1 under s.719(3.1), Mr. Green is credited with 2,061 days of pre-trial custody, or 68.7 months, being 5.7 years.

    [111] The defence seeks additional credit on the basis of R v Duncan, due to the conditions of his pre-trial detention at TSDC, which are notoriously deplorable. In addition to having experienced lock downs, Covid outbreaks and triple bunking, Mr. Green has additionally suffered physical and psychological upset due to his disability from his stroke. He was also assaulted by other inmates, a number of times.

    [112] There is no question, then, that enhanced credit is warranted in this case. The more difficult question is what exactly that should be.

    [113] The Ontario Court of Appeal has made it clear that “Duncan” credit does not lend itself to a mathematical formula in the same way as “Summers” credit. And the two are not the same, and should not be conflated. The former reflects “particularly difficult and punitive presentence custody conditions”; the latter already takes into account “the difficult and restrictive circumstances offenders often encounter during pretrial custody”: R v Marshall, 2021 ONCA 344, at para. 50.

    [114] Put another way, the “Summers” credit is calculated to “identify and deduct from the appropriate sentence the amount of sentence the accused has effectively served by virtue of the pretrial incarceration”: R v Marshall, para. 51. It is statutorily capped at 1:5 days to 1 day; it is not a mitigating factor. Conversely, the “Duncan” credit is not a deduction from an otherwise appropriate sentence, but one of the factors to be taken into account in the determination of an appropriate sentence: R v Marshall, para. 52.

    [115] A specific number of days or months may be given as “Duncan” credit. However, with respect, such credit does not lend itself to double or triple credit, as proposed by defence counsel. I note, further, the triple credit cases relied on by the defence, such as Fermah, pre-date the Court of Appeal’s decision in R v Marshall. Such a credit calculation would risk “skewing” the calculation of the “ultimate sentence”: see Marshall, para. 53.

    [116] This very point was recently made in R v Brown, 2025 ONCA  164, at para. 4, where it was stated that sentencing judges “must recognize that where an offender has already experienced particularly punitive conditions during their pre-sentence custody, the punishment they receive should be reduced to take this into account, but the degree of mitigation is a matter of discretion in all the circumstances, and not a matter of mathematical precision”.

    [117] I accept the defence submission that the harsh conditions of incarceration at TSDC are mitigating. Mr. Green was subject to lockdowns, staff shortages and triple bunking for more than 240 days, as well as Covid outbreaks, assaults, and the added stress arising from his disability, which requires physical activity and treatment.

    [118] Giving effect to this “Duncan” credit, as further explained in Marshall, I have determined that Mr. Green’s “Summers” credit of 2,061 days of pre-trial custody, or 68.7 months, being 5.7 years, should be rounded up to 72 months, or 6 years.

    Conclusion: Penitentiary Sentence for Mr. Green

    [119] Having determined that an 8-year (96 months) total sentence for Mr. Green is a fit sentence, and allowing for pre-trial custody of 6 years (72 months), I today sentence the defendant to 2 years’ imprisonment in the penitentiary.

    [120] For the purposes of apportioning this period of imprisonment, and accompanying pre-trial credit, to the six counts in the information, the sentences for each count are as follows:

    - possession of child pornography (July 14, 2019 – July 28, 2020): 3.5 years’ (42 months) imprisonment, less 32 months pre-trial custody: 10 months’ imprisonment;

    - accessing child pornography (July 14, 2019 – July 28, 2020): 10 months’ imprisonment, concurrent

    - possession of child pornography (June 13, 2020 – June 29, 2021): 3.5 years’ (42 months) imprisonment, less 32 months pre-trial custody: 10 months’ imprisonment, consecutive;

    - accessing child pornography (June 13, 2020) – June 29, 2021): 10 months’ imprisonment, concurrent;

    - breach of probation order: 6 months imprisonment, less 4 months pre-trial custody: 2 months’ imprisonment, consecutive;

    - breach of release order: 6 months imprisonment, less 4 months pre-trial custody: 2 months’ imprisonment, consecutive.

    [121] In this manner, I have credited Mr. Green with pre-trial custody, on the basis of Summers and Duncan, for a total of 72 months as indicated above, and when subtracted from the 8-year sentence of 96 months, there is a further period of 24 months or 2 years’ imprisonment being imposed on today’s date.

    A Long-Term Supervision Order for the Maximum Period of 10-years is Made Out

    [122] Having concluded that a sentence of 2 years in the penitentiary is a fit sentence for Mr. Green, I next consider whether the defendant should be found to be a long-term offender, and placed on a long-term supervision order for up to 10 years.

    [123] I believe that the evidence is overwhelming that such an order, for the maximum length of time, must be imposed.

    [124] To begin, the statutory criteria pursuant to s.753.1(1)(a) has been satisfied. I have determined that a sentence of imprisonment of two years or more for the offence for which the offender has been convicted would be appropriate: Criminal Code, s.753.1(1)(a).

    [125] Section 753.1(1)(b) next requires a finding that there is a “substantial risk” that the offender will reoffend.

    [126] Despite being sentenced to 15 months’ imprisonment in 2018 for child pornography offences, and placed on the maximum length of probation for 3 years, as well as additionally subject to a substantial release order, $5,000.00, with two sureties, all the while on probation, Mr. Green again committed child pornography offences. He was also receiving psychiatric care from Dr. Pallandi at the Riverdale Clinic at the time. There is evidence, in fact, that his use of pornography resumed within a little more than 6 months of being released from serving his sentence at OCI.

    [127] These are very significant factors, in my respectful opinion, when gauging the defendant’s risk to reoffend, at the present time.

    [128] Dr. Iosif places the defendant in the moderate category of risk for reoffending, especially when substance use is involved. Moreover, if he were to relapse, upon release, to stimulant use by resorting to amphetamines, she explained that his risk for reoffending by accessing or possessing child sexual abuse material would not only be high, it would “very likely occur”. Hence, it is noteworthy that, to date, the defendant has not been able to make significant or sustained efforts to address this underlying substance use issue, which, to his very own knowledge, fuels his desire to engage in child pornography offences. Moreover, his level of interest in child pornography, specifically keeping and accessing such materials, as Dr. Iosif noted, is not limited only to the times when he is intoxicated.

    [129] I do not consider, then, the issue of whether Mr. Green is a pedophile, or not, to be a driving factor in the s.753.1(1)(b) determination. He has a long-standing and demonstrable attraction to young boys. His relationship with his former partner, Mr. Swalm, who had such physical attributes, and women who resemble youthful male body-builders, substantiates this. He has a preference for youthful men; he has exclusively been in relationships with men. His collection of child pornography, principally of adolescent males between the ages of 5-12, dates back to 2012. Hence, as Dr. Iosif testified, non-exclusive pedophilia is not unheard of: Mr. Green’s relationships with adult men does not detract from his sexual interest in adolescent males.  

    [130] When sentencing Mr. Green in 2018, Justice Borenstein made reference to Dr. Bloom’s report, which I find consistent with that of Dr. Iosif. Dr. Bloom noted that the defendant was engaged in “impression management” and very much wanted to leave Dr. Bloom with the impression he was not pedophilic. Dr. Bloom felt, in fact, that in all likelihood, Mr. Green was not a pedophile. However, the doctor was concerned that the defendant’s efforts to ensure that he came to that conclusion, as well as his “almost exclusive blame on crystal methamphetamine for his behaviour,” raised red flags.

    [131] With respect to whether the defendant was likely to reoffend, Dr. Bloom was of the opinion that the principal risk factors for Mr. Green were his attraction to “a youthful male physique and his drug use”.

    [132] It can be seen, then, that moving forward six years to 2024, the time of Dr. Iosif’s report, the concerns in respect of the defendant’s risk factors are substantially the same. Indeed, they may be considered heightened, in that Dr. Bloom’s assessment of Mr. Green was as a first offender; Dr. Iosif dealt with him as a recidivist.

    [133] It is also significant, and particularly relevant from a risk of reoffending perspective, to note that the defendant is a not unsophisticated consumer of child pornography.  His collection at the time of his first sentencing was described as being substantial in size. It was in his possession for four years, between May, 2012 to August, 2016. It was made available through a file sharing or peer-to-peer network. Justice Borenstein rightly considered these to be serious aggravating factors.

    [134] Consequently, the persistent use of the same sophisticated child pornography access tools is particularly concerning. Moreover, the size and content of Mr. Green’s collection is not that of an unsophisticated or neophyte possessor or accessor of such contraband. He also took active steps to conceal his use of pornography from his probation officer, regardless of whether this was motivated by shame or subterfuge, as well as his two sureties, which placed them at considerable risk of forfeiting the $5,000.00 they pledged to supervise the defendant’s behaviour, and ensure he would not reoffend.

    [135] Returning, then, to the assertion by the defence that Dr. Iosif’s unconfirmed diagnosis of Mr. Green as a pedophile takes away from the need to impose a long-term supervision order, I respectfully disagree. Whatever the label, the risk factors of Mr. Green reoffending and committing the offence of possession of child pornography or accessing child pornography (enumerated offences under s.753.1(2)(a)) are overwhelming, let alone substantial. He has had a long-standing interest in young boys which manifests itself through the use of child pornography; his attraction to young boys in this medium is exacerbated by his use of crystal methamphetamine, which he has not been able to control for more than a decade. The latter has played “a significant role in this offence”, as Justice Borenstein noted when sentencing Mr. Green in 2018. It remains so, unabated, in 2025. The defendant’s collection of child pornography dates back to May, 2012. He has yet to stop offending, on his own accord. The red flags, as first noted by Dr. Bloom in 2017, remain as readily apparent, and obvious, and now considerably more ominous.

    [136] In the result, I have concluded that there is a substantial risk Mr. Green will reoffend: Criminal Code, s.753.1(b). Moreover, he has shown a pattern of “repetitive behaviour”, of which the offence for which he has been convicted forms a part, that shows a likelihood of the offender’s causing death or injury to other persons or inflicting severe psychological damage on other persons: Criminal Code, s.753.1(2)(b)(i). What drives this pattern of offending behaviour is not simply the defendant’s substance use, but his underlying sexual preference, which is for male children.

    [137] With respect to the harm caused by those, such as the defendant, who access and possess child pornography, Trotter J.A., for the Court, commented in R v Snowden, 2023 ONCA 768, at para. 93:

    Applied in the context of child pornography, the initial harm lies in the sexual assault of the infant or child. Added to this is the related harm of recording this abuse. This harm is different in kind, but still an extension of the sexual abuse. Similarly, the sharing of recordings with others furthers or perpetuates the harm caused by the initial recording and underlying abuse. Accessing and possessing child pornography fulfills the objective of the person who shares it or makes it available. Indeed, this market for child pornography, whether for purchase, trade or otherwise, may motivate those who make it available, and in turn, those who create it. Accessing and possessing child pornography may further harm the children depicted in the child pornography where they become aware that their image has been accessed or possessed. Thus, cognizable harm has the potential to extend beyond the initial abuse involved in creating child pornography. It may not be the same type of harm at each stage, but it need not be to remain within the ambit of conduct that inflicts or is likely to inflict severe psychological damage.

    [138] The nature of such severe psychological damage is further evidenced by the Community Impact Statement on behalf of the Canadian Centre for Child Protection, which details the horrific impact of child pornography on survivors, including attempts to take their own lives, change or conceal their identifies, and persistent fear of being recognized, and identified, in public.

    [139] To return to the requirement of “a pattern of repetitive behaviour” under s.753.1(2(b)(i), Mr. Green has a criminal history for one type of criminal offence only: possessing and accessing child pornography. His other convictions are inextricably interwoven with them: being in possession of drugs – used while possessing and accessing child pornography, and breach of probation and breach of a release order – by violating terms prohibiting his accessing and possessing child pornography.

    [140] The remaining condition precedent to the imposition of a long-term supervision order is that there is a reasonable possibility of eventual control of the risk in the community: Criminal Code, s.753.1(1)(c).

    [141] The parties are in agreement that this element of the Code provision has been made out. Dr. Iosif considered this to be so, noting that there are pro-social elements in Mr. Green’s makeup. This includes his having been in a long-term romantic relationship with an adult male, albeit with boyish features, his previous work experience, support from his mother, and his ability to remain out of trouble while residing with his mother, although that living arrangement is no longer available to him. He is also in his mid-40’s now, although burnout is not a particularly relevant factor in the case of non hands-on child abuse offenders. I consider Mr. Green’s stated expression to me of remorse, and wish to lead a pro-social life, to be genuine. However, he said the same thing to Justice Borenstein upon being sentenced in 2018.

    [142] Paramount to the defendant’s ability to control his risk in the community setting, is the imperative that he seek counselling for substance use and his well-being, including ADHD. He has yet to do so, notwithstanding the clear recognition and need for doing so, as Dr. Bloom noted in 2017.

    [143] High intensity substance use programs, and associated support and maintenance programs to assist the defendant in conducting himself in a pro-social manner, so that he does not revert to drug use when he is bored or prone to impulsivity and hyperactivity, due to ADHD, allows for a reasonable possibility of eventual control of the risk in the community.

    [144] I have therefore determined that the defendant should be found to be a long-term offender, and a long-term supervision order imposed, as the conditions precedent in s.753.1(1) have each been satisfied.

    [145] The maximum length of a long-term supervision order is 10 years: Criminal Code, s.753.1(3)(b).

    [146] In my respectful opinion, the duration of the order must be for this maximum period. I have arrived at this conclusion for a number of reasons.

    [147] First, it is clear on Dr. Iosif’s evidence that treatment programs for Mr. Green will not be sufficient if they are short term. He requires structure and supervision. He has yet to take meaningful treatment for substance use; ADHD remains unaddressed. The institutional records show that his willingness, voluntarily, to participate in treatment programs was poor, and he was removed from them. This continued to his post-release programming, although in fairness, Mr. Green may have taken some steps to commence treatment, which was not immediately available to him.

    [148] Particularly relevant, however, is that he has had made available to him almost five years of correctional involvement and supervision, in the context of 15-month jail sentence at OCI, and 3-years probation upon the completion of his jail sentence. He was also on a substantial release order, two sureties who pledged $5,000.00, for part of this time. This time period approximates the 5-year length of long-term supervision order that defence counsel says should now be imposed. To the contrary, this, in my view, substantiates why a lengthier period of supervision must be imposed now.

    [149] It is also clear that Mr. Green’s involvement with illegal pornographic images has continued unabated, for more than a decade.

    [150] This is not a case, then, like Bluecloud, para. 50, where a long-term supervision order of more than 7-years would be so long that the offender “despairs and loses his way”, and thwarts his “significant progress”. In fact, the maximum period of 10 years supervision is required, if there is to be any prospect of the defendant breaking his offence cycle, and managing his risk in the community.

    [151] And in the event that the defendant is, in fact, a pedophile, which as Dr. Iosif testified, is the most likely explanation for his conduct, and I also consider to be so, the risk of re-offending will never be totally eliminated, as there is no treatment for pedophilia. Mr. Green will remain attracted to male children, regardless; that is his clear and long-standing preference. The label for this attraction is not crucial. Other children, as Dr. Iosif explained, remain in potential danger as well.

    [152] For these reasons, I have determined that the defendant is a long-term offender, pursuant to s.753.1, and that a long-term supervision order of 10 years should be imposed: Criminal Code, s.753.1(3)(b).

    Ancillary Orders

    [153] There are a number of ancillary orders that are applicable, and require my consideration, the majority of which are not in dispute. I propose to deal with them separately below.

    DNA

    [154] Pursuant to s.487.04(1)(b), child pornography under s.163.1 is a primary designated offence. Accordingly, an order is authorized under s.487.05(1) for the taking of samples of bodily substances from Mr. Green that are reasonably required for the purpose of forensic DNA analysis. It is not contested that such an order should be made in this case. I note that such an order was previously made by Justice Borenstein.

    Weapons Prohibition

    [155] A weapons prohibition under s.109(1)(a) of the Criminal Code is also a mandatory order. Pursuant to this provision, Mr. Green is prohibited for life from being in possession of firearms, crossbows, restricted weapons, prohibited devices, a firearm part, any ammunition, prohibited ammunition or an explosive substance. As he has been previously convicted of similar offences, the duration of the order is for life: s.109(3). This order is likewise not opposed.

    SOIRA

    [156] Pursuant to the provisions of ss.490.012(1) and 490.013(4) of the Criminal Code, it is appropriate to order in Form 52 that the defendant is required to comply with the Sex Offender Information Registration Act for life. The lifetime duration is mandatory, as Mr. Green was previously given a SOIRA order (for life) by Justice Borenstein.

    Section 161 Order of Prohibition

    [157] In the proceedings before Justice Borenstein, a s.161 order was not issued, as the Court considered that the terms in it that were reasonable were incorporated, instead, into the probation order. This is patently not the case here, as the defendant will be serving his sentence in the federal system, where probation is not available.

    [158] The defence resists the terms of s.161 being imposed in their entirety, and that a lifetime order is not required. He submits that such an order would be excessive, and that limiting Mr. Green’s use of the Internet and social media, in particular, would impede his prospects for securing gainful employment. Moreover, he has not attended any of the enumerated places where children under the age of 16 years frequent, and has never committed a hands-on child abuse offence. Indeed, he did not use social media to commit the child pornography offences in question.

    [159] I consider, with respect, that these are not sufficient reasons to refrain from restricting the defendant’s whereabouts and limiting his access to the Internet. His probation order prohibited him from accessing any child pornography websites; his release order contained a similar prohibition. Both were proven to be ineffectual. He hid his breaching conduct from his supervising probation officer, and two sureties, respectively. As a result, the need for public protection must be paramount at the time of sentencing the defendant at this juncture of time.

    [160] Consequently, I agree with the Crown that the defendant should be bound by a s.161 order prohibiting him from attending any of the public places enumerated under subs.(1)(a), (a.1), such as public parks, daycare centres, schoolgrounds and playgrounds, where persons under 16 years are present or can reasonably be expected to be present.

    [161] Likewise, the restrictions in subs.(1)(b) prohibiting the defendant from seeking employment or volunteering that involves being in a position of trust or authority towards persons under the age of 16 years is appropriate, as is the prohibition against having contact, including communication by any means, with a person who is under the age of 16 years: subs.(1)(c).

    [162] I turn, lastly to the prohibition against using the Internet or other digital network, pursuant to s.161(1)(d). There is good reason to impose this condition as well, subject to limited exceptions to permit Mr. Green, when supervised by an appropriate adult, to use the Internet, for the purposes of seeking and maintaining employment only, as well as for the purposes of banking, seeking medical attention, pharmaceutical needs or grocery needs.

    [163] Despite being ordered to forfeit electronic material by Justice Borenstein, and prohibited under both a probation order and release order from accessing child pornography websites, it did not take long for Mr. Green to do so. He did this all the while being supervised by his probation officer and two sureties. He is, as I have noted, a not unsophisticated purveyor of child pornography. It is appropriate, therefore, that a s.161(1)(d) order, for life, be imposed against him, with very specific terms setting out the parameters of this aspect of the order. A lifetime order is authorized under s.161(2). I note that should the circumstances change, after the imposition of the order, which make it desirable to vary this condition, or any of the other conditions set out in the s.161 order, they may be varied by the court, upon application by the offender: s.161(3).

    [164] It is in the interests of all concerned that the terms of the prohibition order under s.161(1)(d) be reduced, separately, to writing, and attached to the transmission of materials that will be sent to CSC, pursuant to s.760 of the Criminal Code. This will ensure that there is no confusion or uncertainty as to the limited manner in which Mr. Green is to have any access to the Internet, or Internet capable devices.

    [165] The specific items to be addressed by this part of the order should include, at least, the following six areas: (i) a prohibition against the use of any encryption software or security program designed to prevent access to the content of the defendant’s Internet capable devices; (ii) a prohibition against the use of installing any program or service designed to allow anonymous use of the Internet; (iii) not being permitted to directly or indirectly access any peer-to-peer file sharing networks, such as, but not being limited to, motherless, Lime Wire, Gnutella, Bearshare, Shareaza; (iv) not being able to use the Internet to access any social media sites, social networks, or maintain a personal profile on services such as, but not being limited to, Facebook, Twitter, Tinder, Instagram, Snapchat, or any equivalent or similar service; any exceptions to this must be restricted for the purposes of seeking or maintaining employment, while in the presence of, or under the supervision of, a named party, approved of by his supervisor (v) a requirement that the defendant identify himself by his full, real name, and not by any pseudonym, nickname or code to identify himself, in any communication with anyone while using a computer or electronic device; (vi) a requirement to provide access upon request by his supervisor to any computer or electronic device he has used, or is in his possession, including installing and activating, at his own expense, such programs or hardware as ordered by his supervisor.

    [166] In order to finalize the above six terms and conditions in writing, I direct the parties to re-attend before me, no later than 14 days from today’s date, for the purpose of crafting the s.161(1)(d) portion of the Order, in accordance with para. 165 of these Reasons.   

    Forfeiture Order

    [167] As noted earlier, Justice Borenstein ordered forfeiture of the “electronic material” seized from the defendant, when sentencing him in 2018. A separate forfeiture order was also made for property, that although not identified, was apparently used by both the defendant and Mr. Swalm, some of which was suitable for return, as confirmed by the police.

    [168] In this case, a number of electronic devices were searched by the police, which contained child pornography. These included iphones, an ipad and a laptop computer. Sections 490.1 and 164.2 of the Criminal Code authorize forfeiture of all property seized from the offender which contain child pornography.

    [169] It is not clear to me whether the parties have considered whether a forfeiture order of these devices in the possession of the defendant would be appropriate in the circumstances of this case. At first blush, it appears warranted, particularly as a previous forfeiture order was brought against the offender, when being sentenced in 2018, for similar materials, and appears, in fact, to have been uncontested.

    [170] In order to provide the parties with an opportunity to address this issue, I direct that the parties appear before me, no later than 14 days from today’s date. The parties may present me with a draft forfeiture order, at this time, if the matter proceeds on consent.

    Victim Surcharge

    [171] Having been convicted of six indictable offences, Mr. Green is subject to a $200.00 victim surcharge, in respect of each count, pursuant to s.737(2)(b)(ii). This amounts to a total victim surcharge of $1,200.00.

    [172] It is appropriate to waive the victim surcharge on Mr. Green’s behalf, on the basis of undue hardship, specifically his disability due to his stroke, which limits his ability to work: Criminal Code, ss.737(2.1)(a), (2.2).

    Transmission of Reasons to CSC

    [173] Pursuant to s.760 of the Criminal Code, I order that that a copy of all reports and testimony given by Dr. Iosif, and other transcripts and reports, including these Reasons, and the accompanying ancillary orders, be forwarded to the Correctional Service of Canada for information.

    Summary of Sentencing Dispositions and Ancillary Orders

    [174] By way of summary, I have imposed the following sentencing dispositions and orders in respect of Mr. Green:

                (i)  a global sentence of 2 years’ imprisonment;

                (ii) a long-term offender designation;

                (iii) a long-term supervision order for 10 years;

                (iv) DNA Order for Primary Designated Offences;

                (v) Section 109 Weapons Prohibition Order for life;

                (vi) SOIRA Order for life;

    (vii) Section 161 Order of Prohibition for life;

    (viii) Hearing to be arranged within 14 days, in respect of wording of Section 161(1)(d) Order restricting use of Internet;

    (ix) Hearing to be arranged within 14 days, in respect Forfeiture Order of Devices containing Child Pornography seized from the offender;

    (x) Waiver of Victim Surcharge in the amount of $1,200.00;

    (xi) Transmission of Reasons and Materials to CSC.

    Concluding Comments

    [175] I wish to conclude by acknowledging the professionalism and high quality of written and oral advocacy by counsel for the Crown, and counsel for Mr. Green, over the past 26 months. The administration of justice is greatly enhanced when counsel conduct themselves in this manner. In preparing these Reasons, I have reviewed the numerous materials they have put before me, including their written submissions and supporting cases and documents. For this I am very grateful and appreciative of the efforts and diligence of both Ms. Garcia and Mr. Prajs.

    [176] Finally, to Mr. Green I say the following. Like Justice Borenstein, who you appeared before eight years ago, on similar charges, I consider your stated intention to me, to live a pro-social life, to be genuine. You have, however, not been able to do so to date. As a result, the stakes have gotten higher, and the greater punishment reflects this. Previously, you were sent to the reformatory; this time you are being sent to the penitentiary. The sentencing tools that are available to deal with any further child sexual abuse conduct in the future are therefore fewer, but do include, potentially, an indeterminate sentence and dangerous offender designation. This is the time, then, to redouble your efforts to be the best version of yourself, and follow the course of treatments that will be made available to you now, and in the future. It is my hope that, this time, they will be successful.

     

     

     

     

    R. Libman, J.
