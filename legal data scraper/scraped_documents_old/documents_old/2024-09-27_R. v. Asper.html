    ONTARIO COURT OF JUSTICE

    CITATION:  R. v. Asper, 2024 ONCJ 732

    DATE:  2024 09 27

    COURT FILE No.: 4810 998 23 48126592 00

     

     

    BETWEEN:

     

    HIS MAJESTY THE KING

     

     

    — AND —

     

     

    MATTHEW ASPER

     

     

     

    Before Justice Lori Montague

    Heard on July 30, 2024

    Reasons for Judgment released on September 27, 2024

     

     

    C. Chapin............................................................................................. Counsel for the Crown

    M. Gourlay/ B. Chung ………………………Counsel for the defendant Matthew Asper

     

    Montague J.:

    I. Overview

    [1]        Matthew Asper is charged with impaired operation of a motor vehicle and “over 80”. His defence intends to challenge the constitutionality of Section 320.31(9) of the Criminal Code of Canada, which allows the Crown to use compelled statements under the Highway Traffic Act (HTA) as admissible evidence for the purpose of justifying a demand for a breath sample (under Sections 320.27, or 320.28).

    [2]        The Crown has applied for directions with respect to the order of operations for the trial of the matter. The defence has thus far resisted setting trial dates, as it is their position that the constitutional matter should be disposed of first, and the trial should follow thereafter.  The Crown takes the opposite position, and has filed this application for directions on the specific issue of scheduling.

    II. Relevant Facts of the Case

    [3]        The pertinent facts of the case for the purpose of this application are:

    •      The Respondent is alleged to have run a red light while driving under the influence.

    •      He is alleged to have crashed into another car after running the red light.

    •      After the collision the Respondent was transported to a hospital.

    •      Police went to the hospital; they spoke with the Respondent.  They asked him questions, including about whether there was a second person in the car.

    •      They asked him if he had a passenger.

    •      The Respondent answered that he was alone.

    •      The Crown intends to use the statements at the hospital as grounds for the breath demand.

    •      The defence argues that the statements were compelled under s. 199 of the Highway Traffic Act, and in accordance with R. v. White, 1999 CanLII 689 (SCC), [1999] 2 S.C.R. 417, that the statements are inadmissible for the Crown’s intended purpose.

    •      Section 320.31(9) of the Criminal Code renders the statements admissible, despite White (hence the defence’s constitutional application).

    III. Issues

    [4]        This court has reviewed the caselaw in this area.  While each case is (as they should be) determined, in part, based on their individual factual situations, there is consensus that the general rule is that constitutional matters should be heard at the end of a trial. However, there are exceptions: and on the application before this court, the parties disagree on whether or not Mr. Asper’s, is such an exceptional case.

    [5]        The Applicant argues that the general rule applies, and that this is not an exceptional case.  The Applicant’s position is that none of the criteria recognized by the Supreme Court of Canada in R. v. DeSousa (1992 CanLII 80 (SCC), [1992] 2 S.C.R. 944, at paragraph 17) favour departing from the general rule.  Those criteria are:

    A.   That there is an ongoing constitutional violation that requires immediate attention;

    B.   It would save time to decide the constitutional question before proceeding to trial; and

    C.   The constitutional issue does not depend on facts to be elicited at trial.

    [6]        I shall deal with each of those factors, below.

    A.    Is there an ongoing constitutional violation that requires immediate determination?

    [7]        The defence did not address this criteria for exceptionality: and no such ongoing violation is apparent to this court. The interests of justice do not necessitate an immediate decision being made, either. I therefore find, that this criteria of “exceptionality” is not applicable, on the application before me.

    B.    Would it save time to decide the constitutional question before proceeding to trial?

    [8]        Specifically with respect to the second criteria, time saving, the Crown argues that the impugned provision will have no application unless the accused establishes that the statements were made under statutory compulsion.  As a result, conducting the trial may render the constitutional challenge moot if the court finds the statement was not compelled (i.e., if not compelled, then no application of the impugned section is applicable).

    [9]        Further, the constitutional challenge may also be unnecessary if the Crown establishes grounds aside from the statements at issue, for the demand (i.e., if other grounds exist, then the statement will not matter.)

    [10]      Finally, if the challenge proceeds first, and the outcome is successful for the defence, the Crown submits that it still does not dispose of the need for a trial.  A trial must still proceed to determine whether there is evidence aside from the impugned statements to use as grounds for the demand (i.e. if there is a successful constitutional challenge  application, then the case will still need a trial. In other words, there will be a trial regardless of the order of operations).

    [11]      The Crown also argues that the constitutional challenge requires expert evidence in response, and if not necessary, none of that evidence will need to be called.

    [12]      The defence agrees that a trial will be required regardless.  However, they argue that the trial will be more streamlined, and benefit from a ruling on the constitutional issue in advance because the trial judge will not need to grapple with what may be inadmissible evidence in the context of the rest of the issues.  The trial evidence will not be “muddled”.  Further, there will be no need for a White hearing if the challenge is successful.   The defence argues there is judicial economy in receiving a ruling on the challenge first.

    [13]      The defence also argues that Charter values favour a pretrial determination because that will preserve the accused’s right to retain the choice of whether or not to testify at his own trial, after the close of the Crown’s case.  If the trial proceeds before the challenge, he will be required to testify on the White hearing: whereas if the constitutional challenge is in advance, and proves successful, he would not need to do that.

    C.   Is determination of the constitutional issue dependent on facts to be elicited at trial?

    [14]      On the third factor, it is the Crown’s position that the constitutional challenge is “inextricably tied” to the trial evidence.  Their position is that the defence will have to first establish that the respondent made a compelled statement.  That will require evidence from the officer and the accused.  Thus, hearing the constitutional application first will require duplication of evidence, the Applicant submits. The Crown also highlights that they will be calling evidence at the constitutional challenge stage, and so it is not an application without evidence.

    [15]      The defence does not agree that this is a prerequisite to litigating the constitutional question.  They submit that the court need not conclude that the accused’s statements were compelled before the court addresses the constitutional question.  One should note, that it does not appear the defence would be calling the accused to give evidence on that issue at the constitutional question stage. 

    [16]      The defence relies on DeSousa to submit that it matters not whether the section applies to the defendant’s conduct. The defence submits that Sopinka J. found that it did not matter whether the accused’s conduct attracted criminal responsibility, as that would not resolve “the issue if the section in its other applications criminalized conduct that did not meet constitutional standards.” (see DeSousa, at para. 18).  As such, there is no need to first establish that the section applies to statements at issue in this case.  The defence also relies on Mills for this proposition: “The respondent (defence) need not prove that the impugned legislation would probably violate his right to make full answer and defence.  Establishing that the legislation is unconstitutional in its general effects would suffice….” (see paras. 29-33 of the Respondent’s factum).

    [17]      The defence focuses on the factors articulated at paragraph 17 of DeSousa, to argue that this is a case where the constitutional question should be answered in advance.  They submit that the Charter challenge is meritorious and does not depend on facts to be elicited at trial.

    [18]      The defence argues that the challenge is meritorious because the Court of Appeal in R. v. Soules, 2011 ONCA 429 [leave to appeal refused (2011 CanLII 82377 (SCC)] has answered the question at issue.  The Court of Appeal for Ontario has said that a statutorily compelled statement under the HTA can be used to prove non-compliance with the Act and “nothing more”.

    [19]      Support for this position, the respondent argues, can be found in R. v. Porter, 2015 ABCA 279, R. v. Roberts, 2018 ONCA 411, and R. v. Korduner, 2022 ABKB 1790.  Korduner is the case that the respondent also relies on to submit this question can be answered without relying on “adjudicative facts”.  Korduner held the following: “Section 320.31(9) is an attempt to override this accepted jurisprudence and alter the legal effect of White…Section 320.31(9) cannot change nor alter s. 7 Charter rights” (and the court in that case found so, without citing the trial evidence in that case to make that finding).

    IV. Legal Analysis

    [20]      On the basis of what I have been made aware, in terms of how both the Crown and defence intend to proceed, I find that it would not significantly save time, to decide the constitutional question before proceeding to the trial proper. I concur with the submission of the applicant: that there will be a trial, regardless of the order of operations of its aspects. A trial is still going to proceed to determine whether there is evidence aside from the impugned statements to use as grounds for the demand. There may also be other issues – Charter, or other evidentiary-based – that arise from the disclosure already provided, and or during the in-court presentation of the Crown’s case as it takes place.

    [21]      In R. v. Mills, (1999) 1999 CanLII 637 (SCC), 3 SCR 668, the SCC discouraged the adjudication of constitutional issues without factual foundation.  However, the court also noted that there are exceptions to the general rule.  For example: “An apparently meritorious Charter challenge of the law under which the accused is charged, which is not dependent on facts to be elicited during the trial, may come within the exceptions to the general rule.” (see para. 37).  In Mills, the trial judge was found not to have erred in dismissing the motion before hearing evidence. The challenge was not without merit, and the trial evidence would not have assisted in the determination of the issues.

    [22]      Mills was relied on by the OCA in R. v. Levkovic, (2010 ONCA 830). The Court of Appeal for Ontario said that it was difficult to “understate the importance of a factual basis in constitutional challenges”. However, the authorities that insist on an adequate factual foundation recognize that this general rule is “not inflexible or intolerant of exception” (see para 38 of Mills). It is dependent on the nature of the challenge, the interests at stake, and the likelihood or improbability that the evidence to be adduced at trial would assist in the resolution of the issue (see para. 40 of Lekovic).

    [23]      In R. v. Pfaller, [2008] O.J. No. 4633, Justice M. Green held that the pre­sump­tive position, with only rare exception, was that there needs to be adjudicative facts upon which an application would be heard. Further, Justice Green held that challenges to the constitutionality of the law should only be considered as a last resort to the determination of the matter. (see para. 5, citing guidelines from DeSousa).

    [24]      Having reviewed the cases provided, DeSousa really enunciates “first principles” in this regard. It is relevant to look at the basis for the constitutional challenge in that case: which was, in short, a court’s power (and duty), to review the elements of a criminal offence.  Mr DeSousa was involved in a fight in which a bystander was injured by a glass fragment from when a bottle he (allegedly) threw, broke against a wall.  As a result, Mr. DeSousa was charged with unlawfully causing bodily harm, pursuant to Section 269 of the Criminal Code. It is that section which his defence brought a motion at the onset of the trial, to have declared of no force or effect, on the grounds that it infringed Section 7 of the Charter. That application was granted, before the hearing of any evidence, on the basis that Section 268 created criminal responsibility for causing bodily harm by way of a lawful act – which could include an offence of absolute liability (despite of an accused’s lack of intent to cause the consequence of bodily harm): and, since the section also allowed for the risk of imprisonment without a blame-worthy state of mind, that it thus contravened Section 7, and was not Section 1 – justified. To distill the essence of the basis upon which the trial judge made her ruling: it involved a consideration of the wording of a Criminal Code section and its potential penalty, and how that section interacted with a simple, undisputed narrative and legal concept - absolute liability.  Not only would testimony from Mr. DeSousa have been unnecessary to provide an “evidentiary record” for her consideration: but the judge’s finding was also not dependent on facts to be elicited during the trial. It was based on the “general effect “ of the Criminal Code legislation, entirely independent of Mr. DeSousa’s actual actions/words.  Evidence heard at a trial would not have assisted in the resolution of the constitutional question being put to the trial judge: the SCC found that it was irrelevant, whether the facts at trial would establish a mental element compatible with constitutional mens rea requirements.  I also note that no objection was taken at trial by the Crown, to the procedure the justice followed.

    [25]      Justice Sopinka noted that “a motion to quash which questions the validity of the law under which the accused is charged” involves a “defect which goes to the jurisdiction of the court to proceed with the charge”, and that “such an application may be brought at any time.”  However, while finding that there was no question that the trial judge had jurisdiction to hear and dispose of the motion to quash the indictment on the grounds of constitutional invalidity, he continued “whether he or she is bound to do so or whether as a matter of practice should do so, is more problematic.”  He then references a trial judge being “empowered to reserve on any application until the end of the case”: and that their exercise of discretion is dependent upon the dual policy considerations that “criminal proceedings should not be fragmented by interlocutory proceedings which take on a life of their own: and that adjudication of constitutional issues without a factual foundation should be discouraged.  The court emphasizes that “both these policies favor disposition of applications at the end of the case”, and that in exercising their discretion, a trial judge “should not depart from these policies unless there is a strong reason for doing so.”

    [26]      While I find that the Respondent’s proposed constitutional challenge is meritorious, I cannot find that it is also non-dependent on facts to be elicited at trial (emphasis added).  Unlike Justice Sopinka, I am not “satisfied the evidence at trial would not have assisted in the resolution of the constitutional question, given the nature of the appellant’s (i.e., defendant’s) submissions”. The “evidentiary record” before me on this motion is not fulsome. It will be, much more so, before an actual trier of fact. I find that evidence to be adduced at trial would assist in the resolution of the constitutional issue. As previously noted – it is also possible, that trial evidence may be produced in a manner where the proposed constitutional issue becomes moot, and or where other issues which the defence (and potentially, the Crown) become the subject of argument before this (or higher) levels of court.

    [27]      I find that this is a situation where the dual policy considerations in DeSousa favor “disposition of the (here, constitutional) issue at the end of the case”, and that in exercising my discretion, there has not been a “strong reason” established, why I should depart from those policy considerations.”

    V. Conclusion

    [28]      I find that the trial in this matter should be scheduled to proceed, and that the constitutionality of Section 320.31(9) of the Criminal Code of Canada should be determined only after the trial evidence has been heard, with the benefit of a more fulsome evidentiary record.

    [29]      This court has come to its conclusion, but I should note, not without consideration for whether there are any overarching administrative concerns with respect to issuing a ruling on this issue, at this stage in the proceedings.

    [30]      This ruling on the order of operations for the constitutional challenge was made based on the positions of the parties and an assessment of the law, including the caselaw submitted by counsel.  The Criminal Rules of Procedure also reflect the power of a court to schedule applications and trial dates. (See Rule 2.5 Criminal Rules - Ontario Court of Justice [ontariocourts.ca]).

    [31]      On the issue of setting trial dates as part of case management, in R. v. Manchisi, 2024 ONCJ 171, Acting Regional Senior Justice Leitch disposed of a defence application for a case management judge under ss. 551.1-551.7 of the Code.  The defence took the position that judicial pretrial judge did not have the jurisdiction to direct that the trial be set in a certain order (ie, with the stay application proceeding last). 

    [32]      The JPT judge approved a two-day trial including a motion for a stay, to be heard last.  Justice Leitch concluded that courts routinely find that decisions on stay applications should be deferred until the end of trial so that the degree of prejudice can be determined on a full evidentiary record. However, Justice Leitch also found that while case management powers did give a case management judge the power to establish schedules and impose deadlines, it did not follow that the appointed case management judge is the “exclusive repository of this power”. (see para. 4). Justice Leitch also held that once a trial judge is assigned, they have jurisdiction to hear motions in the order they think fit to ensure a fair trial.

    [33]      Thus, any rulings with respect to the scheduling of dates may be re-examined by the trial judge once they are assigned to the matter.  It may be of assistance, to avoid any scheduling issues arising after such a ruling, to direct that the constitutional materials be filed early in this matter (e.g. 60 or 90 days before the scheduled trial date), with a corresponding confirmation date(s) and or focus hearing, that might serve as a check-in on the proposed schedule, with the benefit of more information about the constitutional application being provided to the assigned justice.
