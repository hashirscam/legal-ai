    The Law Relating to R. v. W. (D.) and R. v. Villaroman

    [83]      As in any criminal case, Mr.  Taylor Javer is presumed innocent until proven guilty. I have reminded myself that I need not firmly believe or disbelieve any witness and that I can accept all, some, or none of a witness’ testimony.  I have also reminded myself that the Crown must prove the essential elements of the offence beyond a reasonable doubt, as this term has been defined and explained by the Supreme Court of Canada in R. v. W. (D.).[3] Proof of a probability of guilt does not amount to proof of guilt beyond a reasonable doubt. Proof of guilt to a near certainty is required in criminal proceedings.

    [84]      The onus remains on the Crown to prove Mr. Javer’s guilt beyond a reasonable doubt throughout his trial. A reasonable doubt is a doubt based on reason and common sense, one that arises logically from the whole of the evidence or absence of evidence. I recognize that the rule of reasonable doubt applies to the issue of credibility.  Accordingly, I must acquit the defendant if I accept his evidence or if it raises a reasonable doubt after considering it in the context of the evidence as a whole. If I reject his evidence, and it does not leave me with a reasonable doubt, I must go on to ask whether the evidence that I do accept convinces me of the guilt of the defendant beyond a reasonable doubt.

    [85]      A determination of guilt or innocence must not, however, devolve into a mere credibility contest between two witnesses. Such an approach erodes the operation of the presumption of innocence and the assigned standard of persuasion of proof beyond a reasonable doubt: W.(D.);[4] Avetsyan v. The Queen.[5]

    [86]      As the Ontario Court of Appeal in R. v. Hull,[6] noted:

    W.(D.) and other authorities prohibit triers of fact from treating the standard of proof as a credibility contest. Put another way, they prohibit the trier of fact from concluding that the standard of proof has been met simply because the trier of fact prefers the evidence of Crown witnesses to that of defence witnesses.

    [87]      I must assess the evidence of the complainant and the defendant in light of the totality of the evidence, which includes and permits comparing and contrasting the evidence of those witnesses, other witnesses and the exhibits. The Court of Appeal in Hull continued:

    However, such authorities do not prohibit the trier of fact from assessing an accused’s testimony in light of the whole evidence, including the testimony of the complainant, and in so doing comparing the evidence of the witnesses.  On the contrary, triers of fact have a positive duty to carry out such an assessment recognizing that one possible outcome of the assessment is that the trier of fact may be left with a reasonable doubt concerning the guilt of the accused.

    [88]      Proof beyond a reasonable doubt means what it says. There is thus nothing illogical in rejecting the defendant’s evidence but still not being sufficiently satisfied by the complainant’s evidence to find that the case has been proven. A state of uncertainty at a trial like this, where the court has heard two conflicting versions from the two parties involved, is not uncommon. Ultimately, if I have a reasonable doubt on the whole of the case that arises from the evidence of the Crown witnesses, the evidence of the accused or the evidence of any other witnesses, or the absence of evidence, the charge must be dismissed: Lifchus.[7]

    [89]      I have also reminded myself that circumstantial evidence may or may not prove a fact from which an inference may be drawn, that is, a factual conclusion that logically and reasonably flows or may be drawn from that evidence. However, I have also reminded myself that the only inferences that may be drawn are those based solely on the evidence in this case, and that they may not and must not be based on conjecture or speculation. It is speculative to draw an inference when there is no direct or indirect factual or evidential basis to support it. However, it is the cumulative effect of all of the evidence that must meet the standard of proof beyond a reasonable doubt, not each individual item of evidence.

    [90]      More importantly, I have reminded myself that where the only evidence relative to a particular fact that is alleged is circumstantial evidence, before I can find the accused guilty on the basis of that evidence, I must be satisfied beyond a reasonable doubt that proof of the particular element of the offence, or guilt relative to the offence as a whole, is the only reasonable or rational conclusion or inference that can be drawn from the whole of the evidence. It is important to note that I do not need to be satisfied to that standard relative to each individual piece of evidence, particularly where more than one conclusion may flow from the particular piece of evidence under consideration. However, within the context of the evidence as a whole, I must be satisfied that the Crown has made out the elements of the offences beyond a reasonable doubt.

    [91]      Therefore, where the Crown relies upon circumstantial evidence to prove the essential elements of the offences beyond a reasonable doubt, the test, pursuant to R. v. Villaroman,[8] is “whether the trier of fact, acting judicially, could reasonably be satisfied that the accused's guilt was the only reasonable conclusion available on the totality of the evidence,” (see R. v. Wu.[9])

    [92]      These are the principles I must use in my assessment of the totality of the evidence led during Mr. Taylor Javer’s trial.

    Mistaken Belief in Age: s. 150.1(4) Criminal Code of Canada as it Relates to ss. 151 and 152.

    [99]      Section 150.1(4) applies to the sexual assault, sexual interference and invitation to sexual touching offences facing Mr. Javer.

    s. 150.1(4) It is not a defence to a charge under section 151 or 152, subsection 160(3) or 173(2), or section 271, 272 or 273 that the accused believed that the complainant was 16 years of age or more at the time of the offence is alleged to have been committed unless the accused took all reasonable steps to ascertain the age of the complainant.

    [100]   This defence is not available where the claim is entirely devoid of an objective basis: R. v. W.G.;[16] R. v. Morrison;[17] and R. v. Carbone.[18] The threshold evidentiary burden on the defence is to adduce evidence sufficient to give the defence an air of reality: Morrison, at paras. 84, 118; R. v. Levigne;[19] and Carbone, at 129. Where the defence of s. 150.1(4) is in play, the Crown may negate it in one of two ways: prove the accused did not honestly believe that the complainant was at least 16 years old at the time of the alleged offence or the Crown may prove, despite the accused’s claim of honestly believing the complainant was at least 16, the accused did not take all reasonable steps to ascertain the complainant’s age. Morrison, at para. 88 and Carbone, at para. 118.

    [101]   The “all reasonable steps” analysis is highly contextual and fact specific. In W.G., at paras. 60 to 62, the Ontario Court of Appeal defined reasonable steps:

    60 "Reasonable steps" are steps that a reasonable person would take, in the same circumstances known to the accused at the time, to ascertain the complainant's age. The reasonable steps requirement includes both objective and subjective elements. The steps, viewed objectively, must be reasonable. And the reasonableness of those steps must be asserted in the circumstances known to the accused: Morrison, at para. 105.

    61 "Reasonable steps" are steps that provide information reasonably capable of supporting an accused's belief that the complainant is of legal age. In other words, the steps must be meaningful. Steps that solicit information that does not reasonably support a belief in legal age are not reasonable steps. Relevant considerations include not only the nature of the steps themselves, but also the information that those steps solicit. The steps need not always be active. However, where the early steps of an accused could reasonably support a belief that the other person is of legal age, but later events raise "red flags" that the other person may not be of legal age, additional steps may be required to meet the standard of s. 150.1(4): Morrison, at paras. 106-109.

    62 There is no magic number or exhaustive list of steps that an accused must take to satisfy the "all reasonable steps" requirement in s. 150.1(4). A practical, common sense approach should prevail, informed by the overarching purpose of the provision - to protect vulnerable young people from sexual crimes by assigning responsibility for preventing adult/youth sexual activity on adults. And by an enhanced standard. Not simply "reasonable steps", but "all reasonable steps to ascertain the age" of the complainant.

    [102]      Where the defence under s. 150.1(4) has been disproven by the Crown, this does not mean that on this basis alone the offences have been proven beyond a reasonable doubt. Whether the offences charged have been so proven depends on whether the judge concludes the Crown has proven beyond a reasonable doubt that the accused believed the complainant was underage: Morrison, at para. 129. W.G., at paras. 69 to 70 succinctly sets out the fault element under s. 151, 152 and 271 when dealing with an underage complainant:

    69 The fault element under s. 151 may be proven by establishing that the accused believed that the complainant was under 16, or that the accused was wilfully blind to the fact that the complainant was under 16. The Crown may also establish the fault element by proving beyond a reasonable doubt that the accused believed that there was a risk that the complainant was under 16, but went ahead anyway, choosing to do so despite the risk. In other words, the accused was reckless as to the complainant's true age. And the Crown may also demonstrate that the accused never turned their mind to the complainant's age as they proceeded. This too may establish recklessness on the accused's part with respect to the complainant's age. Reckless indifference is a subjective state of mind bespeaking a choice on the part of an accused to treat the complainant's age as irrelevant and to assume the risk associated with their choice: Carbone, at paras. 123, 126-127.

    70 As Carbone teaches, the fault element of recklessness is subjective. It involves the appreciation of some level of risk coupled with the conscious decision to take that risk. Under s. 151, an accused who chooses to proceed with the activity the section prohibits - sexual activity with young persons - after having adverted to the possibility that the complainant was underage, will inevitably be found to have been reckless with respect to the complainant's age: Carbone, at para. 125. So too, at least in most cases, indifference to the complainant's age, a state of mind that reflects a positive choice by an accused to treat the complainant's age as irrelevant to their decision to engage in the sexual activity: Carbone, at paras. 126-127.

    The Law Respecting the Offence of Luring, s. 172.1(1)(b)

    [106]   In R. v. Legare,[21] Fish J., for the court, dealing with the mens rea required pursuant to s. 172.1 (child luring) held:

    It will immediately be seen that s. 172.1(1)(c) creates an incipient or "inchoate" offence, that is, a preparatory crime that captures otherwise legal conduct meant to culminate in the commission of a completed crime. It criminalizes conduct that precedes the commission of the sexual offences to which it refers, and even an attempt to commit them. Nor, indeed, must the offender meet or intend to meet the victim with a view to committing any of the specified secondary offences. This is in keeping with Parliament's objective to close the cyberspace door before the predator gets in to prey. [emphasis added]

    [107]   Child luring is an offence designed to catch sexual predators before they act, not after. The focus is on the predator’s intentions at the time of the communication, not after.

    [108]   The Supreme Court of Canada in R. v. Morrison,[22] confirmed the three essential elements of child luring as previously set out at para. 23 of R. v. Levigne[23]:

    (1) an intentional communication by means of telecommunication;

    (2) with a person who is, or who the accused believes is, under the requisite age; and

    (3) for the purpose of facilitating the commission of a designated offence with respect to that person.

    [109]   In Bertrand Marchand,[24]the Supreme Court held: “The text and purpose of s. 172.1 demonstrates that it casts and was intended to cast, a wide net of potential liability for those who lure children in a virtual environment. …” (see para. 10). Further, “...While a specific intention to facilitate a designated secondary offence forms part of the requisite mens rea, criminal liability will "crystallize" before any actions are taken on the part of the accused to engage in a designated offence…The offence of luring does not require that the parties ever meet or touch” (see para. 11). The Crown is not required to prove multiple communications over an extended period of time between the parties and when the other elements of the offence are satisfied it can be committed even by sending one message (see para. 13).  

    [110]   In R. v. So, it is knowing, wilful blindness, or recklessness. Fox in our Ontario Court of Appeal. The sections that deal with that are ss. 15-18 and also 47.  I will just read para. 47. 

    In summary, to establish the mens rea as to age for the first mode of offence under s. 172.1(1)(a) involving an actual child, the Crown must prove beyond a reasonable doubt that the accused either knew, was wilfully blind, or was reckless to the fact that the person was under the prescribed age. Accordingly, the trial judge erred in concluding that recklessness as to age was insufficient mens rea for luring an actual child.

Yet this was the only two topics Mr. Javer could come up with. that there were discussions about.  I do not believe him at all on this issue. 

    [164]   Another issue is when Z. provided her statement to D.C. Waugh, how old did she look? When she testified on March 15, 2024, she had just turned 17. In my view when she was on zoom (CCTV because of her being under 18) testifying she looked like a young teenager. However, I have watched the video of her speaking to D.C. In my view she presented as a child, she is sitting cross-legged on a couch, leaning forward as she answered the officer’s questions. Her voice is high-pitched with a lisp that even Mr. Javer agreed she had, although Mr. Javer said he could not remember what her voice sounded like because it was on the phone. When Mr. Javer is questioned about her appearance, he initially said she looked on the police video statement like she did when he met her March 27, which makes sense because it is only about five weeks apart. People do not change that much in five weeks. But later his testimony changed, and he testified he was not able to really make out if it was the same on the video as the person he met on March 27, as he couldn’t see the monitor as it was very “fuzzy”. The monitor was a large TV screen and Z. was larger than life. When the Crown then created a screen capture of her face and suggested this was a childlike picture of a girl of 14. Mr. Javer said Z. sent him photographs of her face when they were conversing. He described the photographs of being just her face and said that they appeared to be professionally done.  Of course, none of those photographs were provided to the Court by Mr. Javer.  He said he did not wish to be rude, but she was a little overweight in the video and her face was a little “wider” than when they met. The video was taken on May 3, 2022, just five weeks after he met her for the first time for less 15 minutes total on March 27. He had originally said what was on the video looked like the person he met on March 27.  Again, another example of his evidence changing.   

    [165]   There were many red flags presented to Mr. Javer over the four months he was communicating with Z. that in my view required him to take additional steps. I have gone through some of those in my reciting of things that I do not believe that Mr. Javer has indicated, but I am now going to point out some, but this is not an exhaustive list.

    1. The difference in their ages alone required Mr. Javer, in my view, to investigate Z.’s age further. He was 25. Even if she told him she was 16, that is a 9 year difference.  And it is not just a difference between somebody who is 40 and somebody who is 33, or somebody who is 60 and somebody who is 53.  This is a difference in age between an adult and a teenager. It is not good enough just to accept what he says she told him that she was 16, in my view. Interestingly, he implied in his testimony if a girl was 16, this meant any sexual relationship he, as a 25 year old man, had with her was legal. That was a concerning statement to me. That is a statement that demonstrates as an adult, he has understood the age of consent being 16. 

    2. Her telling him on a couple of occasions in texts he mom had taken her Snapchat away and was stopping her from using it. That, in my view, is an indication and a red flag to somebody who is 25 that maybe he should be investigating a little bit further to find out exactly how old she is if her mother is able to tell her whether she can be on Snapchat.

    3. Her other personality “Ivy” was between 5 and 7 and posted “poop” emojis and clearly was under 16. And in my view, that alone was a red flag that should have required Mr. Javer to make further inquiries, demanded that he make further inquiries. 

    4. And when you couple that with the “tics” that I referred to, which is the fourth item I have here, that caused her to twitch, with sounds and movements, which was aggressive mostly towards herself but also towards others. He did not have the photo, which he described as being done “professionally,” but in my view, the video clearly demonstrates in her statement to DC Waugh that she is a young teenager, someone who should have caused him to make further inquiries if she looked as she looked in the video, which I find that she did, when he met her on March 27. 

    [166]   An area that caused me great concern in Mr. Javer's evidence that occurred once in-chief and twice in cross, the two most blatant examples were in cross, but it was an attempt by him to cast criticism on Z.B.’s chastity and suggesting that she was involved with others sexually even at 16 or 15. There was no application under s. 276. There was no application under the new provisions of the Criminal Code. These were things that Mr. Javer volunteered, where he said that Z. told him that she hung out with her father and his friends, that she dated her father's friends.

    [167]   First of all, I heard no evidence about Z.'s father.  I do not know who Z.'s father is. All I know is she has a mother. That is all I know. I do not know if she has a father.  I know that there were problems. I know there was a reference at one point where Z. testified that she viewed Mr. Javer as an older male figure in her life, that she did not have somebody like that. And that has certain inferences that could be drawn from the statement that she made, but she was never asked about her father.  And yet, this was something, I believe, that Mr. Javer made up. It was never said by Z., and I find it despicable, to be frank, to suggest that a 16-year-old is talking about dating her father's friends. 

    [168]   It did not end there though, because when reference was made to whether Z. had advised him she had missed her period, his initial response was that had not happened. He did not have any recollection of that.  When he was shown his texts, he had to concede that, yes, she did tell me that.  But then again, in almost retaliation for her making that suggestion to him, he made reference to the fact that she was talking and seeing other individuals that she had met online, implying that maybe things of a sexual nature were occurring with others. 

    [169]   That was completely improper. I do not take any of that evidence into consideration, as I indicated before.  But in my view, it affects the credibility of Mr. Javer in terms of my assessment of his credibility, and I find that it affects his credibility very negatively.

    [170]   As I say, all of these allegations were made without a proper application and foundation being laid.  And you add to that Mr. Javer's evidence that it was Z. who was the one who was initiating all conversations about sex and sexual activity, that he was not going to have sex with someone he was meeting for the first time.

    [171]   Although it is interesting, Ms. Reynolds submitted at one point during her submissions, that Mr. Javer, “knew sexual activity was obviously a possibility as they had discussed it before.  His intention, on that day was just to meet up with her and see where things went.” 

    [172]   That was not Mr. Javer's evidence before me in the witness stand. But as I have already indicated, I believe that was his intention, was to get together to have sex with someone that he says he was told was 16, with all sorts of red flags that he should have investigated to determine whether or not she actually was, on his evidence, 16. As I have indicated, I do not believe that evidence. I believe Z., who told him she was 14, as I am going to get into now.

    [173]   Mr. Javer did not present any evidence of any positive, actual steps he took to verify or determine Z.B.’s actual age after any of these flags arose. He simply accepted without question what he said Z. told him. Given my previous findings of Mr. Javer been shown to be lying and not being truthful respecting issues important in this case, I do not believe that Z. told him she was 16. Z. testified she did not lie about her age and that when they first met in December 2021 she told him she was 14. She was telling Mr. Javer about how she had “tics” and a multiple personality in “Ivy”, she had no reason to lie about her age. She was giving him personal information because she trusted him, and that could only have come about because of the conversations that they were having between a 25-year-old man and the 15-year-old young girl. She told him her birthdate, contrary to what Mr. Javer testified to, I do not believe him.  I believe Z. told him her birth date was February [x], 2007, is my memory. Mr. Javer testified he did not know her birthdate but more importantly did not ask her what her birthdate was. Anyone who is engaging in reasonable steps to ascertain age is going to ask what the birthday is. That is usually where people trip up, when they are trying to pretend they are older and they do not really know because they know their real birthday, but they do not know the one that makes them older. That is a typical question that they ask in bars to people who come in or at the LCBO when you try to buy liquor.

    [174]   In my view this was a reasonable step Mr. Javer would reasonably have been expected to take.  He did not take that step. He did not take any steps to ascertain Z.'s age, and in my view, this supports my finding that on the totality of the evidence presented in this trial, there is no “air of reality” to Mr. Javer's position that he was honestly mistaken about Z.'s age. And based on Carbone and W.G., the fact the defence under s. 150.1(4) is not available, is not a pathway to conviction. The Crown must still prove beyond a reasonable doubt that Mr. Javer knew, was wilfully blind, or reckless that Z.B. was under 16 years of age. 

    [175]   I have quoted some of these passages again, but I think they are worth quoting again. In W.G., at paras. 68 and 69, Justice Watt held:

    68 Where the Crown has disproven the honest belief in age defence in s. 150.1(4), the trier of fact is left with three possible states of mind. The accused may have believed or have been wilfully blind to the fact that the complainant was under 16. The accused may have appreciated that there was a risk that the complainant was under 16 but decided to go ahead anyway despite that risk. Or the accused may never have adverted to the complainant's age and chose to proceed with the touching: Carbone, at para. 122.

    69 The fault element under s. 151 may be proven by establishing that the accused believed that the complainant was under 16, or that the accused was wilfully blind to the fact that the complainant was under 16. The Crown may also establish the fault element by proving beyond a reasonable doubt that the accused believed that there was a risk that the complainant was under 16, but went ahead anyway, choosing to do so despite the risk. In other words, the accused was reckless as to the complainant's true age. And the Crown may also demonstrate that the accused never turned their mind to the complainant's age as they proceeded. This too may establish recklessness on the accused's part with respect to the complainant's age. Reckless indifference is a subjective state of mind bespeaking a choice on the part of an accused to treat the complainant's age as irrelevant and to assume the risk associated with their choice: Carbone, at paras. 123, 126-127.

    [176]   I accept Z.’s evidence that she told Mr. Javer she was 14 when they first met online and later 15 when she had her birthday in February. I do not believe she told him she was 16. As a result, I find he knew full well Z.’s age was under 16 because she told him so. He was completely indifferent as to how old she was. He had one purpose and that was to get her to agree to have sex with him, which she did.

    [177]   A final piece of evidence I want to address that was led by the Crown during this trial was the evidence of A.C., Z.’s mom, as to her speaking to Mr. Javer on the phone, while she was with Z., and they had both been sending messages as if they were from Z. on Z.’s phone. I will say, Ms. C.’s evidence is probably moot at this point given my findings of fact and accepting Z.'s evidence and not accepting Mr. Javer's evidence.  He knew exactly how old she was, but I do want to speak about A.C.'s evidence.

    [178]    A.C.’s testified at some point in April, after Z. had disclosed what happened between her and Mr. Javer because of her becoming upset by a question asked by the nurse when she was getting a vaccination at her school in relation to COVID. She testified she was with Z. when Z. and Mr. Javer were texting each other. She was trying to find out information about Mr. Javer because of what she was told happened to Z. in March when they met in person. As a result, some of the texts sent from Z.’s phone were actually sent by A.C., Z.’s mom. These texts were identified in Exhibit 3, pp. 26, 27 and 28, by both A.C. and Z.. Both A.C. and Z. testified that they spoke to Mr. Javer by phone.

    [179]   Ex 6 was an affidavit from D.C. Waugh as to a search he conducted on Cellebrite during the time of these texts between Z. (Z.’s mom) and Mr. Javer in April 2022, after Z.’s mom found out about Mr. Rossi. As a result of his search, he found there were no phone calls from Z.’s phone to Mr. Javer’s cell phone in this period. The last call that DC Waugh found that was answered by Mr. Javer's cell phone number, which is 1-647-695-7770, and that is according to Exhibit 1, the agreed statement of facts and it is on the Cellebrite report, in his affidavit, which is Exhibit 6 in box 18, the last call that was accepted as an outgoing call to Mr. Javer's phone was on March 31st, 2022.     [180]   Most of the calls are on April 13.  It is interesting that also on April 13 there is a text.  And I do not think it was ever identified what number these texts were coming from because in Exhibit 3, whenever it is a yellow text message, which is Mr. Javer, under the column that says, from, it says, "Null.  Null." I do not know that was ever explained in any of the documentation that was provided to me. But not one, every single yellow text says, "Null.  Null." Now, on April 13, this is when Mr. Javer says he knows he is being threatened and extorted by Z. and her mother The first text that he sends is 19:18:08 where he sends it to Z.'s phone number. "Okay.     [181]   In my view, that was a message being sent by Mr. Javer to Z. that she was not going to be able to call him anymore because, this was one of Crown's submissions to me, he was ghosting her.  He had had sex with her. He did not need to have sex with her again, and he was telling her that she would not get any more phone calls.  Now, I do not know whether the private number that calls 15 times to Z.'s phone after I think the first call from this phone starts around April 4 and it goes through to April 20.     [184]   So, taking the totality of the evidence, in my view, the Crown has proven beyond a reasonable doubt in respect of the charges of sexual assault, sexual interference, and invitation to sexual touching Mr. Javer knew that Z.B. was 15. 

    Luring Offence

    [185]   Dealing with the luring offence, I am not going to go through the same detail. I do not think I need to because it is the same test, the exact same test under 276.1(4).  They just do not put the word, all, in front of reasonable steps. They just say reasonable steps. This is an actual child, and Fox makes it very clear, just as Carbone and the W.G. does, that where one is considering not a fictitious child in a sting operation, an actual child, that the three forms of mental mens rea, the fault requirements are applicable. So it is that he knew, the same applies with the luring offence, that he knew that Z. was under 16, that he was wilfully blind that she was 16, or that he was reckless, and I have found that he knew. 

    [186]   The communications with Z. were telecommunications, and although an accused does not have to carry out the specified secondary offence, in this case it was sexual interference, and in this case, Mr. Javer did carry out that offence through his child luring.  His texts were all about him initiating conversations about sex with Z..  His texts and his contact with her for over four and a half months, I have found, as I have said previously, were instances of grooming on his part. That is discussed in Legare that I have talked about already.  It went as far in terms of his grooming of her that Z. believed she needed to ask permission from him to touch herself, and he talked about how they would handle "Ivy" if she came out during sexual relations that they were having.  In my view, the Crown has proved beyond a reasonable doubt Mr. Javer's guilt respecting the luring offence as well.  And I make that finding on all of the elements of the offence, he facilitated, it was through telecommunications, and even though the offence did not need to be committed, it was committed. 

    [187]   The telecommunications were grooming behaviour on his part, and they were sexual in nature, even though they did not need to be.  She clearly, during the time that these communications were taking place, she trusted him to the point where she invited him to come to Barrie.  He did have sex as a 25-year-old man and with a 15-year-old girl.  And in those circumstances, the Crown, in my view, has proven that charge beyond a reasonable doubt as well.  There will be convictions in respect of all four charges.
