    L. KIM J.:

    Introduction

    [1]         B.B. stands charged that on March 24, 2020, he committed the offences of sexual interference, sexual assault and making available sexually explicit material to the complainant whom he believed to be under the age of 14 years old for the purposes of facilitating the commission of an offence under section 271 of the Criminal Code. The complainant, S.W., was 12 years old at the time of the allegations, 15 years old at trial commencement and 16 years old by the second day of this trial. 

    [2]         The Crown alleges that B.B. volunteered to babysit the complainant and her younger brother, as well as watch horror movies with her, while her mother was making plans that evening which included an arrangement to pick up B.B.’s partner, C.E., from Espanola. B.B. and C.E. were in positions of trust in relation to 12-year-old S.W., who looked up to him as an uncle, and her as a second mother.

    [3]         It is alleged that while B.B. was home alone with S.W. and her younger brother, he showed her graphic pornography videos on his phone before lifting up her shirt and sucking her breasts. He is also alleged to have then “French kissed” S.W. while having her lay down on a bed before removing the complainant’s pants and underwear and performing oral sex on her. The Crown also alleges that B.B. then sucked on S.W.’s breasts for a second time while they were both seated in the living room.

    [4]         This trial commenced before me on August 16, November 10, December 18, 2023, February 5, and February 16, 2024.  The Crown called the complainant, S.W., and her mother, J.W. The Defence called the accused, B.B., and his girlfriend, C.E.

    [5]         The dates of these allegations stem from March 24, 2020. In addition to issues surrounding the credibility of the witnesses, the reliability of the evidence presented will also form a foundational part of the Court’s analysis.

    Testimonial Aids – CCTV and Support Person

    [6]         Prior to calling the complainant as their first witness, Ms. Whillans, on behalf of the Crown, indicated that on consent, S.W. would be testifying by CCTV and with the assistance of a support person.

    The Nature of the Relationship Between B.B. and S.W.

    [7]         According to S.W., she had met B.B. on one occasion prior to the alleged incident of March 24, 2020. She explained that her mother is a longtime friend of C.E., the partner of B.B. S.W. had known C.E. from a very young age and looked up to her as a second mother. On the single occasion of prior contact with B.B., S.W. had met him for the first time during an overnight visit at her place with C.E. After staying the night, B.B. and C.E. departed the next day to go look at their new trailer in Espanola. In S.W.’s best estimation, this happened not too long prior to the events in question of March 2020, but she is not sure exactly when.

    [8]         She described her relationship with B.B. as “an okay relationship” and perceived him as an uncle. From her perspective, there were no incidents of conflict or arguments with B.B. and they “got along pretty nicely.”

    [9]         J.W. corroborated S.W.’s characterization of how it came to be that S.W. and her younger sibling met B.B. More specifically, J.W. described B.B. as “awesome” and “really trustworthy” in the week and a half after she was introduced to him by her trusted friend, C.E. Because of this positive nature of the relationship, both S.W. and G.W. referred to B.B. as “Uncle B.” According to J.W., her two children never had fathers in their lives, and this likely fueled their desire to become emotionally attached to B.B. after only meeting him on one prior occasion, approximately a week prior to the alleged incident. 

    [10]      The evidence is undisputed that the nature of the relationship with B.B. is one of a trusted family friend, who S.W., a child complainant, looked up to as an uncle. Indeed, in B.B.’s own testimony, he admitted that the children referred to him as “Uncle B.” The existence of this relationship of trust is not in dispute when one considers B.B.’s connection to C.E., whom the complainant saw as a second mother in the lead up to March 2020.

     

    The Babysitting Arrangements involving B.B. on March 24, 2020

    [11]      S.W. testified in chief that she was unsure where she was earlier that day, but that she recalls being away from home for most of the day and returning home in her mother’s vehicle with her younger brother. When her mother pulled into their residential driveway in Levack, she observed B.B. to be situated in their driveway in his truck with C.E.’s dog. S.W. recalls her mother greeting him in the driveway and being very friendly with B.B. He was then invited into the residence. 

    [12]      However, during cross-examination, Mr. Michel pointed out that in S.W.’s video statement to police, she told Cst. Jesse Sell that she, along with her mother and G.W. were already home and inside when B.B. arrived there unannounced. The difference here is that in her video statement, S.W. was already present at home and not in the process of pulling into the driveway in her mother’s vehicle and seeing B.B. waiting in her driveway. Nor did she tell the officer that her mother had carried her younger brother from her vehicle to the house, but this was her testimony in court.

    [13]      S.W. admitted to this inconsistency in what she initially told Cst. Sell in her video statement and her testimony in court before me. Her explanation was, “It’s not true from how I remember it today.” This inconsistency speaks to S.W.’s reliability and will be addressed in more detail later in these Reasons. 

    [14]      While inside, S.W. recalls that her mother commented to B.B. that she needed a babysitter because she had intended to go grocery shopping and also pick up C.E. in Espanola and return to Sudbury. S.W. was rather unsure in explaining the reasons for why her mother needed a babysitter but, in her words, she is “90 percent sure” the reason stated here is accurate.

    [15]      According to S.W., B.B. volunteered to babysit her and her younger brother and had suggested that he could watch horror movies with S.W. which included the movie “Jigsaw.” Throughout this entire interaction, she explained that her four-year-old brother was asleep, and he had been brought into the residential living room from their vehicle by her mother. 

    [16]      S.W. suggests that within the first minute of walking through the front door, that B.B. had offered to babysit her and her young brother. After this arrangement was made, S.W. explained that her mother then departed their home with B.B.’s pickup truck to go get groceries and pick up C.E. in Espanola. 

    [17]      When cross-examined by Mr. Michel on precisely when her mother departed and returned, S.W. was not able to provide a specific hour but only that it was beginning to get dark, and that she doesn’t remember if it was prior to or after supper. She guessed that it might have been around 7:00 pm. She also testified that she went to bed around 10:00 p.m. and that her mother returned home after she went to bed. Because she was asleep when her mother returned, she was unable to pinpoint a specific time other than a general range of after 10:00 pm, to as late as the following morning at 9:30 to 10:00 a.m. and possibly the following morning.  

    [18]      During cross-examination, S.W. admitted that she told the police officer in her video statement that her mother was calling around searching for a babysitter because she was going on a date. However, in court she does not have an independent recollection of her mother mentioning that she was going on a date. Instead, she remembers her mother discussing the need to go grocery shopping and it was for this reason, she was in need of a babysitter.

    [19]      Also, during cross examination, Mr. Michel pointed out that in S.W.’s video statement to police, she stated that while they were inside the home, her mother had asked her if she would be okay with B.B. babysitting her. This is different than what she told the Crown in their examination in chief. S.W. disputes that this conversation ever took place in her testimony. 

    [20]      In response, S.W. insisted that the description of how it came to be that B.B. was at her residence and ended up babysitting her was as she explained it in court. She maintains that after being absent for most of the day, and likely returning from Espanola, when she pulled into the driveway in her mother’s car, B.B. was already present. She also maintained that the reason for her mother requiring the assistance of a babysitter was because she was going grocery shopping, to the best of her knowledge, and that the childcare arrangements involving B.B. were discussed and decided within a very brief period of time.

    [21]      Also, during cross-examination, S.W. admitted that she made no mention in her video statement to police that her mother had carried her younger brother, who was asleep, from their car to the home, shortly after arriving home and discovering B.B. to be in his truck in their driveway.

    [22]      According to S.W., these discrepancies in her evidence is a byproduct of feeling rushed and confused on the day she provided her video statement to police in April 2020.

    The Presence of Inadvertent Tainting

    [23]      Inadvertent tainting impacts a witness’ reliability and is succinctly described by Justice Nordheimer of the Ontario Court of Appeal at paragraph 28 of G.(C.):

    ““inadvertent collusion,” occurs when one witness discusses the events with another witness with the consequences that the evidence of one or both of them may be altered. Put another way, a witness’ evidence may be “inadvertently” impacted by the fact that they have heard the evidence of other witnesses which “can have the effect, whether consciously or unconsciously, of colouring and tailoring their descriptions of the impugned events”[1]

    [24]      In my view, there is no reason why the impact of inadvertent tainting would be limited to discussions with witnesses only. Throughout her testimony, S.W. has admitted to speaking to her stepmother, her father, J.W., her counsellor, Constable Jesse Sell, and the Crown, prior to testifying before me for the first two days of this trial. As a 15-year-old teen, recalling events dating back to when she was 12, and having to repeat and discuss those details with these people, appears to have had an impact on her ability to reliably remember certain details. 

    [25]      As it relates to S.W.’s reliability, I find that there was inadvertent tainting of S.W.’s evidence that impacted her ability to recall the facts leading up to the alleged offences, specifically, as it relates to how it came to be that B.B. arrived at her home and had volunteered to babysit her and her brother. The question is what impact this inadvertent tainting has on S.W.’s reliability on certain aspects of her recollection.

    [26]      I have considered the inconsistent responses offered by the complainant on the above factual points. I do not place much weight in the discrepancies as to why S.W.’s mother required childcare on the date of these alleged sexual assaults. Whether that need arose out of a need to go grocery shopping or to go out on a personal date or to traffic in illicit drugs, is of little relevance. These were adult decisions far outside the control of the child complainant on the date in question.

    [27]      While I appreciate the need to test S.W.’s memory as a whole, and the potential impact this may have on her credibility and  reliability, the extent of questioning in so far as how it came to be that B.B. had been permitted to babysit S.W. and her younger brother places little weight in my overall interpretation of her evidence in light of how she would perceive those circumstances as a child witness.

    [28]      Whether B.B. was waiting in the driveway in his truck or arrived while S.W. was inside the home and how her younger brother was moved from her mother’s vehicle to their home while asleep relate primarily to S.W.’s reliability. Those factual aspects of S.W.’s evidence are ancillary to the core allegations of a sexual nature S.W. has made against B.B., and I accept this inconsistency to be an innocent by-product of the passage of time on an ancillary factual point.

    [29]      Furthermore, it is of little consequence whether S.W.’s mother pulled her daughter aside to check with her if it was okay for B.B. to babysit her, which S.W. maintains did not happen. 

    [30]      It is also uncontradicted and I find as a fact, that the mother needed childcare, and the eventual outcome of that need was B.B. offering to fulfill that responsibility to babysit S.W. and her younger brother at their residence on the day of these allegations.

    [31]      However, what these discrepancies in S.W.’s prior statement do provide me is reason to be cautious and aware of the possibility that the mother of the complainant had attempted to influence the testimony of a child prior to that child meeting with police to provide a video statement in relation to the current allegations. 

    [32]      In addition to inadvertent tainting, the Court heard evidence of advertent collusion, which will be discussed in more detail below. The suggestion that a parent may have told or influenced the evidence of a child complainant must be considered in my overall analysis of the evidence, with particular attention to how this might have impacted the veracity and reliability of S.W. ’s testimony in court throughout this trial.

    [33]      I wish to be clear that my examination of S.W.’s evidence throughout this trial is assessed with this particular caution in mind.

    [34]      Having considered these factors, I find that S.W.’s mother did not pull her aside and seek her approval in advance of this happening.

    [35]      I conclude that it is inconsequential whether the complainant’s description of how it came to be that B.B. arrived at her home and ended up babysitting her on the day of these allegations is precisely aligned with that of J.W. or B.B. I need not make a finding of fact on this given that it is not in dispute what those arrangements eventually looked like leading up to the alleged offences.  

    [36]      I also need not determine precisely why S.W.’s mother required a babysitter on the day of the alleged incident. The specific reason for J.W.’s absence that evening in my determination of the facts is of limited probative value. Having said that, I acknowledge in J.W.’s own evidence, she admitted it was because she had to leave S.W. and her younger brother at home to sell illicit drugs in order to support her and her children financially. I wish to make it clear that the Court is not casting judgment on the mother’s choices in this regard.

    [37]      I am satisfied that J.W. was in need of childcare on the date of the alleged incident and that B.B. had volunteered to babysit S.W. which presented an opportunity for him to commit these alleged offences. I am also satisfied that B.B.’s expectation was that J.W. would return a few hours later with C.E. from Espanola.

    [38]      Additionally, I find that B.B. was ultimately given this childcare responsibility because of the pre-existing relationship of trust that had been established between C.E. and the accused with S.W., G.W., and their mother.

    J.W. and B.B. departing the home to purchase and consume cocaine

    [39]      In cross-examination, Mr. Michel asked S.W. if she remembers her mother leaving her home with B.B. and returning with cocaine and alcohol. S.W. repeatedly denied that this took place and insisted that:

    i)            B.B. had never left her residence after arriving there on March 24th, 2020; and

    ii)            While B.B. was at her residence, he molested her.

    [40]      When asked if she remembers her mother cooking the cocaine over the stove, S.W. insisted that this also never happened.  Mr. Michel pursued this line of questioning to challenge the reliability of her memory going back some three and a half years.

    [41]      My assessment of S.W.’s evidence on this factual point must include a recognition of the contextual reality of S.W.’s perception as a 12-year-old child at the time, and the subject-matter of drug use and her mother’s drug dealing that likely would not have been deliberately exposed to her by her mother and B.B. on the day in question.

    [42]      The evidentiary record from each of the adult witnesses that testified in this trial, points to the fact that B.B. was actively engaged in the consumption of crack cocaine on the date of these alleged events, and that this drug habit may have contributed to his unexpected visit to S.W.’s home, in order to purchase this drug from her mother. Upon making arrangements for that drug transaction, both the consumer (B.B.) and the seller (J.W.) testified that they briefly left the home to pick up the crack cocaine and access cash from a bank machine to pay for it.

    [43]      It does not appear that S.W. was aware that B.B. and J.W. had left the home to carry out this drug transaction. At the time, J.W. had just had her two children, including S.W., returned to her by Kina Gbezhgomi, an Indigenous-based child protection agency, just four to five months prior. None of this would have impacted S.W.’s recollection of what actually transpired that evening because it is highly improbable that she was in the immediate vicinity of her mother and B.B. for each and every conversation the adults had regarding their pending drug transaction.  

    [44]      It makes little sense to me as how and why S.W. would have knowledge of this aspect of events on the date in question. There is no evidence before me that J.W. or B.B. told S.W. about this brief absence from the residence in furtherance of an illicit drug transaction.

    [45]      Put another way, there is no evidentiary link that draws the complainant into any of her mother’s illegal drug trafficking activities with B.B. on the day or evening in question, and its probative value in so far as my credibility or reliability assessment on the complainant herself is if little value to this Court.

    Clothing Provided by S.W. to Police – Forensic Analysis 

    [46]      S.W. stated in her evidence that she provided to police the clothing that she might have been wearing at the time of this alleged incident. Photos of these clothing items are provided in Exhibit #1 and these conclusions are found in the CFS Report of Ronald Lai, a forensic scientist in his report dated June 1/23 filed as Exhibit #4.

    The Allegation of Making Sexually Explicit Material Available to S.W. to Facilitate Sexual Assault

S.W. repeatedly rejected this evidence and insisted that each aspect of this line of questioning was untrue.     Credibility of J.W.

    [96]      I have carefully reviewed the transcript of J.W.'s testimony in this trial. She candidly admitted to engaging in drug trafficking at the time it is alleged that B.B. sexually touched her 12-year-old daughter. J.W.'s evidence included many of her own personal struggles that touched upon financial setbacks, substance misuse, and the combination of these factors in how they impacted her ability to parent.

    [97]      It is undoubtedly a very challenging time in J.W.'s life as a single mother of two young children. She had only had her children back in her care from Kina Gbezhgomi for three to four months before this alleged incident. She openly discussed her financial struggles as a justification for her need to sell drugs to support herself and her children. To her credit, she also exhibited many aspects of resilience and courage in turning her life around and achieving sobriety from substances today.

    [98]      While I sympathize with J.W.'s personal journey, I must identify concerns I have with her evidence in this trial. S.W.'s own testimony speaks to times when her mother told her what to say to police in support of these allegations of a sexual nature brought against B.B. However, J.W. testified that she never once discussed details of these allegations with her daughter. She further asserts that she never told her daughter what to say to police and certainly did not discuss with her aspects of this case in order to have their stories match up.

    [99]      I do not accept J.W.’s evidence on this point and prefer the evidence of S.W. as it relates to the existence of collusion between the two of them. I find that J.W. had repeatedly told S.W. what to say to police and was aware that some of her suggested information to S.W. was knowingly false or exaggerated.

    [100]   I also find that J.W. did so while she had primary care and responsibility of S.W. and that at the time of these allegations, J.W. had a significant influence over her 12-year-old daughter.

    [101]   In my own observations of J.W., she did not seem to appreciate the serious problem with lying to the police about why she left her children alone with B.B., namely that she was going out for a “girl’s night out” when in fact she was actively selling and delivering drugs in and around the Sudbury community.

    [102]   J.W. readily admits to misleading the police in her video statement. Similarly, I find that she was more than willing to mislead the police in these allegations by explicitly telling her daughter, the complainant, what to say to them.

    [103]   I find J.W.’s testimony to be highly problematic. To the extent that there is any conflict in the evidence of J.W. and S.W., I prefer the evidence of S.W. and reject J.W.'s testimony.

    [104]   I do not accept J.W.’s answers as expressed to Mr. Michel in cross-examination when she denies telling S.W. what to say to the police.     Law and Analysis

    [171]   B.B. is presumed innocent, and the burden falls solely on the Crown to prove guilt at the high standard of beyond a reasonable doubt. It is not enough for me to believe that he is probably or likely guilty.

    [172]   B.B. chose to waive his right to silence, and he testified in his own defence. He denied sexually assaulting S.W. both during his evidence in chief and while cross-examined by the Crown. This requires me to assess his credibility in the context of the totality of evidence in this trial.

    [173]   In assessing the evidence, I remind myself of the differences between credibility and reliability.  Credibility relates to the honesty or veracity of a witness. Specifically, whether the witness is expressing themselves truthfully to the best of their abilities.

    [174]   Reliability relates to the accuracy of the witness’ testimony. In determining this, I must consider their ability to accurately observe, recall and recount the events in issue.  A credible witness may give unreliable evidence[13]. Accordingly, there is a distinction between a finding of credibility and proof beyond a reasonable doubt.[14]

    [175]   In assessing the credibility of a witness, I am to consider any inconsistencies in what a witness said at different times.  Inconsistencies on minor matters or matters of detail are normal and do not generally affect the credibility of a witness. However, one or more inconsistencies involving material matters can justify concluding that the witness is neither credible nor reliable.  In determining this, it is necessary to look to the totality of the inconsistencies.[15]

    [176]   The principles set out by the Supreme Court of Canada in R. v. W.(D)[16] provide a requisite framework in determining whether the Crown has proven B.B. 's guilt beyond a reasonable doubt:

    1.            If I accept B.B.’s evidence I must acquit him;

    2.            If I do not believe B.B., but his evidence leaves me with a reasonable doubt on any essential element of the offence, I must acquit;

    3.            Finally, even if B.B.’s evidence does not leave me with a reasonable doubt, if after considering all the evidence that I do accept, I am not satisfied beyond a reasonable doubt of his guilt, I must acquit.

    [177]   As the trier of fact, I may believe some, none, or all of the testimony of any witness, including that of an accused.

    [178]   I must also remind myself that if, after a careful consideration of all of the evidence, I am unable to decide whom to believe, I must acquit.[17]

    [179]   In applying the test in W.(D), the totality of the evidence must be considered. This was discussed by Justice Campbell [as he then was], in R. v. E.M.W., 2009 NSPC 33, aff’d 2011 SCC 31 at para. 47:

    “...It is not only appropriate, but necessary for judges to consider all the sources of reasonable doubt. The sources may include the doubt left by the complainant's evidence, the doubt created by the evidence of the accused, the doubt found in any other evidence or the doubt arising from the combination of those sources.”

    Assessing the Evidence of Children

    [180]   At trial, the complainant was between 15 to 16 years old but testified to events that she recalled happened when she was 12 years old. As it relates to child witnesses, I am mindful of the instructions by the Supreme Court of Canada in R. v. B.G., 1990 CanLII 7308 (SCC), [1990] 2 S.C.R. 30 at para. 48:

    “…regarding the credibility of child witnesses, it seems to me that he was simply suggesting that the judiciary should take a commonsense approach when dealing with the testimony of young children and not impose the same exacting standard on [page55] them as it does on adults. However, this is not to say that the courts should not carefully assess the credibility of child witnesses and I do not read his reasons as suggesting that the standard of proof must be lowered when dealing with children as the appellants submit. Rather, he was expressing concern that a flaw, such as a contradiction, in a child's testimony should not be given the same effect as a similar flaw in the testimony of an adult. I think his concern is well founded and his comments entirely appropriate. While children may not be able to recount precise details and communicate the when and where of an event with exactitude, this does not mean that they have misconceived what happened to them and who did it. [emphasis added] In recent years we have adopted a much more benign attitude to children's evidence, lessening the strict standards of oath taking and corroboration, and I believe that this is a desirable development. The credibility of every witness who testifies before the courts must, of course, be carefully assessed but the standard of the "reasonable adult" is not necessarily appropriate in assessing the credibility of young children.”

    [181]   Two years after the release of B.G., Madame Justice B. McLachlin, as she then was, in W.(R),[18] reminded us of two notable developments in the law affecting the analysis of evidence from children.

    [182]   The first was to remove any notion that the evidence of children was inherently unreliable and must therefore be treated with special caution. As a result of this change in the law, the requirement that a child's evidence be corroborated has been removed from the Criminal Code. Justice McLaughlin made it clear at the end of paragraph 23 in W.(R), that “…if a court proceeds to discount a child's evidence automatically, without regard to the circumstances of the particular case, it will have fallen into error.”

    [183]   The second notable change in the law towards the evidence of children was a clear understanding that it would be wrong to apply adult tests in assessing credibility to the evidence of children[19]. Justice McLaughlin goes on to explain that children may experience the world differently from adults and that we should not be surprised that details important to adults such as time and place may be missing from the memory of a child. Trial judges are instructed to take a common-sense approach and to not apply the same exacting standard that we would with respect to adult witnesses. This was a reiteration of the Supreme Court's decision in B.G. two years prior.

    Collusion and Tainting Legally Defined

    [184]   The testimony of S.W. includes references to J.W. telling her what to say when they discussed these allegations prior to reporting to police. I am alive to the direct implications this has on S.W.’s credibility and reliability with respect to the core facts connected to the four distinct acts of sexual touching and the viewing of pornography.

    [185]   As discussed above, Justice Nordheimer in G.(C.) has provided us with clear definitions of what constitutes deliberate, or advertent collusion and unintentional, or inadvertent tainting.[20] Both are applicable in this case at bar:

    “[28] As I have intimated, the term "collusion" has been used to describe two different phenomena. The first is deliberate or "advertent collusion", that is, where witnesses get together and fashion their evidence in concert in order to appear to be reciting a consistent and reliable story. The other, commonly referred to as "inadvertent collusion", occurs where one witness discusses the events with another witness with the consequence that the evidence of one or both of them may be altered. Put another way, a witness' evidence may be "inadvertently" impacted by the fact that they have heard the evidence of other witnesses which "can have the effect, whether consciously or unconsciously, of colouring and tailoring their descriptions of the impugned events": R. v. B. (C.), 2003 CanLII 32894 (ON CA), [2003] O.J. No. 11, 171 C.C.C. (3d) 159 (C.A.), at para. 40. This point was made in R. v. F. (J.), 2003 CanLII 52166 (ON CA), [2003] O.J. No. 3241, 177 C.C.C. (3d) 1 (C.A.), where Feldman J.A. said, at para. 77:

    -      The reliability of a witness's account can be undermined not only by deliberate collusion for the purpose of concocting evidence, but also by the influence of hearing other people's stories, which can tend to colour one's interpretation of personal events or reinforce a perception about which one had doubts or concerns.

    [29] While the issue of collusion most often arises when a court is considering the admissibility of similar fact evidence, it is an issue that has relevance to the evaluation of a witness' evidence in general.

    [30] As this case illustrates, it is unfortunate that the term "inadvertent collusion" has been coined. As I will explain, "advertent collusion" affects the credibility of evidence. "Inadvertent collusion", or accidental tainting, does not do so. It affects only the reliability of such evidence. As a result, an entirely different analysis is required in determining the impact that "inadvertent collusion" may have on the evidence in question. Yet the term "inadvertent collusion" obscures this because the term "collusion" connotes conspiracy, which is a credibility concern. It would be better if the term "inadvertent collusion" was avoided and replaced by the term "inadvertent tainting". Given that the term "inadvertent collusion" was used during the matter before us, I will continue to refer to "inadvertent collusion" but as I say, that is a term that would best be avoided going forward.

    [31] I will begin with advertent collusion. It is self-evident that this first form of collusion is particularly problematic. Deliberate collusion among witnesses will inevitably undermine the credibility of the evidence given. No court would be comfortable relying on evidence from witnesses who have gotten together and decided on what they are going to say when questioned under oath, at [page731] least not without independent corroborating evidence establishing that their evidence is reliable, notwithstanding the collusion.

    [32] The second form of collusion, "inadvertent collusion", is more difficult. The fact that one witness has heard what another witness will say, or for that matter has even discussed what another person's recollections were, does not mean that either witness is not telling the truth, or is not giving their independent recollection, or that their evidence has been tainted. Indeed, even where the evidence of one of the parties to the discussion is inadvertently affected by what another person has said, the account of that other person may not change. For example, in this case, it is possible that when, during the joint meeting with the Defence lawyer, the appellant's wife offered reasons why the appellant could not be guilty, the appellant did innocently incorporate those reasons into his testimony, but that does not mean that his wife's observations were inaccurate or that her testimony had changed as a result of their discussion. Nor does it necessarily follow that the appellant's testimony became unreliable. He may have recognized from his own knowledge the truth and importance of what his wife had observed. The key point is that, unlike advertent collusion which corrupts the evidence of all participants, where inadvertent collusion has occurred, a close examination is required to determine what impact that innocent sharing of information may have had on the evidence of each of the witnesses who is a party to the exchange. As Sopinka J. said in R. v. Burke, 1996 CanLII 229 (SCC), [1996] 1 S.C.R. 474, [1996] S.C.J. No. 27, at para. 45:

     

    • Under this approach, the trier of fact is obliged to consider the reliability of the evidence having regard to all the circumstances, including the opportunities for collusion or collaboration to concoct the evidence and the possibility that these opportunities were used for such a purpose.

     

    (Emphasis in original)”

(4th) 375, at para. 31, aff’d 2020 SCC 39, [2020] 3 S.C.R. 780, the Alberta Court of Appeal observed that triers of fact may rely on reason and common sense, life experience, and logic in assessing credibility. (3d) 293, the same court held that triers of fact “must invariably fall back on their common sense, and their acquired knowledge about human behaviour in assessing the credibility and reliability of witnesses” (para. 6). Finally, in R. v.     Advertent Collusion between S.W. and J.W.

    [225]   As the Court of Appeal stated in G. (C.),[32] advertent collusion is deliberate and takes place when witnesses get together and tailor their evidence together in order to present a consistent and reliable story. Advertent collusion directly impacts a witnesses’ credibility.

    [226]   There are four aspects of S.W.’s evidence where it is clear to me that there was advertent collusion between S.W. and her mother. In each of those areas, J.W. had discussed details of the alleged events with S.W. that touched upon the events leading up to the sexual interference and the sexual touching itself.

She admitted during questioning that she told the police in her statement that after the initial disclosure to her mother, that she speculated with J.W. about what B.B. might have done to her while she was sleep.     The Impact of Collusion and Other Materially Inconsistent Statements from S.W.

    [241]   I re-iterate that there is no evidence that speaks to precisely when S.W. was purported to have attempted to scream throughout the course of the four distinct acts of sexual interference on March 24, 2020, in her video statement to police.

    [243]   This is not inconsistent with what she testified to before me on the first two days of this trial. I find that this suggested point of inconsistency in S.W.’s video statement is not made out.  

    [244]   Accordingly, I find that S.W.’s general admission to falsely stating in her video that B.B. covered her mouth when she attempted to scream is in no way anchored to her specific description of how and what B.B. did to her when he initially invited her into the room, told her where to sit, showed her pornography, lifted up her shirt to suck on her breasts, and discussed specific details connected to his own experience of child sexual abuse.

    [245]   I am alert to the existence of advertent collusion. However, ultimately, I maintain that S.W. was a credible witness throughout this trial. When Mr. Michel or Ms. Whillans put to her that she had said certain things to police or at a Discovery that were untrue, she admitted this to be the case without hesitation, and expanded on why she felt influenced by her mother to say certain things that she knew to be inaccurate.

    [246]   Due to the detailed manner in which S.W. was questioned by both lawyers, it is not difficult to identify what S.W. accepts as being “her story” of what B.B. did to her, and what areas of her prior evidence were exaggerated or falsely stated.

    [247]   She testified in a straightforward and descriptive manner by readily admitting when she could not recall certain details, or when she “zoned out”, or lost feelings of sensations in her leg when it went numb due to the way in which B.B. had performed oral sex upon her. She corrected both lawyers if she felt there was a misstatement or inaccurate characterization or contextualization of what happened from her perspective.

    [248]   As it relates to her reliability, and inadvertent tainting due to discussions of the incident with her mother, there is indeed the potential for unintended or unconscious colouring of her recollection of the events that are not sufficiently corroborated through other evidence.[37] 

    [249]   Her physical description of how B.B. performed oral sex upon her was the subject of vigorous questioning by both lawyers in the two days that S.W. testified. Having considered her evidence in this regard, I do not accept Mr. Michel’s submission that it would have been physically impossible for B.B. to have performed oral sex upon her in the way that she explained.

    [250]   The way in which S.W. explained in yoga terms, “child’s pose” and positioning of her legs in relation to B.B. is completely reasonable. There is nothing on the evidentiary record before me that speaks to this being anatomically impossible. What is highly unlikely is that B.B. remained in this same position for the entirety of the duration when he performed oral sex upon S.W., some 20 minutes, in her mind. It is likely that B.B. initially commenced the act of oral sex with this positioning as described but then would have likely adjusted his positioning throughout the course of this sexual act.

It would also be unrealistic to expect a child of this age to know how long this act took, and precisely where the hands and legs of the perpetrator, or herself, to be specifically positioned throughout the course of this inherently highly invasive sexual act upon her.

    [252]   In consideration of the legal principles in B.G. and W.(R) in relation to the assessment of credibility of child witnesses, to expect that level of recollection from a 15- or 16-year-old girl would turn back the clock in how our courts are expected to interpret and analyze the evidence of children in today’s justice system. 

    [253]   I am aware that at various points in her testimony, S.W. testified to “zoning out”, being frozen or in a state of shock and being unable to move. Most notably, when asked about what she was feeling when B.B. was licking her vagina, S.W. stated that her mind went blank, zoned out from all the sounds, and that she looked up at the ceiling at the little dog on the bed. In doing so, I have rejected the evidence of J.W. when she claims to have never told S.W. what to say at all.

In my respectful view, any facts subsequent to the alleged sucking of S.W.’s breasts and conversation that B.B. and S.W. had at bedside regarding his own sexual victimization as a child, is particularly prone to this unconscious tailoring of S.W.’s recollection due to the lack of independent corroboration.

    Did B.B. touch S.W.’s Breasts after making pornography available to her?

    [263]   I remind myself that although I have made a finding of collusion, this does not automatically render S.W.’s evidence valueless.[41] For ease of reference, I will reproduce paragraph 31 of G.(C.), where the Ontario Court of Appeal makes it clear that where there is advertent collusion, my analysis does not end there. The presence of independent corroborating evidence must still be considered:

    “No court would be comfortable relying on evidence from witnesses who have gotten together and decided on what they are going to say when questioned under oath, at [page731] least not without independent corroborating evidence establishing that their evidence is reliable, notwithstanding the collusion.” [emphasis added]”

    [264]   Here, there are several facts that were expressed by S.W. that are corroborated by Mr. B.B. in his own testimony that supports the reliability of her evidence as it relates to the showing of the pornography and the alleged sucking of her breasts in the bedroom:

    [267]   I am also satisfied that the reliability of S.W.’s evidence as it relates to these same facts has not been tainted despite the potential for inadvertent collusion due to any discussions she may have had with J.W.

    [268]   The similarities in the subject-matter, physical location, logistical organization of the room and the words uttered on very unusual topics of masturbation and B.B.’s own child sexual abuse experience, adds a strong layer of reliability to S.W.’s evidence that is compelling, even in the face of advertent collusion with J.W. detached from this particular line of factual inquiry.

    [269]   I come to this conclusion because in a careful review of the specific areas that S.W.’s admits were a product of what J.W. told her to say, they do not detract away from the reliability in the way S.W. explained how she came to be situated in the bedroom or the details surrounding the showing of pornography and sucking of her breasts. Today, she no longer lives with her mother and has had very little contact with her in over a year. The net impact of this change in S.W.’s life is that it assists me in assessing her testimony before me as evidently free of any influence or input from J.W. in this trial. 

    [295]   As discussed above, I remind myself that the unfolding of events as described by S.W. is corroborated to a significant degree by B.B.’s own testimony. Those facts that align between the testimony provided by S.W. and B.B., add a significant layer of reliability and credibility to her evidence, despite my findings as it relates to advertent collusion in these Reasons.

    [296]   In a careful review of S.W.’s evidence in conjunction with the totality of the record, I am persuaded beyond a reasonable doubt and accept S.W.’s description of how her sexual integrity was violated by B.B. from the moment he arrived up to and including the showing of the pornography, him sucking on her breasts and the discussions that took place immediately afterwards regarding his own sexual victimization as a child. 

    Conclusion

    [297]   I remind myself of the Crown’s burden to prove each of the essential elements of the offences to the standard of beyond a reasonable doubt. In accordance with the principles in W.D., I make the following conclusions having considered the totality of the evidence and applicable legal principles:

    1.            I do not accept the evidence of B.B.

    2.            His evidence as presented in its entirety does not leave me with a reasonable doubt on any of the essential elements of the offences at Counts 1, 2 and 3.

    3.            After considering all of the evidence that I do accept, I am satisfied beyond a reasonable doubt that B.B. committed each of the offences on Counts 1 to 3 as charged.

    [298]   Accordingly, there will be a finding of guilty on Count one on the Information, the offence of Sexual Assault contrary to section 271 of the Criminal Code.

    [299]   I am also making a finding of guilty on Count three, the offence of Making Sexually Explicit Material Available to a Child Under the Age of 14, contrary to section 171.1(1)(b) of the Criminal Code.

    [300]   Count two, which is the offence of Sexual Interference pursuant to section 151(a), is hereby stayed pursuant to the principles in R. v. Kienapple, 1974 CanLII 14 (SCC), [1975] 1 S.C.R. 729.
