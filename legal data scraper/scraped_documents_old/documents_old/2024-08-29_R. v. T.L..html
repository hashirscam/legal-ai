    Introduction

     

    1.           The accused, T.L., stands charged that between the 14th of January and the 26th of March, 2021, he did

    a)      criminally harass the complainant, M.C., by repeatedly communicating with her, and

    b)      intimidate M.C. by persistently following her,

    contrary respectively to sections 264(2) and 423(1)(c) of the Criminal Code of Canada (the “Code”).

    2.           Further, T.L. stands charged that between the 1st of September and the 30th of November 2020, on three separate occasions, he did commit sexual assaults on M.C. contrary to section 271 of the Code.

    3.           Lastly, T.L. stands charged that between the 1st of September and the 30th of November 2020, on two separate occasions, he did commit assaults on M.C. contrary to section 266 of the Code.

    4.           At the outset of hearing the evidence on the trial, T.L., through his counsel, admitted the jurisdiction of the Court to hear the matter, and the ability of M.C. to identify him as the accused perpetrator of the offences allegedly committed upon her.

    5.           At the conclusion of the Crown’s case on October 24, 2023, the Crown consented to the application for a directed verdict of acquittal brought by defence counsel on behalf of the accused concerning the criminal harassment and intimidation offences alleged against T.L. owing to a lack of evidence adduced from the Crown’s witnesses to support findings of guilt in respect of those two charges.

    6.           T.L. is a member of the Canadian military stationed at Garrison Petawawa.  M.C. is a resident of the Pembroke area who was employed for a large portion of the time she was involved with T.L. as a Personal Support Worker (“PSW”) in a home for the aged. Their domestic relationship was relatively short-lived. It lasted roughly from May 2019 to November 2021.  It ended quite badly.

    7.           As with so many criminal trials, the outcome of this one will turn on an assessment of the credibility and reliability of the accounts given by the witnesses called at trial, but with a focus on M.C.’s and T.L.’s testimony, in a classic, ‘he says, she says’ case.

     

    The Relevant Evidence

     

    M.C.:

     

    8.           When M.C. began giving her evidence on February 16, 2023, she was 22 years old.  She was living in Chalk River, ON with her two sons, J. and V.  T.L. is the father of the younger boy, V., born […], 2020.

    9.           M.C. graduated from college and had previously worked as a PSW; however, she was employed at the time of testifying as an esthetician in Pembroke.

    10.      She knew T.L. since 2016 through mutual acquaintances.  They lost contact but reconnected through a dating app in 2019.  They saw one another over the course of that summer and split up around the beginning of the fall of 2019.  They then resumed a period of cohabitation lasting roughly for a year between November 2019 and November 2020.

    11.      M.C. became pregnant with V. in December 2019.  The relationship was “good and healthy” between T.L. and her, she reckoned, until shortly after V.’s birth in September 2020 around the time they resumed intimacy.  As she put it, “Things got worse with V. around.”  T.L. “put hands” on her and became physical with her.  She no longer felt comfortable in the home she shared with T.L. in Petawawa.

    12.      Prior to V.’s birth, M.C. recalled an incident in June 2020 where she tossed T.L.’s cell phone across the floor to him.  He then picked hers up and smashed it on the floor.  She started pushing him toward the door telling him to get out.  In response, he slammed her against a wall.  Her older son, J., then only two years of age, started hitting T.L.

    13.      In terms of their relative statures, M.C. explained that she is 5’, 4” tall, and weighs 160 lbs. whereas T.L. is 5’, 10”, and is just under 300 lbs.  In addition to his training as a soldier, he has in the past wrestled competitively.

    14.      M.C. described her character as a “flight” type – one who would run away from conflict and go to bed.  By contrast, T.L. would rip the blankets off her.  He would insist on eye contact, grab her and make her talk to him.  He would tell her if she listened, things would not happen between them.

    15.      By the end of June 2020, M.C. estimated that T.L. and she would be arguing two to three times per week.  As a result, she told him several times she wanted things to be over.  She ventured it was not a shock to him when she finally ended their relationship in November 2020.

    16.      Hearkening back to when she had V., M.C. testified that T.L. offered her no assistance while she was still recuperating in hospital immediately following their son’s birth. She had undergone a Caesarian section to deliver V. She asked for T.L.’s help in caring for their newborn. T.L. told her he had to sleep. M.C. had to rely on a nurse for assistance.

    17.      T.L. was particularly insensitive when they left the hospital. T.L. took V. and put him in their vehicle. M.C. was sore walking. She began to weep. T.L. told her to stop being “a crybaby” and to just get in the vehicle.

    18.      M.C. later began to speak up for herself but to no avail. T.L. would criticize even the way she got herself into bed using a stool.

    19.      On one occasion, she broke a glass of wine. It spilled on the floor, and she did not clean it up right away. T.L. asked her, “What kind of mother are you? You are a mess.” In response, M.C. asked him to leave but he would not because he said it was “his house”.

    20.      M.C. was crying hysterically. She pushed him away from her. He told her as a result, “You’re abusive. You’re not a good mom. You’re not a good person.” M.C. lamented that she began to feel that perhaps she was all of what he was saying to her.

    21.      On the next occasion M.C. testified that she was assaulted by T.L., after the October incident when she was thrown into the wall and landed on a shoe rack, she was trying to get by him while he was blocking the doorway and screaming at her. She tried to squeeze past him. He put his hands up and said, “Don’t touch me.”

    22.      The next day, T.L. was rustling through the closet looking for something. She told him she had just reorganized it. He wanted diapers. She slid a box of them toward him across the floor. He picked them up and threw them at her back. The box struck her.  She broke down crying. Again, he told her that she was being “a crybaby”.     23.      M.C. recalled speaking to her cousin, B.M., about the diaper box being thrown by T.L.  M.C. showed B.M. the mark on her back caused by the strike during a Facebook call. B.M. was living in Winnipeg at the time.

He had previously taken his phone out to record her granting him permission to take V. to visit his mother.     40.      Initially, M.C. thought that T.L. was happy with her decision to leave him. They were trying to focus on coparenting their young son, V. T.L. stayed on living at the house owned by the military, and for which he paid the rent. M.C. moved to her father’s place in Chalk River.

    41.      Soon after separation, M.C. and T.L. began to have their disagreements again. They argued about when they should begin feeding solids to their baby. She believed this occurred sometime in December 2020.

    42.      She testified that she spoke to the police a second time in December 2020. This was the first occasion when she reported to the authorities the instances of forced anal sex upon her by T.L. As soon as she brought up sex, the military police in Petawawa told her to stop speaking. They would call her back.   On the first occasion she met with police, she gave only a written statement without mention of anything sexual.

    43.      Originally, M.C. and T.L. came to an informal custody arrangement between themselves for V. Their infant son would spend two days with her and then two days with him. They did their exchanges without the assistance of an intermediary, but after a couple of instances where M.C. believed she was being followed in her vehicle by T.L., M.C.’s father, D.C., became involved in the pick up and drop off of V.

    44.      Under cross-examination, M.C. agreed that her relationship with T.L. was a rocky one. They had begun to cohabit in his apartment on the Petawawa military base during the summer of 2019, but she moved out and back with her father by the end of August of that year. They remained in contact and rekindled their spark by November 2019. They resumed cohabiting in his apartment by the next month in December.

    45.      In February 2020, they moved from the apartment to a private married quarters (“PMQ”) on base. By this point, M.C. knew she was pregnant with V.

    46.      T.L. and she were getting along well, but things deteriorated as the birth of their child approached. She estimated that she said it a handful of times before she actually moved out. He proposed marriage to her to try to reconcile, but later he would fall back to his old ways, insult her and call her “a fat pig”.

    47.      Eventually, she gave back the engagement ring he had given to her. She tossed it at a cupboard in the living room/hallway area of their home. The gem partially came out of it due to the impact with the cupboard. T.L. was going to get it fixed, but she never wore it again.

    48.      She placed this incident as having occurred in June 2020, a few weeks before her older son J.’s second birthday.

    49.      M.C. recalled that she left the PMQ she shared with T.L. on November 24, 2020, exactly one week after he left for Montréal with their son V. to visit his mother. M.C. denied that T.L. decided it was time for her to leave. It was her own decision to do so. He went to Montréal to give her time and space to pack and to leave.

    50.      On November 18, 2020, she texted him to tell him she had rented a U-Haul to move her belongings. She took all of hers, as well as her older son J.’s, but left half of her younger son V.’s. She maintained that T.L. did not want her to go, but she wanted him to get help. She was clear about the fact that T.L. was “a good dad”, but he was not a good partner to her.

    51.      When cross-examination resumed for M.C. on May 4, 2023, she testified that she understood the importance of being complete and truthful in her testimony. She confirmed that she also remembered what she had said on her first day in Court on February 23, 2023.

    52.      She was aware that she gave a written statement to police on December 12, 2020. She provided an audio/video statement to police later on December 16, 2020, followed by a further audio/video statement she gave on March 27, 2021.

    57.      Defence counsel then drew her attention to page 3 of her six page, written statement she gave to police. In it, she spoke of throwing a ring while she was “outside in the grass”.     67.      She acknowledged that she told police she is a very emotional person, whereas T.L. was not.  Postpartum she was at times more so while her hormones were flaring, but what she wanted was stability and to live in a caring, supportive environment.

    Assessing Credibility and Reliability:

    400.   A couple of months ago, in R. v. R.P. 2024 ONCJ 240, I commented on how greatly assisted I was by the decision of the Supreme Court of Canada in R. v. Kruk, 2024 SCC 7 as follows:

    551.   Recently, the Supreme Court of Canada hearkened back to the fundamental principles of law to be applied in every criminal trial in Kruk.  The Court held:

    [59]                          The overarching principle of the presumption of innocence, enshrined in s. 11(d) of the Charter, and the correlative principle of the Crown’s burden of proof, must always govern the fact-finding process. The presumption of innocence — a “hallowed principle lying at the very heart of criminal law” (R. v. Oakes, 1986 CanLII 46 (SCC), [1986] 1 S.C.R. 103, at p. 119) — requires the Crown to bear the onus of proving all essential elements of the offence charged, beyond a reasonable doubt, before a conviction may be entered (Osolin). Closely related to the presumption of innocence is the accused’s right to silence as enshrined in s. 11(c) of the Charter, which safeguards human dignity and privacy against processes or reasoning that would compel an accused person to incriminate themselves with their own words (R. v. Noble, 1997 CanLII 388 (SCC), [1997] 1 S.C.R. 874, at paras. 69-78).

    [60]                          Various protections relating to the assessment and weighing of evidence flow from the presumption of innocence and the right to silence. Notably, an accused’s silence at trial may not be treated as evidence of guilt, as such reasoning would violate both principles (Noble, at para. 72). Likewise, it is improper to discount the credibility of accused persons on the basis that, because they face criminal penalties, they will say anything to protect themselves. Though considering the possibility that the accused may have a motive to lie will not necessarily offend this rule, courts should be wary of going further and drawing the “impermissible assumption” that they will do so in all cases (R. v. Laboucan, 2010 SCC 12, [2010] 1 S.C.R. 397, at para. 14). Such reasoning is premised on a supposition of guilt and therefore offends the presumption of innocence (para. 12).

    [61]                          The presumption of innocence also restricts how credibility is assessed in cases of conflicting testimony between defence and Crown witnesses. The analysis of testimony must never be treated as a contest of credibility, and triers of fact need not accept the defence’s evidence or version of events in order to acquit (R. v. Van, 2009 SCC 22, [2009] 1 S.C.R. 716, at para. 23; R. v. W. (D.), 1991 CanLII 93 (SCC), [1991] 1 S.C.R. 742, at p. 757). The burden never shifts to the accused to establish their own innocence, and the onus always lies with the Crown to prove every essential element (R. v. J.H.S., 2008 SCC 30, [2008] 2 S.C.R. 152, at para. 13).

    [62]                          Reasonable doubt applies to credibility assessments such that if the evidence the Crown adduced does not rise to the level required of a criminal conviction, an accused cannot be found guilty simply because they are disbelieved (see W. (D.)). Some elements of the totality of the evidence may give rise to a reasonable doubt, even where much — or all — of the accused’s evidence is disbelieved. Any aspect of the accepted evidence, or the absence of evidence, may ground a reasonable doubt. Moreover, where the trier of fact does not know whether to believe the accused’s testimony, or does not know who to believe, the accused is entitled to an acquittal (J.H.S., at paras. 9-13; R. v. H. (C.W.) (1991), 1991 CanLII 3956 (BC CA), 68 C.C.C. (3d) 146 (B.C.C.A.); R. v. S. (W.D.), 1994 CanLII 76 (SCC), [1994] 3 S.C.R. 521, at p. 533; R. v. Avetysan, 2000 SCC 56, [2000] 2 S.C.R. 745, at para. 19).

    552.   Later, the Court reminded trial judges of their roles in assessing the credibility and reliability of witnesses in the following passages:

    [72]                        It is widely recognized that testimonial assessment requires triers of fact to rely on common-sense assumptions about the evidence. In R. v. Delmas, 2020 ABCA 152, 452 D.L.R. (4th) 375, at para. 31, aff’d 2020 SCC 39, [2020] 3 S.C.R. 780, the Alberta Court of Appeal observed that triers of fact may rely on reason and common sense, life experience, and logic in assessing credibility. In R. v. R.R., 2018 ABCA 287, 366 C.C.C. (3d) 293, the same court held that triers of fact “must invariably fall back on their common sense, and their acquired knowledge about human behaviour in assessing the credibility and reliability of witnesses” (para. 6). Finally, in R. v. S. (R.D.), 1997 CanLII 324 (SCC), [1997] 3 S.C.R. 484, this Court considered that the life experience of trial judges — though of course not a substitute for evidence, and subject to appropriately circumscribed limits — “is an important ingredient in the ability to understand human behaviour, to weigh the evidence, and to determine credibility”, and assists with a “myriad of decisions arising during the course of most trials” (para. 13). Reasoning about how people generally tend to behave, and how things tend to happen, is not only permissible, it is often a necessary component of a complete testimonial assessment.

    [73]                        In turn, common-sense assumptions necessarily underlie all credibility and reliability assessments. Credibility can only be assessed against a general understanding of “the way things can and do happen”; it is by applying common sense and generalizing based on their accumulated knowledge about human behaviour that trial judges assess whether a narrative is plausible or “inherently improbable” (R. v. Kiss, 2018 ONCA 184, at para. 31 (CanLII); R. v. Adebogun, 2021 SKCA 136, [2022] 1 W.W.R. 187, at para. 24; R. v. Kontzamanis, 2011 BCCA 184, at para. 38 (CanLII)). Common sense underpins well-established principles guiding credibility assessment — including the now-universal idea that witnesses who are inconsistent are less likely to be telling the truth — and assists in assessing the scope and impact of particular inconsistencies. Reliability also requires reference to common-sense assumptions about how witnesses perceive, remember, and relay information, invoking generalizations about how individuals tend to present information that they are remembering accurately and completely, as opposed to matters about which they are unsure or mistaken. A trial judge may, for example, infer that a witness was credible yet unreliable because they appeared sincere but displayed indicia that tend to suggest an unclear or uncertain memory (e.g., equivocation, phrases such as “hmm . . . let me see”, long pauses, or failure to provide much detail).

    [81]                        Assessments of credibility and reliability can be the most important judicial determinations in a criminal trial. They are certainly among the most difficult. This is especially so in sexual assault cases, which often involve acts that allegedly occurred in private and hinge on the contradictory testimony of two witnesses. The trial judge, while remaining grounded in the totality of the evidence, is obliged to evaluate the testimony of each witness and to make determinations that are entirely personal and particular to that individual. Credibility and reliability assessments are also context-specific and multifactorial: they do not operate along fixed lines and are “more of an ‘art than a science’” (S. (R.D.), at para. 128; R. v. Gagnon, 2006 SCC 17, [2006] 1 S.C.R. 621). With respect to credibility in particular, while coherent reasons are crucial, it is often difficult for trial judges to precisely articulate the reasons why they believed or disbelieved a witness due to “the complex intermingling of impressions that emerge after watching and listening to witnesses and attempting to reconcile the various versions of events” (Gagnon, at para. 20; see also R. v. R.E.M., 2008 SCC 51, [2008] 3 S.C.R. 3, at para. 28; R. v. G.F., 2021 SCC 20, [2021] 1 S.C.R. 801, at para. 81). The task is further complicated by the trial judge’s ability to accept some, all, or none of a witness’s testimony.

    The Meaning of Proof beyond a Reasonable Doubt:

    402.   In a very recent case I decided, R. v. McLaren 2023 ONCJ 92 (CanLII), I adverted, as I must, to the meaning of “reasonable doubt”.  I had the following to say:

    181.   The standard of proof beyond a reasonable doubt is an age-old principle of criminal law in Canada and virtually every other common law jurisdiction. Triers of fact must be ever conscious of the high burden of proof placed upon the Crown, if ever the accused’s right to be presumed innocent is to be rebutted.

    182.   Recently, my colleague, Brochu J., in R. v. Hawryluk, (unreported), offered these helpful commentaries on the meaning of reasonable doubt:

    [39] Like every person accused of a criminal offence, Mr. Hawryluk begins this trial presumed to be innocent of the offence with which he is charged. That presumption remains intact unless and until the Crown proves beyond a reasonable doubt that he is guilty. It is the Crown that bears the onus of proving the essential elements of the offence beyond a reasonable doubt.  That onus never shifts to the accused.

    [40] Intermingled with the presumption of innocence is the standard of proof required to displace that presumption. To secure a conviction in a criminal case, the Crown must establish each essential element of the charge against the accused beyond reasonable doubt. This standard of proof is very stringent. It is a standard far beyond the civil threshold of proof on a balance of probabilities.

    [41] The expression “proof beyond a reasonable doubt" has no precise definition, but it is well understood. The Supreme Court of Canada outlined a suggested model jury charge in R. v. Lifchus, 1997 CanLII 319 (SCC), [1997] 3 S.C.R. 320. This is the definitive guide for criminal trial courts in Canada. It is worth setting out here verbatim:

    *  The term "beyond a reasonable doubt" has been used for a very long time and is a part of our history and traditions of justice. It is so engrained in our criminal law that some think it needs no explanation, yet something must be said regarding its meaning.

    *  A reasonable doubt is not an imaginary or frivolous doubt. It must not be based upon sympathy or prejudice. Rather, it is based on reason and common sense. It is logically derived from the evidence or absence of evidence.

    *  Even if you believe the accused is probably guilty or likely guilty, that is not sufficient. In those circumstances you must give the benefit of the doubt to the accused and acquit because the Crown has failed to satisfy you of the guilt of the accused beyond a reasonable doubt.

    *  On the other hand you must remember that it is virtually impossible to prove anything to an absolute certainty and the Crown is not required to do so. Such a standard of proof is impossibly high.

    *  In short if, based upon the evidence before the court, you are sure that the accused committed the offence you should convict since this demonstrates that you are satisfied of his guilt beyond a reasonable doubt.

    In situating the criminal standard of proof, “it falls much closer to absolute certainty than to proof on a balance of probabilities”:  Starr, at para. 242, per Iacobucci J.

    315.   Assault, I venture, is one of the most frequently charged offences under the Code.  It is defined as follows:

    265 (1) A person commits an assault when

    317.   In R. v. Barton 2019 SCC 33, Moldaver J. set out the elements of the offence of sexual assault.  He also addressed squarely the legal meaning of consent in an intimate partners context.  He wrote:

    [87]                        A conviction for sexual assault, like any other true crime, requires that the Crown prove beyond a reasonable doubt that the accused committed the actus reus and had the necessary mens rea. A person commits the actus reus of sexual assault “if he touches another person in a sexual way without her consent” (R. v. J.A., 2011 SCC 28, [2011] 2 S.C.R. 440, at para. 23). The mens rea consists of the “intention to touch and knowing of, or being reckless of or wilfully blind to, a lack of consent on the part of the person touched” (R. v. Ewanchuk, 1999 CanLII 711 (SCC), [1999] 1 S.C.R. 330, at para. 42).

    [88]                        “Consent” is defined in s. 273.1(1) of the Code as “the voluntary agreement of the complainant to engage in the sexual activity in question”.[6] It is the “conscious agreement of the complainant to engage in every sexual act in a particular encounter” (J.A., at para. 31), and it must be freely given (see Ewanchuk, at para. 36). This consent must exist at the time the sexual activity in question occurs (J.A., at para. 34, citing Ewanchuk, at para. 26), and it can be revoked at any time (see Code, s. 273.1(2)(e); J.A., at paras. 40 and 43). Further, as s. 273.1(1) makes clear, “consent” is not considered in the abstract. Rather, it must be linked to the “sexual activity in question”, which encompasses “the specific physical sex act”, “the sexual nature of the activity”, and “the identity of the partner”, though it does not include “conditions or qualities of the physical act, such as birth control measures or the presence of sexually transmitted diseases” (R. v. Hutchinson, 2014 SCC 19, [2014] 1 S.C.R. 346, at paras. 55 and 57).

    [89]                        Consent is treated differently at each stage of the analysis. For purposes of the actus reus, “consent” means “that the complainant in her mind wanted the sexual touching to take place” (Ewanchuk, at para. 48). Thus, at this stage, the focus is placed squarely on the complainant’s state of mind, and the accused’s perception of that state of mind is irrelevant. Accordingly, if the complainant testifies that she did not consent, and the trier of fact accepts this evidence, then there was no consent — plain and simple (see Ewanchuk, at para. 31). At this point, the actus reus is complete. The complainant need not express her lack of consent, or revocation of consent, for the actus reus to be established (see J.A., at para. 37).

    [90]                        For purposes of the mens rea, and specifically for purposes of the defence of honest but mistaken belief in communicated consent, “consent” means “that the complainant had affirmatively communicated by words or conduct her agreement to engage in [the] sexual activity with the accused” (Ewanchuk, at para. 49). Hence, the focus at this stage shifts to the mental state of the accused, and the question becomes whether the accused honestly believed “the complainant effectively said ‘yes’ through her words and/or actions” (ibid., at para. 47).

     

    Analysis

     

    Do I believe T.L.?

    318.   I believe T.L.’s evidence with one small reservation, which I will address later in my reasons, but at the outset, I will elaborate why I found his testimony to be credible, insofar as it addressed the five charges left before me to consider.

    319.   Firstly, I found T.L. to be candid, honest and forthright in his insistence that he was not the instigator of any physical aggression in the relationship he shared with his former partner, M.C.  His testimony remained consistent in its core aspects. He was unshaken under close cross-examination by Crown counsel.  I fully accept his evidence that he only laid hands on M.C. to stop her from hurting herself or him.

    320.   Secondly, he conceded what was potentially harmful to his defence such as:

    a)      the existence of bruises on the body of M.C. over the relevant time frame,

    b)      the choking,

    c)      the anal sex,

    d)      his reaction to spilled wine, grapes and/or blueberries, and

    e)      his response to the throwing of the Monster can at him.

    321.   He offered what I find were credible, innocent explanations for what he did and why he did what he did under the circumstances.

    322.   Thirdly, I could not, as urged by Crown counsel, find a basis for rejection of the testimony of T.L. The alleged assault with the diaper box was denied by T.L., but it was not of a blanket variety.  He offered that he could have struck her with a cardboard box, but he was not certain if he did or not.  It was his only explanation for how possibly she could have suffered a bruise to her back.  It was not an outlandish explanation.  It was, strictly speaking, speculative on his part.

    323.   However, it must be remembered that M.C. was challenged under cross-examination by the defence that the bruise was caused by a cardboard box T.L. kicked down the stairs.  M.C. denied it, but if T.L. had not testified to the incident, the Court would have been left to ponder the relevance of the suggestion put to M.C. by defence counsel under cross-examination.

    324.   The defence did not challenge the existence of the bruise.  M.C.’s cousin, B.M., was able to confirm its presence, which she saw, but M.C. could offer no ‘eye witness’ explanation as to its cause.  I can place no evidentiary value in what M.C. told B.M. was the cause, lest I fall into error for admitting into evidence a prior inconsistent statement other than for the exceptional purpose of rebutting recent fabrication.

    325.   B.M.’s testimony was otherwise of little value to the Crown’s case.

    326.   In essence, I find that M.C. could have been telling the truth to the Court about the cause of the bruise to her back, but when I assess the evidence in its totality as it touches upon the assault with the diaper box, I cannot be sure that what M.C. testified T.L. did to her was in fact what occurred.

    327.   The same can be said of the other alleged assaults involving T.L.’s reaction to the spilled wine, grapes and/or blueberries, and the Monster can thrown at him by M.C.  T.L. could have done what M.C. testified he did to her, but I am not at all convinced that he did.  I cannot be sure, because none of what T.L. testified he did regarding those incidents, and why he did what he did, defied logic, reason or common sense. 

    328.   A possibility that an accused could have done what a complainant says the accused did is an entirely insufficient basis upon which to conclude a criminal act has been committed.  A possibility, or even a probability, that the accused did what the complainant said comes nowhere close to the high standard of proof beyond a reasonable doubt which the Crown has the onus to meet before a finding of guilt can ensue.

    329.   Fourthly, I believe T.L.’s testimony regarding the choking incident.  I accept he secured M.C.’s consent that she wished to be temporarily rendered unconscious during an act of consensual sexual intercourse.  T.L.’s version of events made sense.  It was a spontaneous suggestion he made to her.  She agreed to it.  It was something she wanted in the moment.  There is no evidence that he did anything to M.C. of a sexual nature while she was unconscious and unable to voluntarily agree to any form of sexual activity.

    330.   In other words, I can find no grounds upon which to reject what T.L. said he did to render M.C. unconscious with her consent. He stated he eased her down on the bed afterwards and awaited her self-revival. 

    331.   I find T.L.’s testimony that he cannot remember the date believable as well.  The incident took on no great significance for him even though it was a ‘first time’ event.  He enjoyed the intimacy he shared with M.C. in a general sense.  M.C. did too, and she said as much in giving her own evidence.

    332.   I am also somewhat at a loss to understand M.C.’s testimony that she did not mind being choked during intercourse “within reason”. Perhaps she meant she never wished to be rendered unconscious. On the evidence heard at T.L.’s trial however, this limitation of “within reason” was neither elaborated upon, nor explained.

    333.   Fifthly, regarding M.C.’s allegation that, after commencing anal sex with T.L. which she wished to end, and his response was to push her face into the pillow telling her to take it, I accept T.L.’s denial that this incident did not occur.  I believe T.L. because I accept his evidence that sexual intimacy was something M.C. and he did well.  Their sex life together was “healthy”.  Again, M.C. all but confirmed a similar sentiment when she gave her testimony.

    334.   Let me be clear.  I appreciate that abnormal, unusual, nonconsensual and criminal sexual acts can occur between intimate partners.  However, I can conceive of no reason to disbelieve what T.L. said was the one part of the relationship he shared with M.C. that was good.

    335.   As alluded to above, there was one aspect of T.L.’s evidence which I resolutely disbelieved.  It was entirely untrue what he said about his capacity to leave and walk away whenever he grew frustrated or angry with M.C. because she would not follow his suggestions on how things ought to be done.  I find he was headstrong and persistent in wanting her to do things his way.  If she did not, the arguments erupted.  He certainly lost his cool when he smashed her telephone.  T.L. was a person who liked to have things his way, but so too was M.C.  Together sadly, they were an explosive couple.

    336.   That is not to say that T.L. was the sort to resort to physical aggression when tensions flared between them.  I find M.C. was the more so inclined.  It simply does not follow that T.L. must have become assaultive toward M.C. when he did not get his way.

    337.   For the above reasons, I believe T.L.’s testimony about what he says happened in respect of the material events giving rise to his remaining charges, and I will find him not guilty on all five remaining counts.

    Does T.L.’s evidence raise a reasonable doubt?

     

    338.   Having believed T.L. on the core aspects of his evidence as I did, there is no need to assess whether his evidence raised a reasonable doubt.  It did much more to entitle him to an acquittal.  I believed him.

    On the basis of the evidence which I accept, am I satisfied beyond a reasonable doubt in the guilt of T.L.?

    339.   Given my belief in the testimony of T.L., I am, of course, not required to set out the evidence which I do accept, but in this exceptional case, I will.

    340.   First, let me deal with what the defence posited as a motive to fabricate on the part of M.C. She testified that she went forward with her allegations to police, and ultimately came before this Court, out of a desire “to take back for her boys”.  This need M.C. spoke of gave me some pause.  At points, I find, it coloured her recollection of the past events causing her to cast blame on T.L. for the failed relationship they shared.

    341.   M.C. tended to downplay her physicality with T.L. For example, she spoke of “flipping” the Monster can at him, but when pressed under cross-examination, she acknowledged she threw it at him with the intention of hitting him with it. Further, there was an occasion when she struck him while they were driving. She slapped his arm after he smashed her phone. Worse still, she was quite capable of shoving him and spitting on him, if he intruded upon her “bubble”.

    342.   I ponder whether her decision to complain to the police first in December 2020 was an effort on her part to preempt a report by T.L. about her acts of aggression upon him. By her own admission, she was an emotional person. She could also be quick-tempered.

    343.   I note that in late November 2020, T.L. was the one threatening to complain to police if she did not stop calling and texting him. He was characterizing her behaviour then as “criminal harassment”.

    344.   There were also many disagreements between T.L. and M.C. over their son V. before she went to the police. She was lamenting as well having to spend the next 18 years coparenting with T.L.

    345.   It appears to me she did not get much of a police response when she made her first complaint on December 12, 2020. She was then back to the police station within four days on December 16 with fresh allegations of a sexual nature. She may have wished to ‘up the ante’. Four days later again, T.L. was arrested and charged.

    346.   I also have other reservations about M.C.’s credibility, given the animus she clearly harboured for T.L. at the dissolution of their relationship. She was most definitely “angry, hurt and upset” by his decision to open a Tinder account so soon after they separated. It made her feel like a “fool and “used”. As she put it, “Tinder became his first priority”.

    347.   Consequently, I question whether vengeance was motivating her decision to a degree to go to the police in the first place with the various allegations she made concerning T.L.

    348.   Of significance as well, there were, in my view, major inconsistencies in M.C.’s testimony, among them:

    a)      her initial claim that the glass did not break when the wine spilled on the floor, but telling police closer in time to the event occurring that the glass “smashed all over the floor”,

    b)      her reaction immediately thereafter of disengaging with T.L. and going upstairs, whereas she told police on an earlier occasion that he picked her up and slammed her on the couch after the wine spilled,

    c)      her recollection of having been thrown down on the floor by T.L. above the shoe rack only once after she threw the Monster can at him, as she told police, but testifying later that she was thrown down several times,

    d)      her assurance to T.L. that she would not seek child support, but subsequently advising him she would, and

    e)      her evidence that she occasionally had consensual anal intercourse with T.L., but telling police, to the contrary on an earlier occasion and without qualification, that every time they had anal intercourse, it was nonconsensual.

    349.   There were also some serious omissions in the statements she gave to police which form the subject matter of T.L.’s charges, among them:

    a)      her claim that her older son, J., was hitting T.L. after he smashed her cell phone, and

    b)      her insistence that T.L. grabbed and shook her when he put her against the wall on that occasion as well.

    350.   Those details, the type which an honest witness would be disinclined not to tell police about when making a complaint, were only first mentioned by M.C. when she gave her evidence in Court.

    351.   All this aside, there was much of what M.C. said about T.L.’s controlling and dismissive attitude toward her which I do believe wholeheartedly. T.L. was a ‘control freak’. How she parked the car bothered him. I also accept her evidence that he wanted to know “what she ate, what she wore and where she went”. He described her as a “millennial snowflake baby child”. I am fully convinced there were many occasions when T.L. “laughed at [M.C.], tore her down and made her feel below him”, as M.C.’s father, D.C., observed.

    352.   D.C. was witness as well to other disturbing behaviours exhibited by T.L. D.C. noted that T.L. was “pissed off” at the role D.C. was playing post-separation in facilitating the custodial exchanges of V. Most troubling to D.C., T.L. wanted to know where M.C. was living.

    353.   Overall, I found D.C. to be a fair and honest witness. He readily conceded that he never saw T.L. act in a physical way towards his daughter. Nor did D.C. have any cause for concern regarding T.L.’s treatment of either of his grandsons, both V. and J.

    354.   At a fundamental level, I find T.L.’s attitude toward women leaves much to be desired. He described the bruises he saw on M.C.’s forearms, which notwithstanding I accept were work-related, as “stereotypical, phantom women bruises”. While I accept his credibility was enhanced by his admission of their existence, his characterization of them as such betrayed the misogynistic philosophy he seems to have adopted toward women in general. It is quite shocking that as the young man he is, he actually believes all women are subject to bruising of unknown origin. Clearly, it is his thinking that is unacceptably stereotypical.

    355.   Worse still, he would refer to M.C. at times as “a fucking snowflake” or a “chick”. He complained that “she fucked it all up” in describing her attempts to handle their finances as a couple. On other occasions, he opined that she was not in the right “mind space” to make her own decisions.

    356.   Although he readily conceded given his gender, he has never given birth, he seemed to think he knew better how M.C. should get into bed, his truck, or off their couch, shortly after she delivered V. via Caesarean section.

    357.   There will be other women T.L. will come across and develop an intimate relationship with. I pity them. He is in serious need of attitude adjustment.

    Conclusion

    358.   For all the above reasons, I must find T.L. not guilty of assaulting and sexually assaulting M.C. between September 1 and November 30, 2020.

    359.   However, this does not end the matter. I remain deeply concerned about possible future breaches of the peace by T.L. in his ongoing dealings with M.C. regarding custody and access to their son, V.

    360.   Time permitting, I will allow the defence the opportunity today to persuade me otherwise that I should not exercise my common law jurisdiction to require T.L.:

    a)      to enter into a peace bond for an as yet undetermined period,

    b)      to have no contact directly or indirectly with M.C., and not to be within 100 metres of her person, place of residence, schooling, worship, or any other place he knows her to frequent, unless in accordance with a Family Court Order, and

    c)      to keep the peace and be of good behaviour.
